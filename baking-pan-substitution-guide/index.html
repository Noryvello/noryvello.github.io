Baking Pan Substitution Guide

This guide helps you swap one baking pan for another. Pick the pan your recipe calls for and the pan you have, and it tells you whether the batter fits, how full to fill the pan and when to check the bake. A standard pan volume table is included below.

Check your substitution

Pan volumes here are approximate, and pans vary by maker. Always check the fill level before baking and test for doneness rather than trusting the timer alone. This guide covers pan size only, not recipe changes.

Standard pan volumes

The table below lists the approximate volume of common baking pans in cups. Use it to compare the pan your recipe calls for with the pan you have. The closer the volumes, the closer the bake will be to what the recipe intends.

Notice how much volume changes with a small change in size. A 9-inch round pan holds half as much again as an 8-inch round pan, and a 9-inch square pan holds more than a 9-inch round pan. That is why guessing by diameter alone leads to trouble. Compare volumes, not just measurements across the top.

How pan size changes a bake

Pan size changes two things at once: how deep the batter sits and how much surface is exposed to the heat. A smaller pan means deeper batter, which takes longer to bake through and can leave a raw centre if you trust the recipe time. A larger pan means thinner batter, which bakes faster and can dry out if you leave it in for the full time.

Shape matters too. A bundt pan has a hole in the middle, so heat reaches the batter from the centre as well as the edges, and the cake bakes more evenly than the same batter in a deep round pan. A loaf pan is deep and narrow, so the centre takes the longest to bake. When you swap shapes as well as sizes, watch the bake closely the first time and keep notes on what worked.

How to measure a pan you own

If your pan has no size marked on it, measure across the top from inside edge to inside edge. That is the size bakers mean when they say an 8-inch or 9-inch pan. Measuring across the outside, including the rim, gives a larger number and leads to the wrong substitution.

To find the volume of an unmarked pan, pour water into it one cup at a time until it reaches the brim, counting as you go. That gives you the pan's full volume. Remember that you will only fill it about two-thirds full with batter, so compare two-thirds of that volume with the volume your recipe needs.

Fill level and timing rules

Fill most pans about two-thirds full. That leaves room for the batter to rise without spilling over the edge. If the batter fills the pan more than two-thirds, split it between two pans or bake it in batches rather than risking an overflow in a hot oven.

When the new pan is larger than the recipe pan, the layer will be thinner, so start checking for doneness earlier than the recipe time. When the new pan is smaller, the layer will be deeper, so expect a longer bake and test the centre before taking it out. In both cases, test for doneness with a skewer or a light press rather than trusting the timer alone.

Swaps to avoid

Do not swap a shallow pan for a deep one when the batter is large. A deep loaf batter poured into a shallow tray will spread thin and dry out, while a thin tray batter poured into a deep loaf pan may never bake through in the middle. If the volumes are far apart, bake in batches or pick a different recipe.

Be careful with pans that have a special shape, such as a bundt or a tube pan. Their shape is part of how the cake bakes, and a plain round pan will not behave the same way. If the recipe depends on the shape, such as an angel cake in a tube pan, it is better to wait until you have the right pan.

If you bake often, it pays to build up a small set of pans in the sizes your favourite recipes use. Two round pans of the same size let you bake layer cakes without waiting, and a square pan plus a loaf pan covers most tray bakes and breads. Buy pans one at a time as you need them, and choose sturdy pans that sit flat on the shelf.

Questions people ask

Can I use a square pan instead of a round one?

Often yes. A 9-inch square pan holds more than a 9-inch round pan, so the layer will be thinner. Check the volumes in the table and pick the closest match.

How full should I fill a baking pan?

Fill most pans about two-thirds full. That leaves room for the batter to rise without spilling over the edge.

What if my only pan is much bigger?

The batter will spread thin and bake faster. Check for doneness earlier than the recipe time, and expect a thinner result.

Can I split batter between two smaller pans?

Yes. Divide the batter evenly and check both pans for doneness. Smaller pans bake faster than one large pan.

Does pan material change the bake?

Dark pans brown faster and glass pans hold heat longer than light metal pans. If you change material as well as size, watch the bake closely the first time.
