Room Air Conditioner Buying Guide

A good room air conditioner matches the size of the room, suits the way the room is used, and runs efficiently for years. This guide explains how to work out the right size, compare window, split and portable types, read energy ratings, and check installation before you buy.

An air conditioner is something you buy once and live with for years, so it is worth getting the choice right. A unit that fits the room cools evenly, keeps noise down, and uses electricity sensibly. This guide walks through the decisions in order: size, type, efficiency, noise, installation, and the small maintenance habits that keep a unit working well. It names no brands and no prices, because the right choice depends on your room, not on a label.

This page is general guidance only. The right size and the right installation depend on your room, so ask a qualified installer to confirm both before you buy. This page names no brands and no prices.

What size AC does your room need?

Cooling capacity is measured in BTU, short for British Thermal Unit, which describes how much heat a unit can remove from a room in an hour. The bigger the room, the higher the BTU rating you need. That is the whole idea behind sizing, but the details matter, because a room is more than its floor area.

A unit that is too small will run all day and still leave the room warm on the hottest days, which wears it out faster. A unit that is too big has the opposite problem: it cools the air so quickly that it switches off before it has removed much moisture, so the room feels cool but clammy, and the constant switching on and off wastes electricity. The right size runs in steady stretches, cools the room, and takes the humidity down with it.

Floor area is the starting point, but other things push the figure up or down: a high ceiling, afternoon sun through the windows, poor insulation, the number of people in the room, and heat from cooking or appliances. A room that opens into another space, with no door to close, counts as one bigger space. To get a starting figure for your own room, try our AC Size BTU Calculator, which works from your room measurements. Treat the result as a starting point: if your room has an unusual shape, a lot of glass, or a heat source you cannot move, a qualified installer can adjust the figure.

Window, split or portable: which type suits you?

Most room air conditioners fall into three types, and the right one depends as much on your home as on your room.

A window unit is a single box that sits in a window opening, with the noisy compressor on the outside half and the cool air blowing from the inside half. Installation is simple, and it suits a small to medium room with a window that can take the weight and the size. The trade-off is noise: because the compressor sits in the room's window, you hear it working. It also blocks part of the window.

A split unit has two parts: a quiet indoor unit mounted high on the wall, and an outdoor unit that holds the compressor. Because the noisy part lives outside, a split is usually the quietest choice indoors, which makes it a good fit for bedrooms and living rooms. It needs a small hole through the wall for the pipes, a solid wall for the indoor unit, and a place outside for the other half. Installation is a job for a professional.

A portable unit stands on the floor and sends hot air outside through a hose that fits in a window. Nothing is fixed to the wall, so it suits rented homes and rooms where you cannot install anything permanent, and you can move it from room to room. The trade-offs are real: it is usually less efficient than a fixed unit of the same size, the compressor sits in the room so it is noisier, and the unit itself takes up floor space.

Energy ratings in plain words

Every air conditioner carries an energy rating label, and it is worth understanding what it tells you. In plain terms, the rating describes how much cooling the unit gives you for each unit of electricity it uses. A higher rating means the same cooling for less electricity, which means lower running costs over the years you use it.

Ratings are most useful when you compare units of the same size and type. A large unit with a good rating can still use more electricity than a small unit with a poor one, because it does more cooling. So compare like with like, and look at the rating after you have settled the size.

You may also see the word inverter. In a standard unit, the compressor is either fully on or fully off, so the temperature swings as the unit cycles. An inverter unit adjusts the speed of its compressor up and down instead, holding a steadier temperature and often using less electricity over a day. It is a feature worth asking about, not a separate type of air conditioner.

Noise: what to listen for

Noise is easy to overlook in a shop and hard to ignore at home, especially in a bedroom. Manufacturers publish noise figures in decibels, and a lower number means a quieter unit. Check the indoor figure at the low fan setting, because that is how the unit runs most of the time.

Window and portable units put the compressor in the room, so they are the noisiest indoors. Split units keep the compressor outside, so the indoor unit is little more than a fan and is usually much quieter. Do not forget the outdoor half either: an outdoor unit under a neighbour's window, or beside a seating area, can cause friction, so give its placement some thought.

Checks to make before installation

A few checks before you buy save expensive surprises on installation day.

  • Measure the space. Check the width and height of the window or wall, and leave room around the unit for air to flow.
  • Check the power supply. Some larger units need their own circuit, and the wiring in an older home may need a look from an electrician before anything is fitted.
  • Check what the unit will sit on. A window frame must hold the weight of a window unit, and a wall must hold a split indoor unit.
  • Plan the drainage. An air conditioner pulls moisture out of the air, and that water has to go somewhere. Make sure there is a drain or an outside spot for the pipe, and that it will not drip onto a walkway.
  • Find a home for the outdoor unit. It needs clear air around it to work well, and a shaded spot is better than one baking against a wall. Keep the pipe run between the two halves as short as practical. If you rent, ask the landlord before anything is fixed to the building, and check any building rules about outdoor units.

If any of these checks raises a doubt, ask the installer before you buy, not after the unit arrives.

Simple maintenance that keeps a unit efficient

An air conditioner is not a fit-and-forget machine. A little regular care keeps it cooling well and using electricity sensibly.

  • Clean the filters. Dust builds up on the filters and blocks the airflow, so the unit works harder for less cooling. In heavy use, check them every few weeks and clean them as the manual describes.
  • Keep the outdoor unit clear. Leaves, dust, and clutter around the outdoor unit block the air it needs. A clear space around it, and a gentle clean now and then, keeps it breathing.
  • Keep the indoor vents open. Furniture, curtains, or clutter in front of the vents stops cool air reaching the room.
  • Book a professional service once a year. A technician checks the refrigerant level, cleans the coils, and looks over the electrics. It is the one job that is not worth doing yourself.

None of this takes much time, and it makes a real difference. A clean, well-kept unit cools faster, runs more quietly, and lasts longer than one that is left to gather dust.

Questions people ask

How do I know what size AC my room needs?

Start with the size of the room, then adjust for ceiling height, sunlight, insulation, and heat from people and appliances. A BTU calculator can work out a starting figure from your room measurements, and a qualified installer can confirm the final size for your room.

Is a split AC better than a window AC?

Neither is better for everyone. A split is quieter indoors and suits bedrooms and living rooms, but it needs professional installation and a place for the outdoor unit. A window unit is simpler to install and suits a small to medium room with a suitable window. The right choice depends on your room and your home.

What should I check before installation?

Measure the space, check that the power supply can handle the unit, make sure the wall or window can hold the weight, plan where condensation water will drain, and find a spot for the outdoor unit with clear air around it. If you rent, ask the landlord first, and check any building rules about outdoor units.

How often should an AC be serviced?

Clean the filters regularly, checking them every few weeks in heavy use, and keep the outdoor unit clear of leaves and dust. A professional service once a year is a sensible habit: a technician checks the refrigerant level, cleans the coils, and looks over the electrics.
