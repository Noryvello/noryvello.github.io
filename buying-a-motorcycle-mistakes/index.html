7 Mistakes to Avoid When Buying a Motorcycle

The most common mistake when buying a motorcycle is choosing a bike that does not fit your body, your licence or your riding. This guide covers the seven mistakes buyers make most often, plus a fit and inspection checklist to use before you hand over any money.

Buying a motorcycle is exciting, and that excitement is exactly what makes mistakes easy. A bike that looks wonderful on the showroom floor can be the wrong size, the wrong weight or the wrong machine for the riding you actually do. Taking an hour to check the bike properly is always worth it.

The three things that matter most are fit, condition and cost. The bike must fit your body, it must be in sound condition, and the full cost of owning it — not just the price tag — must work for you. The mistakes below cover all three.

This guide is general buying advice, not legal advice. Licence requirements, insurance rules and road rules for motorcycles vary by place — check your local rules and licence class before you buy.

The 7 mistakes to avoid

  1. Buying a bike that does not fit. Seat height, reach to the handlebars and foot position decide whether you can control the bike with confidence. If you cannot reach the ground comfortably when you stop, the bike is too tall for you, and that matters most exactly when you need control — at junctions, on slopes and in slow traffic. Sit on the bike, move it around, and be honest about whether it fits your body.
  2. Skipping the inspection. A shiny bike can hide worn tyres, tired brakes, a stretched chain or a slow leak. Walk around the bike slowly and check the tyres for wear and damage, squeeze the brakes, look at the chain or belt, and check that all the lights work. Look underneath for leaks and check the frame for signs of damage. On a used bike, a careful inspection — or a mechanic's check — is money well spent.
  3. Ignoring the service history. Gaps in servicing can hide problems that only appear later. Ask for service records and look for regular maintenance rather than long gaps. A bike with a full history tells you the owner looked after it; a bike with no records at all is a gamble. If the seller cannot show you any history, factor that uncertainty into your decision.
  4. Forgetting the running costs. The price of the bike is only the start. Insurance, servicing, tyres, fuel and gear all add up over a year of riding, and they vary a great deal between bikes. Before you buy, work out the full cost of owning the bike for a year, not just the purchase price. A bike that seems affordable in the shop can become expensive once you add everything it needs.
  5. Buying power you cannot use. A bigger engine is not better for a new rider. A powerful bike is heavier, faster to get into trouble, and harder to handle at low speed — exactly where new riders spend most of their time. Match the bike to your experience and your licence class, and remember that a smaller bike you can ride well is far more enjoyable than a bigger bike that rides you.
  6. Not test riding. A short ride reveals handling, comfort and problems that no walkaround can show you. Does the bike pull to one side? Do the brakes feel smooth? Does the engine run evenly? Is the riding position comfortable after a few minutes? If the seller will not let you test ride, treat that as a warning sign and ask why. Never buy a bike you have not ridden unless you have no other choice.
  7. Overlooking gear and storage. A helmet, jacket, gloves and a lock are not optional extras — they are part of buying a motorcycle. Budget for them from the start, and think about where the bike will live. A dry, secure place to keep the bike protects it from weather and theft, and a good lock is essential wherever you park. Sorting out gear and storage before the bike arrives saves a lot of trouble later.

Before you buy

Once the checklist is done, take a final step back. Work out the full first-year cost, including insurance, servicing, tyres and gear, and make sure it fits your budget. Check the warranty on a new bike, or the returns policy if you are buying from a dealer. And if anything about the bike feels wrong — the fit, the condition, the seller — trust that feeling and walk away. There will always be another bike, and the right one is worth waiting for.

Questions people ask

What size motorcycle should a beginner buy?

A beginner is usually better off with a smaller, lighter bike that is easy to handle and forgiving of mistakes. Match the bike to your experience and your licence class, not to the biggest engine you can afford.

What should I check on a used motorcycle?

Check the tyres, brakes, chain or belt, lights, and look for leaks or damage. Ask for the service history, and consider having a mechanic look at the bike before you buy.

Do I need a special licence to ride a motorcycle?

Licence requirements vary by place and by the size of the motorcycle. Check your local rules and licence class before you buy, so you know what you are allowed to ride.

How do I know if a motorcycle fits me?

Sit on the bike and check that you can reach the ground comfortably, reach the handlebars without stretching, and work the controls easily. A short test ride will tell you more than sitting still.

Should I buy a new or used motorcycle?

A new bike comes with a warranty and a known history, while a used bike costs less up front but needs a careful inspection. Either can be a good choice if you check the bike properly before you buy.
