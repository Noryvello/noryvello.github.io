Earthquakes Explained

An earthquake happens when rock deep under the ground suddenly slips along a crack called a fault, sending out shaking waves. Most earthquakes happen where the Earth's moving plates meet, and knowing what to do when the shaking starts is the best protection.

The ground under our feet feels solid, but it is always moving — very slowly. The Earth's outer shell is broken into huge slabs called tectonic plates, and those plates creep past, under and away from each other all the time. Most of the time we feel nothing at all. But when plates catch and then suddenly let go, the stored energy escapes as shaking, and we call that an earthquake.

Earthquakes are one of the most powerful natural forces on the planet, yet the basic idea behind them is simple: rock under strain breaks or slips, and the energy travels outward as waves. This guide explains why it happens, why some regions shake far more than others, what the magnitude scale really means, and what to do if you ever feel the ground move.

This page is general information only. If you live in an earthquake-prone area, follow the safety guidance of your local emergency authority, and find the safe spots in your home before an earthquake happens.

What causes an earthquake

The Earth's crust is divided into tectonic plates that float on hot, slowly moving rock beneath them. The plates move only a few centimetres a year — about as fast as your fingernails grow — but over time that movement builds up huge pressure where plates meet. The edges of plates are often rough and locked together by friction, so instead of sliding smoothly they stick, bend and store energy like a stretched rubber band.

When the strain finally beats the friction, the rock slips along a crack in the crust called a fault. The stored energy is released in a burst, and waves of shaking travel outward in every direction. The place deep underground where the slip starts is called the focus, and the point on the surface directly above it is called the epicentre. Shallow earthquakes, with a focus close to the surface, are usually felt more strongly than deep ones of the same size.

Most earthquakes are caused by plate movement, but not all of them. Volcanic activity can shake the ground as magma moves, landslides and mine collapses can cause small local tremors, and even very large reservoirs of water have been linked to minor quakes. These are all small compared with the shaking a plate-boundary earthquake can produce.

Why some places shake more

Earthquakes are not spread evenly around the world. The vast majority happen along plate boundaries, where plates collide, pull apart or grind past each other. The most famous earthquake zone is the Ring of Fire, the horseshoe-shaped belt around the Pacific Ocean where many plates meet. Countries on that belt — Japan, Indonesia, Chile, New Zealand and the western edge of the Americas — feel far more earthquakes than places in the middle of a plate, such as most of northern Europe.

Distance from a boundary is only part of the story. The type of ground matters too: soft, loose soil and reclaimed land shake more strongly than solid rock, so two towns the same distance from an earthquake can feel it very differently. Deep earthquakes are felt over a wider area but usually less strongly at the surface, while shallow ones hit hard close to the epicentre and fade quickly with distance.

Some regions also sit on faults inside a plate, far from any boundary. These intraplate earthquakes are rarer, but they can still be strong, and because they are unexpected they sometimes catch communities unprepared. Wherever you live, it is worth knowing whether your area has any earthquake history.

The magnitude scale

Magnitude measures how much energy an earthquake releases. It is worked out from the recordings of seismometers, instruments that measure ground movement. The scale is logarithmic, which means each whole-number step is much bigger than the one before it: a magnitude 6 quake releases roughly thirty-two times more energy than a magnitude 5 quake, and a magnitude 7 releases roughly a thousand times more than a magnitude 5.

That is why small earthquakes are common and large ones are rare. Thousands of small quakes happen every year, most too small to feel, while a quake of magnitude 7 or above happens only a handful of times a year worldwide. There is no upper limit to the scale in theory, but the largest earthquakes ever recorded are around magnitude 9.5.

Magnitude is not the same as intensity. Magnitude is a single number for the whole earthquake, based on the energy released. Intensity describes how strongly the shaking is felt in a particular place, and it depends on distance from the epicentre, depth and local ground. One earthquake has one magnitude but many different intensities across the area it affects.

What to do during an earthquake

If the ground starts shaking, the standard advice in most countries is simple: drop, cover and hold on. Drop to the ground, take cover under a sturdy table or desk, and hold on until the shaking stops. If there is no table nearby, cover your head and neck with your arms and crouch against an inside wall, away from windows, mirrors and anything that could fall on you.

Stay where you are until the shaking stops. Running outside during the shaking is dangerous because of falling masonry, glass and roof tiles near building exits. If you are outside, move away from buildings, street lights and power lines and stay in the open. If you are in a car, pull over safely away from bridges and overpasses and stay inside until the shaking stops.

Lifts and stairs are not safe places during an earthquake, and doorways are no stronger than any other part of a modern building, so they are not a safe refuge either. The safest choice is almost always to stay put, protect your head and wait for the shaking to pass, which usually takes less than a minute.

After the shaking stops

Once the shaking ends, check yourself and the people around you for injuries, then check your home for damage. Smaller quakes called aftershocks often follow a large earthquake, sometimes for days or weeks, so stay alert and be ready to drop, cover and hold on again. If you smell gas or see damaged wiring, leave the building and call the emergency services rather than investigating yourself.

Listen to local radio, official alerts or your emergency authority for instructions, and avoid driving unless you must, so the roads stay clear for rescue vehicles. If you are near the coast and feel a very strong or long earthquake, move to higher ground at once, because a tsunami can follow — do not wait for an official warning. Keep a small emergency kit ready in earthquake-prone areas: water, a torch, a first-aid kit and a phone charger are a good start.

Questions people ask

What causes an earthquake?

An earthquake is caused by rock slipping suddenly along a fault, usually because the slow movement of tectonic plates has built up strain that is released all at once as shaking waves.

Can earthquakes be predicted?

No. Scientists can say which areas are likely to have earthquakes, but nobody can predict the exact day, time or size of a particular earthquake.

What is the difference between magnitude and intensity?

Magnitude is a single number for the whole earthquake, based on the energy it released. Intensity describes how strongly the shaking is felt in one particular place, and it varies with distance and ground type.

What should I do during an earthquake?

Drop to the ground, take cover under a sturdy table or desk, and hold on until the shaking stops. Stay indoors until it ends, and keep away from windows and anything that could fall.

Why do aftershocks happen?

Aftershocks are smaller earthquakes that follow a large one as the ground settles and the fault adjusts to its new position. They can go on for days or weeks after the main quake.
