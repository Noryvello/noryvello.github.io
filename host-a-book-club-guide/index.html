How to Host a Book Club

Hosting a book club takes a book, a date, and a place to meet — and a little structure to keep the discussion going. This guide covers the meeting format, how to choose books, a discussion structure, and a checklist for the host.

Start small and keep it simple

A book club works best when it starts small. Six to eight members is a good size: enough people for a lively discussion, few enough that everyone gets a chance to speak. You do not need a formal structure, a constitution, or a name. You need a book, a date, and a place to meet. Everything else can grow from there.

Meet regularly, and meet on a rhythm people can remember — the first Tuesday of the month, or every second Sunday. A fixed rhythm matters more than a perfect venue. If members know when the club meets, they will keep the date free, and the club will survive the months when life gets busy.

How to choose the book

Choosing the book is where most book clubs argue, so agree a method early and stick to it. The simplest method is rotation: each member takes a turn picking the book, in an order agreed at the start. Everyone gets a turn, and nobody has to defend their taste. The picker chooses a book they are curious about, and the group reads it in good faith.

Another method is the shortlist vote. Each month, members suggest one book each, and the group votes for the one to read. This works well when members have different tastes, because everyone has a say. A third method is themed months: a month for a classic, a month for a debut novel, a month for non-fiction. Themes give the club variety and make choosing easier, because the theme narrows the field.

Whatever method you use, agree two rules. First, the book should be easy to find — a book nobody can buy or borrow will kill a meeting. Second, a member who does not finish the book is still welcome. Life gets in the way, and a book club that punishes slow readers will soon have no readers at all.

A discussion structure that works

A good discussion needs a little structure, but not much. Start with first impressions: ask each person for one sentence about the book — what they liked, what they did not, or what surprised them. Going around the room gives everyone a voice early, and it tells the host where the interesting disagreements are.

Then move to the heart of the book. Pick two or three questions in advance: a question about a character's choice, a question about the book's main idea, and a question about the ending. Open questions work better than yes-or-no questions. Ask what a character should have done, or whether the ending felt fair, and let the conversation run. The host's job is not to lecture but to keep the talk moving and to invite in anyone who has been quiet.

End by looking ahead. Ask the group what the book left them thinking about, then choose the next book and confirm the next meeting date. A meeting that ends with a decision feels complete, and members leave knowing what to read next.

Keep the club alive

Book clubs fade for simple reasons: the meetings slip, the books get harder to finish, or one voice starts to dominate. The fixes are simple too. Keep the meeting rhythm even when only a few members can come. A small meeting is better than a cancelled one. Keep the books a reasonable length — a very long book will leave half the group behind. And keep the discussion kind. Disagreeing about a book is the fun of a book club; disagreeing about a person is not.

Every few months, ask the group how the club is going. Is the meeting time still right? Is the book choice method working? Are new members welcome? A short check-in keeps the club honest and keeps it alive for years, not just months.

Questions people ask

How many people should be in a book club?

Six to eight members is a good size for a book club. It is enough people for a lively discussion and few enough that everyone gets a chance to speak. Larger groups can work, but they need a stronger host to keep the discussion on track.

How often should a book club meet?

Once a month is the most common rhythm, and it gives members enough time to read the book. Some clubs meet every six weeks, and some meet every two weeks with shorter books. A fixed, memorable rhythm matters more than the exact gap between meetings.

What if someone does not finish the book?

Welcome them anyway. Life gets in the way of reading, and a book club that punishes slow readers will soon have no members. A member who read half the book can still join the discussion, and they may finish it afterwards.

How do we choose the next book?

Rotation is the simplest method: each member takes a turn picking the book. A shortlist vote also works well, as does themed months — a classic one month, a debut novel the next. Agree the method early so choosing never becomes an argument.

How long should a book club meeting last?

About ninety minutes is a good length. It leaves time for hellos, the discussion, and choosing the next book, without dragging on. Online meetings work better a little shorter, at about an hour.
