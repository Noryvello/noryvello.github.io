LinkedIn Profile Writing Guide

This guide walks through each part of a LinkedIn profile — headline, About, experience and skills — with a headline builder and a checklist to tick off as you go. It will not promise you jobs or views, but it will help your profile say clearly what you do.

No profile wording can guarantee job offers, messages or views. This guide helps you write clearly and honestly. Keep every claim on your profile true, and update it when your role changes.

The headline

Your headline is the first thing anyone sees, and often the only thing they read. It sits next to your name in search results, in comments and in connection requests, so it has to say what you do in words a stranger would understand. A job title on its own is rarely enough: it says where you sit, not what you do.

A strong headline has two parts: what you do, and who it is for. Use the headline builder at the top of the page to draft three options from your role, your specialty and your audience, then pick the one that sounds most like you and edit it in your own words. Avoid buzzwords and jargon that nobody outside your field would recognise.

The About section

The About section is where you can say in a few short paragraphs what you do, what you are good at, and what you are looking for. Write it in the first person, as if you were talking to someone at a work event: short paragraphs, plain words, and no third-person biography voice. Start with what you do now, not with your life story.

Give the reader something specific. A line about the kind of work you enjoy, the problems you solve, or the people you help is worth more than a list of adjectives. End with what you are open to, whether that is new roles, collaboration, or simply connecting with people in your field, so the reader knows what to do next.

Experience that reads well

For each role, write two or three lines on what you actually did, not just the job title and the dates. The reader wants to know what the work involved and what changed because of it. A line that says what you delivered, who it was for, or what you learned will do more than a list of duties copied from a job description.

Keep older roles short and recent roles fuller. If you have been in a role for a long time, break it into the main things you did rather than writing one long paragraph. And keep the tense consistent: present tense for a current role, past tense for roles you have left.

Skills and recommendations

List the skills you can actually do, and keep the list honest. A short list of real skills is stronger than a long list of buzzwords, because anyone who knows the field can tell the difference. Put the skills that matter most for the work you want at the top, and remove anything you would not want to be asked about.

Recommendations carry weight because they come from someone else. Ask one or two people who know your work well — a manager, a colleague, or a client — and make it easy for them by saying what you would like them to mention. A short, specific recommendation beats a long, vague one.

Your photo and banner

Use a clear, recent headshot with your face easy to see and the background uncluttered. You do not need a studio photograph: a well-lit photo taken on a phone, with you looking at the camera, works fine. Avoid group photos, sunglasses, and photos where your face is hard to make out.

The banner image behind your photo is a chance to say something about your work, but it is optional. A simple, uncluttered image is better than a busy one, and leaving the default banner is better than using an image that has nothing to do with you.

Keeping it up to date

A profile goes stale faster than you think. When your role changes, update your headline, your About section and your experience on the same day, while the details are fresh. Check the whole profile every few months and remove anything that no longer fits: old skills, old goals, and old claims that are no longer true.

Work through the checklist at the top of the page whenever you update the profile. It covers every section, so you can see at a glance what is done and what still needs attention.

Questions people ask

How long should my LinkedIn headline be?

Short enough to read at a glance: your role and who you help, in plain words. A headline that needs a second reading is too long, and a headline that is only a job title says too little. The headline builder on this page drafts three options to start from.

Should I write my About section in the first person?

Yes. First person sounds like a person talking, which is what the section is for. Third person sounds like a biography written by someone else, and it puts distance between you and the reader.

What if I am between roles?

Say what you do, not what you used to do, and say what you are looking for. A headline that describes your work and an About section that says what you are open to are far better than leaving the profile blank or out of date.

Do recommendations matter?

Yes, because they come from someone else and say things you cannot say about yourself. One or two short, specific recommendations from people who know your work are worth more than a long list of endorsements.

How often should I update my profile?

Whenever your role changes, and every few months otherwise. Check that your headline, About section and experience still match where you are now, and remove anything that no longer fits.
