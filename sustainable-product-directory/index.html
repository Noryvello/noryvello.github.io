The 3 questions before buying anything

The most sustainable product is often the one you already own, so start with three questions. First, do I need it? A new item that sits in a drawer helps nobody, however green its label. Borrow it, rent it, or wait a week and see if you still want it. Second, will it last? Look at the material, the joints and the guarantee. Steel, glass, solid wood and thick cotton usually outlast thin plastic, and a product with a long guarantee tells you the maker trusts it. Third, can it be repaired? Check whether spare parts exist, whether the battery or the head comes out, and whether a local repairer could fix it. A product that fails one of these questions is rarely a good buy, however cheap it looks.

Those three questions also protect your money. A bottle that costs more but lasts five years is cheaper per year than a bottle you replace every few months. The same is true of a razor, a mop or a backpack. Sustainable buying is mostly about buying less often, not about buying something with a leaf on the packet.

Two worked examples

Example one: a kitchen swap. A household that buys bottled water every week switches to a steel bottle, a glass lunch box and a set of beeswax wraps. The bottle and box last years, the wraps last months and then compost. The household still buys food the same way, but the weekly pile of single-use packaging mostly disappears. Nothing about the change needs a new skill — the bottle gets washed like a glass, the wraps get rinsed in cool water.

Example two: a bathroom swap. A safety razor replaces disposable razors, a soap bar replaces a pump bottle, and washable cotton pads replace single-use ones. The razor needs a new blade every few weeks, which costs little, and the pads go in with the laundry. The bathroom ends up with fewer bottles, and the routine takes the same few minutes it always did.

Common greenwashing tricks

Greenwashing is when a product looks greener than it is. Watch for vague words with no proof: eco, natural, green and clean mean nothing on their own unless the packet says what was actually done. Watch for green packaging on an unchanged product — a leaf on the box is not a change in the factory. Watch for one green feature hiding the rest: a bottle made from recycled plastic is still single-use if you throw it away after one drink. And watch for claims you cannot check, such as a percentage with no source. A genuine claim names the material, the standard or the test, and tells you where to check it.

How to make things last longer

Most products fail early for simple reasons. Wash things the way the label says, and dry them properly — dampness ruins wood, steel and fabric alike. Store items dry and out of direct sun. Fix small damage early: a loose screw, a fraying seam or a cracked seal is a five-minute job now and a dead product later. Buy spares when they exist — seals, heads, blades and lids are usually cheap and turn a short-lived item into a long-lived one. And keep the box or the manual for anything with a guarantee, so a real fault can be claimed.

What this directory cannot do

This directory does not test products, rank brands or promise that any single item will last as long as the table says. Lifespans are typical figures for a well-made example used normally; a badly made one will fail sooner. Prices and availability change by country and by shop, so the page does not quote either. If a product matters to you — a mattress, a pair of shoes, a phone — read a proper review and, where you can, handle the item before you buy it.

Questions people ask

What makes a product sustainable?

A sustainable product is one that lasts a long time, can be repaired, uses materials that can be reused or recycled, and does not waste much in making or using it. No product is perfect on every count, so the honest test is the three questions: do you need it, will it last, and can it be repaired.

Is a sustainable product always more expensive?

Not always, and often not in the long run. A sustainable version may cost more at first, but if it lasts five times longer it costs less per year. Some swaps cost almost nothing, such as a bar of soap instead of a pump bottle, or a drying rack instead of a dryer.

What is greenwashing?

Greenwashing is when a product is presented as greener than it really is — vague words like eco or natural, green packaging on an unchanged product, or one small green feature used to hide the rest. A genuine claim names the material, the standard or the test behind it.

How do I know if something is repairable?

Look for spare parts, screws instead of glue, a battery or head you can remove, and a guarantee of a year or more. If the maker sells seals, blades, lids or handles separately, that is a good sign. If the product is sealed shut with no way in, it probably cannot be repaired.

Should I replace working things with greener ones?

No. The greenest thing you own is usually the one you already have. Use it until it wears out, repair it if you can, and only then replace it with a longer-lasting version. Throwing away a working item to buy a greener one wastes the materials and energy already spent on it.

What is the easiest sustainable swap?

Start with something you use every day and throw away often: a reusable water bottle, shopping bags, or a soap bar instead of a pump bottle. Each one is cheap, needs no new skill, and removes a steady stream of single-use waste from your week.
