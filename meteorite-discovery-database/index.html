Meteorite Discovery Database

A meteorite is a rock from space that survived its fall through the air and landed on Earth, and the famous ones have taught scientists how the Solar System began. This guide explains what meteorites are, the most famous finds, how to tell one from an ordinary rock, and what scientists learn from them.

How to use this guide

This guide is a reference about famous meteorites, not a catalogue of rocks for sale and not a promise that you will find one. Meteorites are rare, most rocks that look like one are not, and the only way to know for certain is a test by an expert. What this guide gives you is the knowledge to understand the famous finds, to recognise the signs of a real meteorite, and to know what to do next if you think you have found one.

Read the sections in order if the subject is new to you. The first part explains what a meteorite is and where meteorites come from. The middle part tells the stories of the most famous finds and explains how meteorites are found and identified. The last part explains what scientists learn from them and which mistakes to avoid.

One warning belongs at the start: do not buy a rock as a meteorite on a seller's word alone. The market for meteorites attracts fakes, and a heavy black rock with a confident story is the most common trick. The section on identifying a meteorite explains the real tests, and the section on common mistakes explains how people lose money. If you remember one thing from this guide, let it be this: proof comes from a laboratory, not from a sales pitch.

What a meteorite is

A meteorite is a piece of rock or metal from space that reached the ground. It begins its life as a meteoroid, a lump of rock or metal drifting through space, usually a fragment of an asteroid. When the meteoroid hits Earth's air, friction heats it until it glows, and the streak of light is what we call a meteor, or a shooting star. Most meteors burn up completely in the air. The rare few that survive the fall and land on the ground are meteorites.

Meteorites fall into three broad types. Stony meteorites are made mostly of rock, and they are the most common kind, about nineteen in twenty of all falls. Iron meteorites are made mostly of iron and nickel, and they are heavy, dense and strongly magnetic. Stony-iron meteorites are a mix of both, with metal woven through rock, and they are the rarest of the three. The type matters, because each one tells a different story about where it came from.

Most meteorites come from the asteroid belt, the ring of rocky bodies between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter. Collisions in the belt knock fragments loose, and some of those fragments drift until Earth's gravity pulls them in. A small number come from further away: a few meteorites are pieces of the Moon or of Mars, thrown into space by an impact on those worlds and later captured by Earth. Those are among the most valuable rocks in science, because they are the only pieces of other worlds we can hold without sending a spacecraft.

A meteorite is not the same as a comet. Comets are mostly ice and dust, and they break apart or melt as they approach the Sun. An asteroid is rock or metal, and it is the asteroids that give us meteorites. The difference matters for identification: a meteorite is a solid rock or a lump of metal, never a ball of ice, and it has been heated by its fall, which leaves marks on its surface that this guide explains later.

The most famous meteorites

The largest known meteorite is Hoba, found in 1920 on a farm in Namibia. It is a single block of iron and nickel weighing about 60 tonnes, so large that it has never been moved from the place where it fell. Nobody knows exactly when it fell, because it happened long before anyone recorded it, but its size and its shape tell scientists it arrived slowly, at a shallow angle, which is why it did not dig a deep crater. It is now a protected national monument, and it is the one meteorite you can stand beside and touch.

The Allende meteorite fell over Mexico in February 1969, scattering thousands of black stones across the state of Chihuahua. It is a carbonaceous chondrite, a rare kind of stony meteorite that holds some of the oldest material ever studied: tiny white inclusions, rich in calcium and aluminium, that formed about 4.567 billion years ago, before the planets themselves. Allende arrived in laboratories just as scientists were learning to date the Solar System, and it is still one of the most studied rocks in the world.

The Murchison meteorite fell in Australia in September 1969, only months after Allende, and it surprised scientists in a different way. It is also a carbonaceous chondrite, and when it was analysed it was found to hold amino acids, the building blocks of proteins, along with other organic molecules. The discovery showed that the ingredients of life can form in space without any living thing, and Murchison is still studied today for what it says about how life on Earth may have begun.

The Chelyabinsk meteorite is the most famous fall of modern times. In February 2013, a rock about 20 metres across entered the air over Russia and exploded high above the city of Chelyabinsk, with an energy NASA estimated at about 500 kilotons of TNT equivalent. The blast broke windows across the city and hurt more than a thousand people, mostly with flying glass. Thousands of small fragments were later found on the ground, and the event reminded the world that the sky can still surprise us.

The Sikhote-Alin fall of February 1947, in eastern Russia, was the largest iron meteorite shower ever recorded. A large iron body broke apart in the air and rained hundreds of twisted, torn fragments over the forest, digging small craters and leaving a strewn field that collectors still visit. The fragments are famous for their sculpted shapes, torn by the force of the fall, and for the thumbprint-like hollows that cover their surfaces. Earlier, in 1908, the Tunguska event flattened a vast area of Siberian forest in a great airburst, but no crater and no confirmed meteorite was ever found, which is why scientists still argue about what exactly exploded over Tunguska.

How meteorites are found

Most meteorites are found by accident. A farmer turns one up in a field, a walker notices a strange black rock on a beach, or a desert traveller spots a dark stone where no dark stones should be. Deserts and ice fields are the best hunting grounds, because a dark rock stands out against pale sand or white ice, and because dry places preserve a fallen rock for a long time. Antarctica is the richest hunting ground of all: meteorites that fell thousands of years ago are carried by the ice to places where scientists collect them by the hundreds.

Some falls are seen as they happen. When a bright fireball crosses the sky and people report it, scientists can work out the path from the reports and from camera recordings, and then search the ground where the rock should have landed. This is how the Chelyabinsk fragments were found so quickly, and how several recent falls have been recovered within days. A fall that is seen and found quickly is the most valuable kind, because the rock has had no time to rust or to be contaminated by Earth.

Once a rock is found, it must be identified. The first checks are simple and can be done at home: the rock should be heavy for its size, dark on the outside, and attracted to a magnet in most cases. The next checks need a laboratory: a thin slice under a microscope, or a chemical test for nickel, which is common in meteorites and rare in Earth rocks. A museum or a university with a meteorite collection can do these tests, and most of them welcome genuine enquiries.

Every confirmed meteorite is given an official name, usually the nearest town or place to where it fell or was found, and it is recorded in the official catalogue of meteorites kept by the international society for meteoritics. The catalogue is the reference that scientists use, and it is the reason this guide can name the famous finds with confidence. If a rock is not in the catalogue, it is not a confirmed meteorite, however convincing the story that comes with it.

How to tell a meteorite from an ordinary rock

Start with weight. A meteorite is dense, and a stony meteorite feels heavier than an ordinary rock of the same size, while an iron meteorite feels far heavier. Pick up the rock, then pick up an ordinary stone of the same size from the same place, and compare. If the strange rock is not heavier, it is probably not a meteorite.

Look at the surface. A fresh meteorite has a fusion crust, a thin dark coating formed when its outer layer melted during the fall. The crust is usually black or dark brown, thin as a coat of paint, and it may be cracked or chipped at the edges. Under the crust, a broken face may show light grey stone with tiny bright flecks of metal. Many meteorites also carry regmaglypts, shallow thumbprint-like hollows pressed into the surface by the air during the fall.

Test it with a magnet. Most meteorites contain iron and nickel, so a strong magnet will stick to them or at least pull toward them. A few stony meteorites are only weakly magnetic, so a rock that ignores a magnet is not automatically an ordinary rock, but a rock that attracts a magnet strongly is worth a closer look. Be careful: some Earth rocks, such as magnetite and hematite, are also magnetic, so the magnet test alone proves nothing.

Check for the signs that rule a rock out. A rock full of holes, like a sponge, is almost certainly slag from a furnace, not a meteorite, because meteorites do not have bubbles inside. A rock that is light for its size, or that shows layers like a sedimentary rock, is also an Earth rock. A rock with quartz crystals, or with a glassy shine all over, is an Earth rock too. When in doubt, take clear photographs of the rock from several sides, note exactly where you found it, and ask a museum or a university expert before you do anything else.

What meteorites tell us

Meteorites are the oldest objects you can hold. The Solar System formed about 4.56 billion years ago from a cloud of gas and dust, and the first solid objects to form in that cloud are preserved inside carbonaceous chondrites such as Allende. By dating those inclusions, scientists worked out the age of the Solar System itself, and every date in the history of the planets depends on that measurement.

Meteorites also tell us what the planets are made of. Iron meteorites are pieces of the cores of small planets that broke apart long ago, so they show us the inside of a world we can never visit. Stony meteorites show us the crusts and mantles of those same bodies. By studying both, scientists built the picture of Earth's own layers: a crust, a mantle and an iron core, formed when the young Earth melted and the heavy metal sank to the centre.

The organic molecules in meteorites such as Murchison tell a third story. Amino acids and other carbon compounds formed in space, without life, and were delivered to the young Earth by falling rocks. That does not prove that life came from space, but it shows that the ingredients of life are common in the Universe, and it changed how scientists think about where life can begin.

Meteorites from the Moon and Mars tell a fourth story, about those worlds themselves. A piece of the Moon in a laboratory can be dated and analysed in ways no spacecraft instrument can match, and a piece of Mars can show what the Martian crust is made of and whether water once flowed there. Each one is a free sample return mission, delivered by gravity instead of by a rocket.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is believing that every heavy black rock is a meteorite. Slag from old furnaces, lumps of magnetite, and dark volcanic rock all look the part, and they are far more common than real meteorites. A rock must pass several tests, weight, crust, magnetism and laboratory analysis, before it earns the name, and most hopeful finds fail at the first test.

The second mistake is buying a meteorite with no proof. A certificate printed by the seller is not proof, and a story about where the rock fell is not proof either. Real meteorites come with a record in the official catalogue, and a reputable dealer will name the fall, show the classification, and welcome an independent check. If the seller will not do that, walk away.

The third mistake is cleaning or cutting a possible meteorite before an expert has seen it. Washing a rock can remove the fusion crust, and cutting it can destroy the features an expert needs. Keep the rock dry, keep it in a clean bag or box, and do not grind, polish or wash it until an expert has looked at it.

The fourth mistake is ignoring the law. In some countries meteorites belong to the state, in others they belong to the landowner, and in a few they belong to the finder. The rules differ by place, and exporting a meteorite without permission can be a crime. Check the law for the place where the rock was found before you sell it, move it or claim it.

This guide is general information about famous finds, never a certificate for any rock. Most heavy black rocks are not meteorites, and a seller's claim is not proof, so always ask a museum or a university expert before you buy a rock as a meteorite or claim you have found one.
