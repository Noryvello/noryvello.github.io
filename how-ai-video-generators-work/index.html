How AI Video Generators Work, Explained Simply

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

An AI video generator turns a written description or a still image into a short moving clip. It does this by learning patterns from huge collections of videos, then predicting, frame by frame, how a scene should look and move. This guide explains the process in plain words, why clips are short, and what can go wrong.

Video feels like magic when it works: type a sentence about a boat crossing a harbour at dawn, and a few seconds later you are watching it happen. Behind that magic is a process that is easier to understand than it first appears. A video is a sequence of still images shown quickly enough that your brain reads them as motion, and an AI video generator is, at heart, an image generator that has learned to make many images in a row that fit together.

That last part, making the images fit together, is the hard part. A single beautiful frame is not a video. Every frame has to follow from the one before it, with the same characters, the same lighting, and movement that looks natural. Understanding how generators tackle that problem explains most of what they do well and most of what they get wrong.

Honest limit: AI video is still young. Clips are short, faces and hands often distort, and on-screen text and speech in generated clips are frequently garbled. Interfaces, features, and limits change often, so treat any button-by-button guide as temporary and check your tool's current help pages.

Text-to-video in plain words

Text-to-video starts with nothing but words. You write a prompt describing a scene, for example a cat walking along a garden wall in the rain, and the model generates a short clip that matches. The model has never seen your exact scene before. It has seen countless videos of cats, walls, gardens, and rain, and it uses those patterns to invent a new clip.

The generation happens in stages. First, the model reads your prompt and forms a rough idea of the scene: what is in it, where things are, and what the mood should be. Then it creates the opening frame, much like an image generator creates a picture. After that, it predicts the next frames one after another, each one a small change from the one before, so that the cat takes a step, the rain falls, and the camera holds steady.

The quality of the prompt matters a great deal. A vague prompt, such as "a nice video of a city," gives the model little to work with, and the result is often generic. A prompt that names the subject, the action, the setting, the camera movement, and the mood gives the model a clearer target. "A slow aerial shot drifting over a rainy city street at night, neon signs reflecting on wet pavement" tells the model far more than "city video" ever could.

Text-to-video is best for scenes you can describe clearly in words, especially landscapes, city views, and simple actions. It struggles with precise details: a specific logo, an exact number of objects, or a complicated sequence of events. If you need something exact, image-to-video is usually the better starting point.

Image-to-video: animating a still picture

Image-to-video starts from a picture you provide and brings it to life. You upload a photo or an AI-generated image, add a short description of the movement you want, and the model generates a clip that begins with your image and then moves. The picture sets the scene, and the prompt sets the motion.

This approach gives you far more control over how things look. The characters, the objects, the colours, and the composition all come from your starting image, so they stay consistent. If you want a specific product, a specific person, or a specific place in your video, starting from an image of it is the most reliable way to get there.

The movement prompt should be simple and specific. "The camera slowly pushes in, the curtains sway gently, and steam rises from the cup" works better than "make it cinematic," because it tells the model exactly what should move and how. One or two movements per clip is a good rule. Asking for many movements at once often produces a confused result where nothing moves cleanly.

Image-to-video is also the standard way to extend a video. You can take the last frame of one clip, use it as the starting image for the next, and build a longer video from short pieces. This takes patience, because each join needs checking, but it is how most creators make AI videos longer than a single clip allows.

Frames and motion: what the model is really doing

A video is a sequence of frames, and a generator has to create every one of them. For a short clip, that can be dozens of frames, each one a full image. The model does not draw each frame from scratch independently. Instead, it learns how frames relate to each other, so that the second frame follows naturally from the first, the third from the second, and so on.

Think of it like a flipbook. In a flipbook, each page shows a small change from the previous page, and flipping through them quickly creates the illusion of motion. An AI video generator builds a flipbook, but it has to invent every page itself, deciding how much the subject moves, how the light changes, and how the camera behaves.

The model learns these relationships from training videos. By watching huge numbers of clips, it learns that a walking person moves their legs in a pattern, that water ripples in a certain way, and that a camera pan shifts the whole scene smoothly. When it generates a new clip, it applies those learned patterns to your prompt.

This is also why motion words in your prompt matter so much. Words like "slowly," "gently," "sweeping," and "suddenly" tell the model how fast and how much things should move. Without motion words, the model has to guess, and the guess is often either too still or too wild. A prompt that describes the movement is worth more than a prompt that only describes the scene.

Why AI video clips are short

Most AI video generators produce clips of only a few seconds. There are two reasons, and both come from the same root: video is much harder than images.

The first reason is computing power. A single image is one frame. A few seconds of video is dozens of frames, each one needing the same kind of generation work as an image, plus the extra work of keeping them consistent. Doubling the length of a clip more than doubles the work, because every new frame has to fit with all the frames before it.

The second reason is drift. The longer a clip runs, the more chances the model has to lose track of the scene. A character's face might slowly change shape, an object might morph into something else, or the background might shift in ways that do not make sense. Short clips keep these errors small. Long clips give them room to grow.

This is why the standard workflow for AI video is to generate short clips and join them in an editing tool. Each clip is a shot, and the editing tool is where the shots become a video. A ten-second video made from three good clips usually looks better than a single ten-second generation, because each clip was short enough to stay consistent.

Common artefacts and what causes them

AI video has a recognisable set of flaws. Knowing them helps you spot bad clips quickly and understand which prompts are likely to cause trouble.

  • Warping faces. Faces are the hardest thing for video models to keep stable. A face may wobble, shift, or change expression oddly as the clip plays, especially in close-ups.
  • Hands and fingers. Hands appear, disappear, and change shape more often than any other body part. Fingers merge, multiply, or bend in impossible ways.
  • Morphing objects. An object can slowly change into something else over a few seconds: a cup becomes a vase, a car becomes a different car. This is drift, and it is worse in longer clips.
  • Scrambled text. Signs, labels, and on-screen words often come out as convincing-looking gibberish. If your video needs readable text, add it in editing.
  • Floaty motion. Movement can look too smooth and weightless, like objects drifting underwater. This happens when the model has not learned the physics of the scene well enough.
  • Flicker. Small details may shimmer or change between frames, especially in textures like grass, water, or fabric.

These artefacts appear most in busy scenes, fast movement, and close-ups. Simple scenes with slow, steady motion produce the cleanest results. If a clip has a bad artefact, regenerating it with a simpler prompt often helps more than trying to fix the flawed version.

What the model does not understand

A video generator does not understand the world the way you do. It has learned patterns from videos, but it has no real knowledge of physics, cause and effect, or what things mean. That gap shows up in predictable ways.

Physics is the biggest gap. A generated ball might bounce the wrong way, a person might walk through a table, or water might flow uphill. The model knows what these scenes usually look like, but it does not know the rules that govern them, so it sometimes produces motion that looks right at first glance and wrong on a second look.

Counting and order are also weak. Asking for exactly three birds, or for a sequence where one thing happens and then another, often fails. The model treats the prompt as a general description, not as a set of instructions to follow precisely. Simple scenes with one clear action work best.

Meaning is the third gap. A model can generate a person smiling, but it does not know why someone smiles. A scene that needs an emotional beat, a joke, or a moment of surprise is hard to prompt, because the model responds to visual patterns, not to ideas. This is why human editing matters so much: the editor supplies the meaning that the generator cannot.

How generation time and quality relate

Generating a video takes time, and the wait depends on the clip length, the resolution, and how busy the service is. Short, low-resolution clips return quickly. Longer or higher-resolution clips take longer. Some tools offer a choice between a fast draft and a slower, higher-quality result.

A useful habit is to generate a quick draft first to check the composition and the motion, and only then generate the final version at higher quality. Drafts cost less time and let you catch problems early, such as a wrong camera angle or a movement that does not work. The final generation should be a refinement, not a gamble.

Quality settings also affect artefacts. Higher-quality generations are usually cleaner, with less flicker and more stable faces, but they are not a cure-all. A bad prompt produces a bad clip at any quality setting. The prompt and the scene matter more than the quality slider.

Finally, remember that generation is only part of making a video. The clips a generator produces are raw material. Reviewing them, picking the best, cutting the weak parts, and assembling them into a finished video is where the real work happens, and it is the part no generator can do for you.

Questions people ask

How does text-to-video work?

You describe a scene in words, and the model generates a short video that matches the description. It creates the opening frame from your text, then predicts how each following frame should change, based on patterns it learned from training videos.

What is the difference between text-to-video and image-to-video?

Text-to-video starts from words alone, so the model invents the whole scene. Image-to-video starts from a picture you provide and animates it, so the characters, objects, and setting stay closer to your starting image. Image-to-video usually gives more control over how things look.

Why are AI video clips so short?

Video is far more demanding than images: every second contains many frames, and the model must keep the scene consistent across all of them. Longer clips need much more computing power and are more likely to drift, with objects changing shape or disappearing, so most tools limit clips to a few seconds.

What are common artefacts in AI video?

Common artefacts include warping faces, extra or missing fingers, objects that melt or change shape, text that scrambles, and motion that looks floaty or unnatural. They appear most often in busy scenes, fast movement, and close-ups of hands and faces.

Can AI video generators make sound?

Some video generators can add simple sound, music, or speech, but many produce silent clips. Sound quality varies widely, and spoken words in generated clips are often garbled. For a finished video, most creators add music, voiceover, and sound effects separately in an editing tool.
