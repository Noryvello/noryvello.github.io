Heat control: flame you can see vs heat you set

A gas burner gives you a visible flame you can turn up or down and see change at once. That instant response suits cooking that needs quick changes — searing, then dropping to a simmer. An electric hob takes longer to heat up and longer to cool down, so changes arrive more slowly. Electric gives back steadiness: once it reaches a setting, it holds it evenly, which suits long, gentle simmers.

Running cost: think in sessions, not in stoves

Neither fuel is cheaper everywhere. Gas often costs less per unit of energy, but a gas burner loses more heat around the sides of the pan, while an electric hob puts more of its energy into the pan itself. The honest way to compare is to cost a typical cooking session with your own rates, which is what the calculator above does. Example, using the tool's default values: 1.2 kWh of gas at 0.08 per unit costs 0.096 for one session, while 0.9 kWh of electricity at 0.15 per unit costs 0.135 — so gas comes out lower by 0.039 for that session. Change the rates and the answer can flip. For boiling water alone, a kettle is usually cheaper than either stove — see the kettle vs stove comparison.

Safety and cleaning

Gas brings a flame and a gas supply, so it asks for extra care: never leave a lit burner alone, and make sure the kitchen has ventilation. If you ever smell gas when no burner is lit, turn the supply off and get help. Electric hobs remove the open flame, but the surface stays hot after cooking — a ring can still burn long after it looks cool, so treat every ring as hot until you are sure it is not.

Cleaning is where electric usually wins: a flat glass top wipes clean in one pass, while a gas hob has pan supports, burner caps, and crevices that collect spills and need scrubbing.

Which suits whom?

Gas suits cooks who value instant control and already have a gas supply — especially those who stir-fry, char, or sear at high heat. Electric suits kitchens without a gas connection, households that prefer a flat, easy-clean surface, and anyone who wants steady low heat without watching a flame. Induction is a middle path: gas-like response from an electric supply, at the cost of needing compatible pans. If you mostly reheat leftovers, the stove matters less — see the microwave or oven and air fryer comparisons.

Is gas always cheaper than electric?

No. The cheaper fuel depends on your local rates and how much energy each method uses for the same meal. Run the calculator with your own rates — the answer is personal, not universal.

Questions people ask

How do I calculate stove running cost?

Multiply the energy used in a cooking session by the price per matching energy unit, then multiply by the number of sessions.

Can I compare gas and electricity directly?

Yes, if both usage values and rates are expressed on a comparable energy basis, such as cost per kilowatt-hour equivalent.

Does the calculator include appliance efficiency?

Only indirectly through the energy-use values you enter. It does not measure burner or cookware efficiency.

Why do local rates matter?

Gas and electricity tariffs differ by location, provider, tariff type, and billing structure, so your own rates are needed for a useful estimate.
