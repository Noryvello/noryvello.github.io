Garden Layout Planner

A good garden starts on paper: this guide shows you how to map the sun, size your beds, space your plants, group companions and rotate crops. It includes a printable planning worksheet, and it covers layout design only, not watering.

This page is about layout design, not watering: a separate garden-watering planner lives on ToolBari. Spacing numbers here are general guidance for common vegetables, so always check your seed packet for the exact spacing of the variety you are growing.

Start with the sun: sun mapping

Sunlight is the first thing to plan around, because you cannot move it and every plant needs a known amount of it. Most vegetables need at least six hours of direct sun a day, while leafy crops such as lettuce and spinach manage with four to six, and a few herbs tolerate partial shade. Before you draw a single bed, spend a day watching your garden: note where the sun falls in the morning, at midday and in the late afternoon, and which spots stay shaded by walls, fences or trees.

Draw a simple sketch of your garden and mark the sunny and shady areas at each time of day. This is your sun map, and it decides where everything goes: sun-loving crops such as tomatoes, peppers and courgettes in the sunniest spots, leafy crops where the shade falls in the afternoon, and nothing that needs full sun in a corner that only sees two hours of light. Remember that the sun's path changes through the year: a spot that is sunny in June may be shaded in October, when the sun is lower. Plan for the growing season, not for a single day.

Measuring your space and sizing your beds

Measure your garden before you plan it. Pace it out or use a tape measure, and draw it to a rough scale on paper: a square of graph paper per metre works well. Mark the fixed features first: the house, walls, fences, trees, paths, the water tap and the compost heap. Then mark the sunny and shady areas from your sun map. Only then start drawing beds.

Beds should be no wider than you can comfortably reach across from one side, about 1.2 metres for a bed you reach from both sides, or about 60 centimetres for a bed against a wall. Any wider and you will end up stepping on the soil to reach the middle, which compacts it and damages the roots. Paths between beds need to be at least 45 centimetres wide for walking, and 60 to 90 centimetres if you use a wheelbarrow. Raised beds suit heavy or wet soil and make weeding easier on the back; beds at ground level suit dry soil and cost nothing to build. Either way, keep the layout simple: straight beds are easier to plan, plant and water than clever shapes.

Spacing rules

Spacing is the part of planning that beginners most often skip, and it is the part that matters most once the plants start to grow. Plants that are too close together compete for light, water and food, grow weak and leggy, and trap damp air that encourages disease. Plants spaced correctly grow stronger, need less watering, and are easier to harvest. Small seeds such as carrots are hard to sow at exact spacing, so sow them thinly and thin the seedlings out later, leaving the strongest at the right distance. Tall plants such as runner beans and tomatoes go on the north side of a bed in the northern hemisphere, so they do not shade the shorter crops behind them. And remember that a seedling is small but the adult plant is not: a courgette that looks tiny in May will cover a square metre by July, so give it the space it will need, not the space it needs today.

Companion planting basics

Companion planting is the idea that some plants grow better next to each other, and some grow worse. The evidence for many companion claims is thin, and gardeners disagree about the details, so treat it as useful tradition and observation rather than settled science. The combinations with the best support are practical ones: tall plants shading heat-sensitive crops, strong-smelling herbs confusing pests, and flowers attracting pollinators to the vegetable beds.

Good neighbours to try: basil near tomatoes, marigolds near almost anything, because their roots discourage some soil pests, and nasturtiums near brassicas, because aphids prefer the nasturtiums and leave the cabbage alone. Beans and peas fix nitrogen in the soil, which helps the leafy crops that follow them. Combinations to avoid: onions and garlic near beans and peas, which they can stunt, and fennel near most vegetables, because it does not share well. Keep a notebook of what worked in your garden, because your soil and your climate matter more than any list.

Crop rotation

Crop rotation means not growing the same family of plants in the same bed year after year. The reasons are practical: pests and diseases that attack one family build up in the soil if that family stays put, and different families take different nutrients from the soil. A simple rotation divides crops into four groups and moves each group to the next bed every year: roots such as carrots and potatoes, leaves such as lettuce and cabbage, fruits such as tomatoes and courgettes, and legumes such as beans and peas.

With four beds, the rotation is easy: each bed hosts one group this year and a different group next year, so no group returns to the same bed for four years. With fewer beds, rotate as far as you can and accept that the rotation will be imperfect; even a two-year gap helps. Legumes are the key to the system, because they put nitrogen back into the soil, so follow them with hungry leafy crops that benefit from it. Potatoes are useful too: digging them up breaks up the soil and clears weeds, which leaves a clean bed for the next crop.

Planning through the seasons

A good layout plans for the whole year, not just for spring. When one crop finishes, another should take its place: this is called succession planting. Lettuce sown every two or three weeks gives a steady supply instead of a single glut. Early peas can be followed by late beans in the same bed. Garlic planted in autumn is harvested in early summer, leaving the bed free for a late crop of courgettes or salad leaves.

Think about timing when you draw your plan. Mark the sowing date and the expected harvest date for each crop, so you can see which beds will be free and when. Leave a little space unplanned: there is always a plant that fails, a seedling that needs a home, or a neighbour who offers you a spare tomato plant. And plan for the end of the season too: clear finished crops promptly, compost what is healthy, and sow a green manure or cover the bare soil with mulch over winter so the rain does not wash the goodness away.

A worked example: one small bed planned step by step

Imagine a single raised bed, 2.4 metres long and 1.2 metres wide, in a spot that gets seven hours of sun. The gardener wants tomatoes, lettuce and basil. First, the sun: seven hours is full sun, so all three crops will grow here. Second, the layout: the tomatoes go along the north side so they do not shade the rest, with canes for support. Third, the spacing: at 50 centimetres apart, four tomato plants fit along the 2.4 metre length, leaving room at the ends.

Fourth, the companions: basil goes between the tomatoes, where it enjoys the same sun and water, and lettuce fills the front of the bed, where the tomatoes will shade it a little in the hottest part of the day. Fifth, the timing: the tomatoes and basil go in after the last frost, while the lettuce is sown two weeks earlier and again every three weeks for a steady supply. Sixth, the rotation: next year this bed will hold roots, such as carrots and onions, and the tomatoes will move to another bed. One bed, one plan, and every decision follows from the sun, the spacing and the season.

Common layout mistakes

The most common mistake is planting too much, too close together. A garden plan looks empty in spring and crowded by July, so trust the spacing numbers even when the bed looks bare. The second mistake is putting tall plants on the sunny side, where they shade everything behind them all day. The third is forgetting the paths: a bed you cannot reach without stepping on the soil is a bed you will stop tending, and an untended bed is a weedy bed.

The fourth mistake is planning for spring only and leaving beds empty for the rest of the year. The fifth is growing the same crop in the same place every year and wondering why the harvest gets smaller. The sixth is copying a plan from a book without adjusting it to your own sun, soil and climate. A plan is a guide, not a contract: change it when your garden tells you to, and keep notes so next year's plan is better than this one.

Questions people ask

How much sun do vegetables need?

Most vegetables need at least six hours of direct sun a day. Leafy crops such as lettuce and spinach manage with four to six hours, and a few herbs tolerate partial shade. Fruiting crops such as tomatoes, peppers and courgettes need the sunniest spot you have.

How wide should a raised bed be?

No wider than you can comfortably reach across from one side. About 1.2 metres works for a bed you can reach from both sides, and about 60 centimetres for a bed against a wall. Any wider and you will step on the soil to reach the middle, which compacts it.

What is companion planting?

Companion planting means growing plants together that help each other: tall plants shading heat-sensitive crops, strong-smelling herbs confusing pests, and flowers attracting pollinators. The evidence for many claims is thin, so treat it as useful tradition and keep notes on what works in your garden.

Why should I rotate crops?

Because pests and diseases that attack one plant family build up in the soil if that family stays in the same bed, and different families take different nutrients. Moving each group to a new bed every year breaks the cycle and keeps the soil healthier.

How do I use the planning worksheet?

Print the page and fill in one row per bed: the crop, the hours of sun, the spacing and the sowing date. Keep the sheet where you can see it and add notes through the season about what grew well and what failed, so next year's plan is better.

Can I use this planner for containers?

Yes. The same principles apply: map the sun, give each plant its spacing, and rotate what you grow in each pot from year to year. A large pot is a small bed, and a balcony with several pots is a small garden with its own sun map.
