AI Scams to Watch For: Voice Clones, Fake Videos, and Phishing

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

AI has made scams more convincing: a cloned voice can sound like your child in trouble, a fake video can show a celebrity endorsing a product they never touched, and phishing messages can be written in flawless language. This guide covers the five most common AI scams and gives you a short verification checklist for each. The rule behind every checklist is the same: pause, verify through a separate channel, and never act on urgency alone.

Scams used to be easy to spot. The spelling was wrong, the story was strange, and the voice on the phone did not sound like anyone you knew. AI has removed most of those clues. A scammer can now clone a voice from a few seconds of audio, write a perfect message in any language, and put a familiar face on a fake video. The scams feel personal because they are built from real pieces of real people.

That sounds frightening, but the defence has not changed as much as the attack. Every scam still needs you to act quickly, without checking, usually by sending money, sharing a code, or clicking a link. If you break that chain, by pausing and verifying through a channel the scammer does not control, most AI scams fall apart. The checklists below show you how, scam by scam.

Never send money, gift cards, or verification codes because a call, message, or video told you to, however urgent or convincing it sounds. Real emergencies can wait the few minutes it takes to verify through a number or channel you already trust. If someone pressures you not to check, that pressure is itself the clearest warning sign.

Why AI made scams more convincing

The old defence against scams was spotting mistakes. A message full of errors was easy to dismiss, and a stranger's voice on the phone was easy to doubt. AI tools have taken those mistakes away. They write clean, natural messages, they copy voices well enough to fool family members, and they generate faces and videos that look real at a glance.

AI also makes scams cheap to run at scale. One scammer can now send thousands of personalised messages, each one addressed to the right person and mentioning real details from their public posts. The message feels hand-written because it was tailored, even though a machine wrote it in seconds.

The good news is that scammers still need the same thing they always needed: your quick, unthinking action. A cloned voice, a fake video, and a perfect message are all just ways to create urgency and trust. If you slow down and check, the technology behind the scam stops mattering, because the scam itself cannot survive a careful look.

Voice-clone family emergency calls

This is the scam that frightens people most, and for good reason. You get a call that sounds exactly like your child, your parent, or your partner. They say they have been in an accident, they have been arrested, or they are stranded, and they need money urgently. The voice is real enough to fool you, because it was cloned from a short recording, perhaps from a voice message or a video they posted.

The scammer usually adds details to make it believable: they may know your family member's name, where they live, or where they were travelling. They will beg you not to call anyone else, and they will keep you on the line so you cannot check. The urgency is the weapon. It stops you from thinking.

The defence is a family code word, agreed in advance. Pick a word or phrase that only your family knows, and agree that any genuine emergency call will include it. A scammer cannot guess it, and a real family member in trouble can say it. If you do not have a code word yet, set one up today: it takes a minute and it defeats this scam completely.

Verification checklist for a family emergency call:

  • Hang up and call your family member back on the number you already have for them, not on any number the caller gives you.
  • Ask for the family code word. If the caller cannot give it, treat the call as a scam.
  • Call another family member and ask whether they have heard from the person.
  • Never send money, gift cards, or codes while you are still on the call.

Fake celebrity endorsements

You see a video of a famous person praising a product, a treatment, or an investment, and it looks completely real. The face moves naturally, the voice matches, and the setting looks like a genuine interview. But the person never said any of it. AI tools stitched their face and voice onto a script written by a scammer.

These fakes usually appear as adverts on social media, often with a dramatic claim: a miracle cure, a secret investment, or a product the celebrity supposedly uses every day. The advert links to a shop or a sign-up page that takes your money or your card details. The celebrity's fame is the bait; the link is the trap.

Real celebrity endorsements are easy to check. A genuine endorsement appears on the celebrity's own channels and in real news coverage. A fake one appears only in adverts, usually from an account you have never heard of, and disappears when you search for it alongside the celebrity's name.

Verification checklist for a celebrity endorsement:

  • Search for the celebrity's name and the product together, and see whether any trusted source reports the endorsement.
  • Check the celebrity's own official accounts. A real endorsement will be there.
  • Look at the advertiser's account. A new account with few posts and no history is a warning sign.
  • Never buy from a link in the advert itself. Find the product through a channel you trust, if you want it at all.

AI phishing messages

Phishing messages try to get you to click a link or share a password, a code, or account details. The old ones were full of mistakes. AI-written phishing is clean, polite, and personalised: it may mention your name, your job, or a recent order, and it may copy the style of a real message from your bank, your workplace, or a delivery company.

The message usually creates a reason to act now: your account will be locked, a parcel will be returned, or a payment has failed. The link looks real, but it leads to a fake page designed to collect your details. Once you type them in, the scammer has them.

The strongest defence is to never use the link in the message. If a message claims to be from your bank, your employer, or a delivery company, open the official app or type the official address yourself. A real problem will still be there when you check through the official channel, and a fake one will vanish.

Verification checklist for a suspicious message:

  • Do not click the link or reply to the message. Open the official app or website yourself instead.
  • Check the sender's address or number carefully. Small changes, like an extra letter, are a common trick.
  • Ask whether the organisation would contact you this way. Banks and delivery companies rarely ask for passwords or codes by message.
  • When in doubt, call the organisation on a number you already trust, not on any number in the message.

Fake job offers

A message arrives offering a well-paid job, often working from home, with flexible hours and little experience needed. The interview happens by chat, the questions are easy, and you are offered the job almost at once. Then comes the catch: you must pay for training, buy equipment, or share your bank details and identity documents before you start.

AI makes these scams look professional. The job description is well written, the company website looks real, and the recruiter's messages are polite and prompt. Some scams even use fake video interviews with AI-generated interviewers. The polish is designed to make you feel chosen, and to rush you past the warning signs.

Real employers do not ask you to pay to start work, and they do not hire you after a chat interview alone. A genuine offer comes with a contract, a verifiable company, and time to think. If any of those are missing, step back and check.

Verification checklist for a job offer:

  • Search for the company and the job title together, and look for the role on the company's own careers page.
  • Check whether the recruiter's email and profile match the company. A free email address for a large company is a warning sign.
  • Never pay for training, equipment, or background checks before you start. Real employers do not charge new hires.
  • Never send identity documents or bank details until you have a signed contract and have verified the company independently.

Fake investment videos

A video shows a well-known investor or business leader promising remarkable returns from a new opportunity, often involving cryptocurrency or trading. The video may be a deepfake, or it may be a real clip with a fake voiceover. Either way, the promise is the same: easy money, limited places, act now.

The video usually links to a platform that looks professional, with charts, testimonials, and a dashboard showing your money growing. The dashboard is fake. Small early withdrawals may even work, to build trust, before larger deposits disappear. By the time the victim realises, the platform and the people behind it are gone.

No genuine investment promises guaranteed returns, and no genuine adviser pressures you to act in minutes. The combination of a famous face, a secret opportunity, and a deadline is the signature of this scam, whatever the video shows.

Verification checklist for an investment video:

  • Search for the person's name and the opportunity together. A real investment is reported by trusted financial news, not only in adverts.
  • Ask whether the returns are guaranteed. Guaranteed high returns do not exist in real investing.
  • Check whether the platform is registered with the financial regulator in your country. An unregistered platform is a warning sign.
  • Never invest because a video told you to. Talk to a qualified adviser through a channel you trust, and take time to decide.

If you think you have been targeted

If you have already sent money or shared details, act quickly and without shame. Contact your bank at once and explain what happened; they may be able to stop a transfer. Change any passwords you shared, and turn on two-step verification where it is available. Report the scam to the platform where you found it and to the relevant authorities.

If you only suspect a scam and have not acted on it, you have already won. Block the sender, delete the message, and warn anyone else who may have received it. A short message to family or colleagues can stop the same scam from reaching them.

Finally, talk about it. Scams thrive on silence and embarrassment, and they fail when people share what they saw. Telling your family about the voice-clone trick, or your colleagues about the fake job offer, protects more people than any single checklist. The best defence against AI scams is a community that knows what to look for.

Questions people ask

How can scammers clone a voice from such a short recording?

Voice cloning tools need only a few seconds of clear speech to build a usable copy, and the source can be a voice message, a video, or a call recording. The clone captures the tone and accent well enough to fool someone who is not expecting a fake, especially on a noisy phone call.

Is a family code word really enough to stop voice-clone scams?

Yes, for this scam. A scammer cannot guess a code word they have never heard, and a real family member in trouble can say it. Pick something unusual, share it only within the family, and agree that any emergency call without the code word is treated as fake.

What should I do if a message looks real but I am not sure?

Do not click anything in the message. Contact the organisation through a channel you already trust, such as the official app or a number you have used before. A genuine message will match what the organisation tells you, and a fake one will not.

Can AI scams target children?

Yes. Children can be targeted through games, messages, and fake offers, and their voices and photos can be used to target their parents. Talk to children about not sharing personal details or voice messages with strangers, and agree a family code word that includes them.

Should I report a scam if I did not lose anything?

Yes. Reporting helps platforms remove the scam and helps authorities track patterns. It takes a few minutes, and it can protect the next person who receives the same message or call.
