How to Unclog a Drain

Most slow drains clear with a plunger, a drain snake or a trap check, and the right order depends on the drain. Pick your drain type below for tailored steps, and use the checklist to know when to call a plumber.

Be careful with chemical drain products: they can burn skin and eyes, damage pipes and make a plumber's job dangerous. Never mix cleaning products, and if you have already used a chemical product, tell the plumber before they start work.

Before you start

Clear the area around the drain and put down old towels. Drain work is wet and messy, and a few towels on the floor save a long clean-up. If there is standing water in the sink or bath, bail most of it out into a bucket first: a plunger works far better in a few centimetres of water than in a full basin.

Put on gloves and have a bucket ready. You will need the bucket for the trap check, and gloves make the whole job more pleasant. If you have used a chemical drain product recently, say so before anyone else works on the drain, and do not reach into the water with bare hands.

Plunger first

Always start with the plunger. It is the simplest tool, it works on most blockages, and it tells you a lot: if the water moves, the blockage is close and soft. Use a sink plunger with a flat cup for sinks and baths, and make sure the cup covers the drain fully with enough water in the basin to cover the rim of the cup.

Block the overflow hole with a wet cloth first, or the pressure escapes through it instead of pushing at the blockage. Then plunge with short, firm strokes, keeping the cup sealed to the drain. After fifteen or twenty strokes, pull the plunger away sharply and see if the water drains. Repeat a few times before you move on to anything else.

The drain snake

If the plunger does not clear it, the blockage is further down or more solid, and a drain snake is the next step. A snake is a flexible coil that you feed into the drain and turn, so it either breaks the blockage up or hooks it so you can pull it out. Feed it in slowly, turning as you go, and do not force it around bends.

When you feel resistance, turn the snake a few times, then pull it back slowly and see what comes with it. Hair, soap and grease are the usual finds in bathroom drains, and food and grease in kitchen drains. Run water after each pass to check whether the drain is clear, and repeat until the water runs freely.

Checking the trap

If the blockage is still there, it may be sitting in the trap, the U-shaped bend under the sink that holds water to block smells. Put the bucket under the trap, then unscrew the trap by hand or with gentle pressure from a wrench. Water will spill out, which is what the bucket is for.

Empty the trap into the bucket, clear out whatever is inside, and check the pipe beyond it with a finger or a cloth. Refit the trap firmly but do not overtighten it, run the tap, and check underneath for drips. A trap that leaks after refitting usually just needs the ring tightened a little more, or the washer reseated.

What not to do

Do not keep pouring water into a drain that is not moving: it will only overflow. Do not force a snake around a bend, or use a wire coat hanger, which can scratch pipes and get stuck. And do not mix cleaning products, ever. If one product has not worked, adding another will not help and can create dangerous fumes.

Keeping drains clear

Prevention is easier than clearing. Use a strainer in kitchen and bathroom sinks, and never pour cooking grease down the drain: let it cool and put it in the bin. Clear hair from shower drains regularly, and run hot water through kitchen drains after washing up. Small habits like these are the difference between a drain that runs freely and one that blocks every few months.

Questions people ask

Why does my drain keep blocking?

Build-up is the usual cause: hair and soap in bathroom drains, grease and food in kitchen drains. If the same drain blocks again within days of clearing it, the blockage may be further down the pipe, or the pipe itself may need a plumber to look at.

Can I use a plunger on any drain?

A plunger works on most sinks, baths and showers. Use a flat-cup plunger for sinks and block the overflow hole with a wet cloth first, so the pressure goes to the blockage. Keep the cup sealed to the drain and use short, firm strokes.

What is the trap under the sink?

The trap is the U-shaped bend in the pipe under the sink. It holds a small amount of water that stops drain smells coming back into the room. It is also where blockages often collect, which is why checking it is part of clearing a stubborn drain.

How do I stop drains blocking?

Use a strainer in sinks, never pour cooking grease down the drain, and clear hair from shower drains regularly. Run hot water through kitchen drains after washing up. These small habits prevent most blockages.

Is it safe to take the trap apart?

Yes, if you are confident and prepared: put a bucket underneath, unscrew the trap by hand, and be ready for the water inside to spill out. Refit it firmly and check for drips. If the trap will not budge, or you are unsure, call a plumber.
