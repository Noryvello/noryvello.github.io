Solar Eclipse 2027 for Kids

By the Noryvello team · Published 19 November 2026 · Updated 19 November 2026

On Monday 2 August 2027, the Moon will slide in front of the Sun and turn day into a strange twilight for a few minutes. This guide explains the eclipse in simple words, with fun activities and the safety rules every family needs.

Imagine the Sun slowly disappearing in the middle of the day. The sky gets darker, the air feels cooler, and then, for a few minutes, the Sun is gone, replaced by a dark circle with a glowing ring around it. That is a total solar eclipse, and on 2 August 2027 it will happen across southern Spain, North Africa and the Middle East.

This guide is written for families. It explains what an eclipse is in simple words, where it will happen, how to watch it safely, and what to do on the day to make it a memory children will keep for years.

Children must never look at the Sun without certified eclipse glasses, except during the few minutes of totality. An adult should be in charge of safety at all times.

What is a solar eclipse?

A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes between the Earth and the Sun, and the Moon's shadow falls on the Earth. The Moon is much smaller than the Sun, but it is also much closer to us, so from Earth the two look almost the same size in the sky. That is why the Moon can cover the Sun so neatly.

The Moon's shadow is a narrow band, only about 258 kilometres wide, which sweeps across the Earth as the Moon moves. If you are standing inside that band, you see a total eclipse: the Sun disappears completely for a few minutes. If you are outside it, you see only a partial eclipse, where the Moon takes a bite out of the Sun. On 2 August 2027, the shadow travels from southern Spain across North Africa and the Middle East to north-eastern Somalia.

What will it look like?

An eclipse does not happen all at once. First, the Moon takes a small bite out of the edge of the Sun, and the bite grows slowly bigger over about an hour. The light changes in a strange way: shadows look sharper, colours look slightly odd, and the air feels cooler. Birds may go quiet, as if evening were coming early.

Then comes totality, the main event. The last bit of sunlight disappears, the sky darkens to a deep twilight, and the Sun's outer atmosphere, called the corona, appears as a glowing ring around the dark Moon. Bright stars and planets may come out. In Luxor, in Egypt, totality lasts about 6 minutes 20 seconds, from about 1:02 to 1:08 p.m. local time. Then the Sun returns, and daylight comes back as if nothing had happened.

Where in the world will it happen?

The eclipse crosses a long line of countries. It starts in southern Spain, in places such as Cádiz, Málaga, Granada and Almería, and includes Gibraltar, Ceuta and Melilla. Then it crosses Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia and Libya, then Egypt, where the longest totality on land happens near Luxor. After Egypt it crosses Saudi Arabia and Yemen, and ends in north-eastern Somalia.

The longest totality on land is about 6 minutes 23 seconds, in the Egyptian desert near Luxor, and it is the longest total solar eclipse visible from land until 2114. That makes the 2027 eclipse a special one: many people alive today will never see a longer one. Some famous places miss out: Cairo sees only a partial eclipse, and Aswan is not in the path at all.

How to watch safely

The safety rule is simple and must never be broken: never look at the Sun without certified eclipse glasses, except during the few minutes of totality. Eclipse glasses are special glasses that carry the mark ISO 12312-2. Ordinary sunglasses are never safe, no matter how dark they are. Put the glasses on before you look up, and look away before you take them off.

During totality, when the Sun's bright disc is completely covered, it is safe to look with your eyes alone. The moment the Sun starts to come back, the glasses go straight back on. An adult should be in charge of the timing. A pinhole viewer is another safe way to watch: it projects a picture of the eclipse onto paper, so you never look at the Sun at all.

Make a pinhole viewer

You can make a pinhole viewer in a few minutes with things from home. Take a piece of stiff card and make a small, clean hole in the middle with a pin. On the day, stand with your back to the Sun, hold the card up, and let the sunlight pass through the hole onto a second piece of white paper or the ground. You will see a small picture of the Sun, and as the eclipse goes on, the picture changes shape.

Never look through the hole at the Sun: the viewer works by projection, and looking through it would be dangerous. You can also use a kitchen colander or a straw hat with holes in it. Hold it up and look at the ground: you will see dozens of little eclipse pictures at once, which is one of the loveliest sights of the day.

Fun eclipse activities

An eclipse is a whole afternoon of things to do, not just a few minutes of looking up. Before the day, children can draw what they think the eclipse will look like, and then draw what it actually looked like afterwards. On the day, try drawing the shadows of trees: as the eclipse progresses, the gaps between leaves act like tiny pinhole viewers and project hundreds of little crescent Suns onto the ground.

Keep an eclipse diary: write down the time, what the light looks like, what the birds are doing, and how the temperature feels. During totality, count the seconds out loud together, and see how far you get before the Sun returns. Afterwards, write down the three things you will remember most. Years later, that diary will be worth more than any photograph.

Questions kids ask

Why does an eclipse not happen every month? Because the Moon's orbit is tilted compared to the Earth's orbit around the Sun, so the Moon's shadow usually passes above or below the Earth. Only when the Sun, Moon and Earth line up exactly does the shadow fall on us, and that is rare.

Will it go completely dark? Not quite: totality feels like a deep twilight rather than midnight, and the horizon often stays lighter than the sky overhead. Will animals notice? Birds often go quiet or head to roost, and other animals may behave as if evening has arrived. And will it happen again? Total eclipses happen somewhere on Earth every year or two, but the same place may wait hundreds of years between them, which is why the 2027 eclipse is worth the trip.

Eclipse words to know

Eclipses come with their own special words, and knowing them makes the day more fun. Totality is the few minutes when the Moon covers the Sun completely and the sky goes dark. A partial eclipse is what people outside the path see, when the Moon takes only a bite out of the Sun. The path of totality is the band, about 258 kilometres wide, where the Moon's shadow falls and totality can be seen.

The corona is the Sun's outer atmosphere, a glowing ring that appears around the dark Moon during totality. It is always there, but it is normally hidden by the Sun's brightness, and an eclipse is the only time you can see it with your eyes alone. Greatest eclipse is the moment when the eclipse is at its most complete, which on 2 August 2027 happens near Luxor at about 10:07 UTC. Just before and after totality, the last bit of sunlight can sparkle like a diamond ring, which is why people call it the diamond ring effect.

Questions people ask

Will the sky go completely dark?

Not quite. Totality feels like a deep twilight rather than midnight, and the horizon often stays lighter than the sky overhead. Bright stars and planets may come out.

How long does the eclipse last?

Totality, when the Sun is completely covered, lasts up to about 6 minutes 23 seconds near Luxor in Egypt. In Luxor itself it runs from about 1:02 to 1:08 p.m. local time.

Can I look at the eclipse?

Only with ISO 12312-2 certified eclipse glasses, except during totality, when it is safe to look with your eyes alone. Put the glasses back on the moment the Sun starts to reappear.

Why doesn't an eclipse happen every month?

Because the Moon's orbit is tilted, its shadow usually passes above or below the Earth. Only when the Sun, Moon and Earth line up exactly does the shadow fall on us.

What should I bring on the day?

Eclipse glasses, a pinhole viewer, water, a hat, sunscreen, and something to sit on. Bring a notebook too, so you can keep an eclipse diary.

Will animals notice the eclipse?

Yes. Birds often go quiet or head to roost, and other animals may behave as if evening has arrived early. Watching the animals is part of the fun.
