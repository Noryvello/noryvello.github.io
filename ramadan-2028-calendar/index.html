How to read the table

Each row is one day of Ramadan, numbered from 1 to 29, with the weekday and the Gregorian date. The notes column flags the days that matter most: the first fast, the start of the last ten nights, and the five odd nights.

Note that the Islamic night comes before the Islamic day. The 21st night, for example, is the night that begins at sunset on Wednesday 16 February and ends at dawn on Thursday 17 February, the 21st day of Ramadan. That is why the odd nights are listed by their sunset start in the notes.

The last ten nights

The last ten nights of Ramadan begin at sunset on Wednesday 16 February 2028, the eve of 21 Ramadan. These are the most blessed nights of the year, and Laylat al-Qadr, the Night of Decree, is sought among the five odd nights within them.

The five odd nights and their sunset starts are: the 21st night from sunset on Wednesday 16 February, the 23rd from sunset on Friday 18 February, the 25th from sunset on Sunday 20 February, the 27th from sunset on Tuesday 22 February, and the 29th from sunset on Thursday 24 February 2028. The 27th night is the most widely observed, but the exact night is hidden, so Muslims seek it in all five.

Many people spend the last ten nights in extra prayer, reading the Quran, and giving charity. Those who are able may observe i'tikaf, staying in the mosque for the last ten days of the month in devotion.

If Ramadan has 30 days

The Islamic month is either 29 or 30 days long, depending on the sighting of the new moon. The Umm al-Qura calendar expects Ramadan 1449 to have 29 days, with the last fast on Friday 25 February 2028 and Eid al-Fitr on Saturday 26 February.

If the new moon is not sighted on the evening of Friday 25 February, the month completes 30 days instead. In that case, 30 Ramadan falls on Saturday 26 February 2028, and Eid al-Fitr moves to Sunday 27 February. The decision is made by the moon-sighting announcement in each country, so the two cases can differ from place to place — one country may celebrate Eid on Saturday while another celebrates on Sunday.

This is normal, and it is why the page labels every date as expected. As the end of the month approaches, follow your local mosque or authority for the confirmed announcement.

Using this calendar

The calendar is a planning aid. It helps you see at a glance which Gregorian dates the month covers, when the last ten nights begin, and which nights are the odd nights. It does not give prayer times — those depend on your location, and your local mosque timetable is the right source for them.

If you are planning time off work for the last ten nights or for Eid, it is worth planning around both possible Eid dates, Saturday 26 February and Sunday 27 February, until the moon-sighting announcement confirms which one it is.
