How to Spot Deepfake Videos

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

A deepfake is a video in which a person's face or voice has been replaced or altered by AI, often to make them appear to say or do something they never did. This guide shows you how to check the face, the voice, and the source of a suspicious clip, step by step. No single check is foolproof, so the guide also explains what to do when you still cannot tell.

Video used to be the strongest kind of evidence. If you could see someone say something, it was hard to doubt it. AI video tools have weakened that trust, because a convincing fake can now be made from a few photos and a short voice sample. The fakes are not perfect, and knowing where to look still catches most of them.

The checks below are ordered by how quickly you can run them. Start with the face and the voice, which you can judge while watching. Then check the body and the background, and finally the source of the clip. The whole routine takes a few minutes, and it is worth doing before you share anything dramatic.

Detection tools and checklists are imperfect, and the best deepfakes can pass them all. A clip that passes every check here is not proven real. For anything important, look for independent confirmation from a trusted source, not just a video.

What a deepfake actually is

A deepfake is a video or audio clip in which AI has replaced or altered a person's appearance or voice. The most common kind swaps one person's face onto another person's body, or makes a real person appear to say words they never spoke. The name comes from deep learning, the technology behind the tools that make them.

Not every altered video is a deepfake. Ordinary editing, filters, and special effects have existed for decades. What makes a deepfake different is that AI builds the fake face or voice from examples of the real person, so the result can look and sound like them in ways that simple editing cannot match.

Deepfakes are used for jokes and films as well as for scams and misinformation. The same technique that puts an actor's younger face on screen can put a politician's face on a fake speech, or a family member's voice on a scam call. The checks in this guide work the same way whatever the purpose.

Watch the face: blinking, expressions, and edges

Start with the eyes. People blink at irregular intervals, and their eyes move naturally while they speak. Early deepfakes often had no blinking at all, and newer ones can still get the rhythm wrong: blinks that are too regular, too rare, or timed oddly with the speech. Watch a full minute if you can, not just a few seconds.

Next, watch the expressions. A real face moves as a whole: when someone smiles, the eyes narrow, the cheeks lift, and the mouth moves together. In a deepfake, the mouth may move well while the rest of the face stays flat, or the expression may lag behind the words. Look for a face that feels pasted on, even if you cannot say exactly why.

Finally, look at the edges. The boundary where the face meets the hair, the ears, and the neck is a weak point. You may see a faint line, a change in skin texture, or hair that behaves oddly around the face. Pause the video and zoom in if your player allows it. Still frames often reveal what moving video hides.

Listen to the voice: tone, timing, and lip-sync

A fake voice often sounds right in the first few seconds and wrong over a longer listen. Real speech has natural pauses, breaths, and changes in speed. A cloned voice can sound too smooth, with even pacing and little variation, or it can stumble on unusual words and names. Listen for breathing: real speakers breathe audibly, and a voice with no breaths at all is suspicious.

Lip-sync is one of the strongest checks. Watch the mouth closely while the person speaks. In a real video, the lips match the sounds exactly. In a deepfake, the lips may lag behind the voice, move too little for the words being said, or shape the wrong sounds, especially on words with hard consonants and rounded vowels.

It also helps to know how the person normally sounds. If you have heard the real person speak, compare the tone, the accent, and the favourite phrases. A deepfake often captures the voice but misses the person's habits: the way they pause, the words they choose, or the jokes they make. A speech that sounds like the person but does not sound like them is worth doubting.

Check the body and the background

The face gets the most attention from the faking tool, and the rest of the frame gets less. Watch the hands and the shoulders while the person talks. In a real video, gestures match the words and the body moves naturally. In a fake, the hands may be still when the voice is animated, or the shoulders may move in a way that does not fit the speech.

Look at the background too. A real room has consistent light, steady objects, and a stable camera. In a deepfake, the background may shimmer, objects may wobble slightly, or the lighting on the person may not match the lighting in the room. These faults are subtle, but they often show up if you watch the edges of the frame rather than the face.

Low quality is itself a warning sign. Many fakes are shared as small, blurry, or heavily compressed clips, which hides the faults. If a dramatic clip is only available in poor quality, and no clear version exists anywhere, ask why. A genuine recording of an important moment usually exists in good quality somewhere.

Check the source before you believe the clip

A video is only as trustworthy as its source. Ask who posted it and why. A clip posted by a news organisation, an official account, or a person with a long public history is easier to check than the same clip reposted by an unknown account with a dramatic caption. The original poster's identity matters more than the number of shares.

Look for the same moment from another angle. Real events are usually filmed by more than one person, and real speeches are usually reported by more than one outlet. If only one clip of a supposedly public event exists, and no one else has reported it, that is a strong reason to doubt it.

Check the date and the context. Old clips are often reposted with new captions, and real clips are often trimmed to change their meaning. Search for the person's name and the topic, and see whether trusted sources are reporting the same thing. Silence from every trusted source is itself information.

Reverse image search and other checks

A reverse image search can tell you whether a frame from the video has appeared before. Take a screenshot of a clear frame, preferably one showing the person's face, and search for it. If the same face or scene appears in an older video with a different story, the clip you are watching may be a reuse or a fake.

You can also search for the exact words in the clip. Copy a distinctive phrase and search for it in quotation marks. Real speeches leave a trail: transcripts, reports, and quotes. A fake speech usually has no trail at all, because the words were never spoken in public before the clip appeared.

Some detection tools can analyse a video and give a probability that it is fake. They are useful as an extra check, but they are not decisive. They can be wrong in both directions, and the newest fakes are designed to fool them. Treat a detection result as one more piece of evidence, not as a verdict.

What to do when you find a deepfake

Do not share it, even to warn others, without making the warning unmistakable. A repost with a small note saying fake still spreads the image and the claim. If you must share it, describe it in words and link to a fact-check, rather than reposting the clip itself.

Report the clip on the platform where you found it. Most platforms have a reporting option for manipulated media or impersonation. Reporting takes a minute, and it helps the platform limit the clip's spread. If the fake targets a real person, consider letting them know through an official channel.

Finally, tell the people who shared it with you, kindly and plainly. Most people who share a deepfake do not know it is fake. A short message with a link to a fact-check, or a note about which check gave it away, is usually enough. The goal is to slow the spread, not to embarrass anyone.

Questions people ask

Can deepfakes be detected reliably?

Not yet. Detection tools and visual checks catch many fakes, but the best ones can pass them all. The most reliable approach is to combine the checks in this guide with source checks and independent confirmation from trusted outlets.

Why is lip-sync such a good check?

Matching lips to speech is hard for AI, because the mouth must shape each sound at exactly the right moment. Small delays and wrong mouth shapes are easy to miss at full speed but show up when you watch closely, especially on words with hard consonants.

Are all face-swap videos deepfakes?

A face swap made with AI is a deepfake by definition, but not all of them are harmful. Some are jokes, films, or art. The problem is not the technique itself but how it is used: to deceive, to impersonate, or to spread false claims.

What should I do if someone makes a deepfake of me?

Save evidence of the clip and where you found it, report it to the platform, and tell people you trust so they are not fooled. If the fake is used to scam or harass you, report it to the relevant authorities. You do not have to handle it alone.

Do deepfakes only target famous people?

No. Famous people are common targets because there is plenty of video of them to train on, but ordinary people are targeted too, especially in scams. A short voice clip from a video call or a voice message can be enough to clone a voice for a scam call.
