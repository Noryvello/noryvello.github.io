Financial Literacy Learning Hub

Financial literacy is the set of everyday skills that turn income into a plan: budgeting, saving, understanding interest, and handling debt and credit. This hub explains each idea in plain language, with worked examples you can follow.

This page is education only, not financial advice. The examples use plain numbers in your own currency, and your situation may be different. For a decision about your own money, talk to a qualified adviser.

Why money basics matter

Most people learn about money by making mistakes with it. A budget that is never written down, a credit card paid late, a loan taken without understanding the interest: these are ordinary experiences, and they are expensive teachers. Financial literacy is simply learning the ideas first, so the mistakes are smaller and fewer.

The ideas are not complicated. Money in, money out, money saved, money owed: four ideas, and everything else is built from them. A budget is a plan for money in and money out. Saving is money set aside on purpose. Interest is the price of borrowing, or the reward for lending. Debt is money owed, and credit is the ability to borrow it. Once those four ideas are clear, the rest of money is detail.

This hub explains each idea in plain language, with worked examples in plain numbers. The numbers are examples only, in your own currency, and they are chosen to be easy to follow. The ideas matter more than the numbers, and they work at any income.

Budgeting: the 50/30/20 idea

A budget is a plan for your money before the month starts, not a record of where it went afterwards. The simplest useful budget divides your income into three parts: needs, wants and savings. A well-known rule of thumb, often called the 50/30/20 rule, suggests about half your income for needs, about three-tenths for wants, and about two-tenths for savings and extra debt payments.

Needs are the things you must pay for: housing, food, transport to work, basic clothing and essential bills. Wants are the things that make life pleasant but are not essential: eating out, holidays, new clothes beyond the basics, and subscriptions. Savings covers your emergency fund, your long-term savings and any extra payments towards debt.

Here is a worked example. Suppose your monthly income is 4,000. The 50/30/20 idea would suggest about 2,000 for needs, about 1,200 for wants, and about 800 for savings and extra debt payments. That is 4,000 times 50 percent, 30 percent and 20 percent. If your rent is high and your needs already take 2,600, the rule will not fit neatly, and that is fine: the rule is a starting point, not a law. Use it to see where your money goes, then adjust the shares to your real life.

Start by writing down your income and your fixed bills, then track the rest for a month. Most people are surprised by at least one thing they spend on.

Saving: pay yourself first

Saving works best when it happens first, not last. If you wait until the end of the month to save what is left, there is usually nothing left. Paying yourself first means moving money to savings on payday, before you spend anything, so saving is a fixed bill like rent.

Start with an emergency fund: money set aside for the things you cannot plan, such as a broken appliance, a medical bill or a gap between jobs. A common guideline is three to six months of essential expenses, but that is a goal to build towards, not a starting point. Even a small fund, enough to cover one month of essential bills, changes how a surprise feels.

Keep the emergency fund somewhere safe and easy to reach, such as an ordinary savings account, and do not invest it. Its job is to be there when you need it, not to grow. Once the fund is in place, you can think about longer-term saving, such as saving for a goal or for later life, and about the trade-off between keeping money safe and letting it grow.

  • Save on payday, before you spend. A fixed amount moved automatically is the easiest habit to keep.

Interest: simple versus compound

Interest is the price of using someone else's money, or the reward for letting someone else use yours. When you borrow, you pay interest. When you save or lend, you earn it. The rate is usually quoted as a percentage per year, and the two ways it is calculated, simple and compound, behave very differently over time.

Simple interest is calculated only on the original amount. If you lend 1,000 at 5 percent simple interest a year, you earn 50 a year, because 5 percent of 1,000 is 50. After ten years you have earned 500, and the total is 1,500. The interest never earns interest of its own.

Compound interest is calculated on the original amount plus the interest already earned. At 5 percent a year, compounded yearly, 1,000 grows to 1,050 after one year, then 5 percent of 1,050 is 52.50, so the second year ends at 1,102.50. After ten years the same 1,000 is worth about 1,628.89. The difference between 1,500 and 1,628.89 is the interest earning interest, and it grows larger every year.

Compound interest works for you when you save, and against you when you borrow. That is why the same idea appears in both the saving and the debt sections of this hub. Time is the ingredient that makes compounding powerful: starting early matters more than starting big.

Debt: how it grows and how to pay it down

Debt is money you owe, and it grows when the interest is not paid. A loan of 1,000 at 20 percent a year costs about 200 in interest in a year if nothing is repaid, because 20 percent of 1,000 is 200. If you pay only part of the interest, the rest is added to the balance, and next year you pay interest on a larger amount. That is compound interest working against you.

The first step with debt is to list it: every loan and card, the balance, the interest rate and the minimum payment. Seeing the whole list is uncomfortable, but you cannot make a plan for a number you will not look at. Then pay the minimum on everything, so nothing falls behind, and put any extra money towards one debt at a time.

There are two well-known ways to choose which debt to attack first. The avalanche method pays the highest interest rate first, which costs the least interest overall. The snowball method pays the smallest balance first, which gives quick wins and keeps you motivated. Both work. The avalanche saves more money, and the snowball is easier to stick with. Pick the one you will actually follow.

  • List every debt: balance, interest rate and minimum payment.

If debt is more than you can handle, ask for help early. Many places have free, independent debt advice services, and a lender will often agree to a payment plan if you contact them before the payments are missed. Ignoring debt never makes it smaller.

Credit: scores, cards and the minimum-payment trap

Credit is the ability to borrow money, and a credit card is the most common form of it. When you use a credit card, the card company lends you the money for the purchase, and you repay it later. If you repay the full balance within the interest-free period, the borrowing costs nothing. If you do not, interest is charged on what is left, and credit card interest rates are usually high.

The minimum payment is the smallest amount the card company will accept each month. Paying only the minimum keeps the account in good standing, but it stretches the repayment over years and the total interest can exceed the original purchase. That is the minimum-payment trap: the payment feels small, so the debt feels manageable, while the interest quietly grows the balance.

A credit score is a number that summarises how you have handled borrowing in the past, based on your payment history, how much you owe, and how long you have had credit. Lenders use it to decide whether to lend to you and at what rate. The details vary by country, but the habits that build a good score are the same everywhere: pay on time, do not borrow more than you can repay, and keep your oldest accounts open.

  • Pay the full card balance every month if you can. That is the cheapest way to use a card.

A simple monthly money routine

Money skills are habits, and habits need a routine. This one takes about an hour a month, and it keeps the ideas in this hub working for you.

  1. On payday, move your saving first, before you spend anything.

That is all it takes. An hour a month, a short list, and the habit of looking at the numbers instead of avoiding them. The people who are good with money are rarely the people who earn the most. They are the people who pay attention.

Common money mistakes to avoid

Most money mistakes are not dramatic. They are small, repeated and easy to miss, which is why they cost so much over time.

  • Spending first and saving what is left. There is rarely anything left. Save first.

If you recognise yourself in that list, you are in good company. The point of learning money basics is not to feel bad about the past. It is to make the next month a little better than the last one, and to keep doing that until the habit is yours.

Questions people ask

What is the 50/30/20 budget rule?

The 50/30/20 rule is a simple way to divide your income: about half for needs such as housing, food and transport, about three-tenths for wants such as eating out and holidays, and about two-tenths for savings and extra debt payments. It is a rule of thumb and a starting point, not a law: if your rent is high, your shares will differ, and that is fine.

How much should I save each month?

A common guideline is to save about a fifth of your income, but any regular amount is a good start. Save on payday, before you spend, and start with an emergency fund that covers a month of essential bills, building towards three to six months over time. A small amount saved every month beats a large amount saved never.

What is compound interest?

Compound interest is interest calculated on the original amount plus the interest already earned, so the interest earns interest of its own. At 5 percent a year, 1,000 grows to about 1,628.89 after ten years with compounding, compared with 1,500 with simple interest. It works for you when you save and against you when you borrow.

Should I pay off debt or save first?

Do a little of both: build a small emergency fund first, so a surprise does not become a new debt, then put your extra money towards the debt. Pay the minimum on every debt, and attack one debt at a time, either the highest interest rate first or the smallest balance first. Once the expensive debt is cleared, raise your saving.

What is a credit score?

A credit score is a number that summarises how you have handled borrowing in the past, based on your payment history, how much you owe, and how long you have had credit. Lenders use it to decide whether to lend and at what rate. The habits that build a good score are simple: pay on time, do not borrow more than you can repay, and keep your oldest accounts open.

How do I start budgeting with a small income?

Start with the numbers you know: write down your income and your fixed bills, then track the rest for a month. Group the spending into needs, wants and savings, and look for one change you can make, such as cooking more or cancelling a subscription you do not use. A budget is most useful on a small income, because every amount matters more.
