7 AI Video Mistakes Beginners Make

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

Most disappointing AI videos fail for the same seven reasons: bloated prompts, unreadable text, missing motion words, no shot plan, blind trust in first outputs, skipped editing, and one subject doing too much. This guide names each mistake and gives you the fix.

AI video looks easy. You type a sentence, a clip appears, and for a moment it feels like magic. Then you watch the clip closely and notice the problems: a hand that melts into a cup, a sign that reads as gibberish, a scene that barely moves. Beginners usually blame the tool, but most of the time the cause is one of a handful of avoidable mistakes.

This guide lists the seven mistakes that cause the most trouble, in the order you are most likely to meet them. Each one comes with a fix you can apply on your very next clip. None of the fixes needs special skill, only a little patience.

AI video tools and their limits change often, so treat the fixes here as working habits rather than permanent rules. Every tool has its own quirks, and the only reliable test is watching your own clips closely before you publish them.

Mistake 1: Writing overlong prompts

Beginners often write prompts that read like short stories: three paragraphs of scene-setting, backstory, camera directions, and adjectives. The result is usually a muddled clip, because the model has to guess which parts of the prompt matter most. When everything is emphasised, nothing is.

The fix is to keep each prompt to one shot and a few clear details. Name the subject, one action, the setting, and a couple of words about light and mood. A prompt like "a baker sliding a tray of bread into a brick oven, warm morning light, cozy bakery" gives the model a clear target. If you want more, generate a second clip instead of stuffing more into the first prompt.

Long prompts also make it harder to learn what went wrong. When a short prompt fails, you can usually see which detail caused the problem. When a long prompt fails, you have no idea where to start fixing it.

Mistake 2: Expecting readable text on screen

Asking an AI video tool to show a readable sign, label, or title is one of the fastest routes to disappointment. Letters in generated clips often come out warped, misspelled, or as pure gibberish. This is a known limit of current video models, not a flaw in your prompt, and no amount of rewording reliably fixes it.

The fix is to keep text out of the generation entirely. Generate the scene without words, then add titles, captions, and labels in a video editor, where the text will be crisp and correct. The same goes for logos and brand names: if the words must be right, add them after generation, not during it.

This also applies to numbers. A clock face, a price tag, or a calendar in a generated clip will rarely show the digits you asked for. Plan your shots so that any text the audience must read is added by you, in editing.

Mistake 3: Ignoring motion words

Many first clips come back looking like still photos with a faint shimmer. The usual cause is a prompt that describes a scene but never says what should move. The model fills in the motion itself, and the safest guess is usually almost none.

The fix is to put at least one clear motion word in every prompt. Walking, turning, rising, falling, drifting, spinning, pouring, and opening are all motion words. "A woman walking through a market, turning to look at a fruit stall" will move far more than "a woman in a market." Describe the movement you want, not just the scene it happens in.

Be specific about who or what moves. If you want the camera to move, say so. If you want the subject to move, say so. A prompt that leaves motion to chance usually gets a clip that barely stirs.

Mistake 4: Having no shot plan

Beginners often generate clips one at a time with no plan, hoping a video will somehow assemble itself. The result is a folder of unrelated clips that do not cut together: different lighting, different pacing, different subjects, and no story connecting them.

The fix is to plan the shots before you generate anything. Write down the shots you need in order, one line each: an opening wide shot, a close-up of the subject, a detail shot, a closing shot. Decide the mood and the look for the whole video first, then use the same style words in every prompt so the clips feel like they belong together.

A shot plan also saves credits. Every clip generated without a purpose is a wasted generation. Ten planned clips will usually beat thirty random ones, because planned clips are the ones you actually use.

Mistake 5: Trusting the first output

The first clip a tool generates is a draft, not a finished shot. Beginners often accept it because generating again feels like a waste, or because the small preview looks fine. Then the flaws show up in the final video: a warped hand, a background that flickers, an object that appears halfway through and vanishes.

The fix is to treat every first output as a test. Watch it at full size, from start to finish, before you decide to keep it. If something is wrong, change one thing in the prompt and generate again. Changing one thing at a time teaches you what each word does; changing everything at once teaches you nothing.

It also helps to generate two or three versions of an important shot and pick the best. The extra generations cost a little time, but a clean shot is worth far more than a flawed one you have to explain away.

Mistake 6: Skipping the edit

AI video tools make clips, not finished videos. Beginners sometimes publish a raw generated clip as if it were a complete video, and the result feels unfinished: no opening, no pacing, no captions, no music, no ending. The audience notices, even if they cannot say why.

The fix is to treat generation as the first half of the job and editing as the second. Trim the weak frames from the start and end of each clip. Cut between shots to create pace. Add captions, music, and a clear opening and closing. Even a few minutes of editing lifts a raw clip into something that feels finished.

Editing is also where you hide the flaws you cannot fix. A warped hand at the end of a clip disappears if you trim the last second. A flickering background matters less if the shot only lasts two seconds. Good editing covers a surprising number of generation sins.

Mistake 7: Asking one clip to do too much

The last mistake is asking a single short clip to carry a whole story: a character who enters, speaks, picks something up, and leaves, all in a few seconds. The model cannot stage that much action in one shot, so the clip comes back confused, with actions blending into each other.

The fix is one action per clip. If your scene needs three actions, make three clips and cut between them. Film and television work the same way: a scene is built from shots, and each shot does one job. AI video is no different, and accepting that makes your work much stronger.

This habit also makes your prompts shorter and your results more reliable. A prompt that asks for one clear action is far easier for the model to follow than a prompt that asks for a sequence.

A quick checklist before you generate

Before your next session, run through this list. Is the prompt one shot, with one subject and one action? Does it include a motion word? Is there any text the model would have to render? Do you know where this clip fits in your shot plan? Will you watch the full clip before keeping it? Will you edit it before publishing?

If you can answer yes to all six, you have already avoided most of the mistakes in this guide. The rest comes with practice. Every clip you make teaches you something about what the tool does well and what it does badly, and that knowledge is worth more than any single prompt trick.

Questions people ask

Why do my AI videos look like still photos?

Usually because the prompt describes a scene but never says what should move. Add at least one clear motion word, such as walking, turning, or rising, and name the subject that should move.

Can AI video tools show readable text?

Not reliably. Letters and numbers in generated clips often come out warped or misspelled. Generate the scene without text and add titles and captions in a video editor instead.

How many times should I regenerate a clip?

There is no fixed number. Watch each version at full size, change one thing in the prompt when something is wrong, and stop when the clip is clean enough for its place in your edit.

Is a shot plan really necessary for short videos?

Yes, even for a ten-second video. A short list of shots in order keeps the lighting, mood, and pacing consistent, and it saves credits by cutting out generations you will never use.

What is the single biggest beginner mistake?

Publishing raw clips without editing. Trimming weak frames, cutting between shots, and adding captions and music is what turns generated clips into a finished video.
