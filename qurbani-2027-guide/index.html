Qurbani 2027

Qurbani is the sacrifice offered at Eid al-Adha, remembering the willingness of Prophet Ibrahim (AS). In 2027 it is offered on the 10th, 11th and 12th of Dhul-Hijjah, after the Eid prayer, with Eid al-Adha expected on Sunday 16 May 2027. This guide explains who offers Qurbani, which animals count, how shares work, and how the meat is divided.

Qurbani, also called udhiyah, is one of the central acts of Eid al-Adha, the festival that closes the Hajj season. Each year, Muslims who are able offer an animal in remembrance of the willingness of Prophet Ibrahim (AS), and the meat is shared with family, neighbours and people in need. The rules are straightforward, but the details vary between schools of thought, so it is worth knowing the basics before the days arrive.

This guide covers the 2027 dates, who Qurbani is owed by, which animals may be offered, how shares work for larger animals, and how the meat is divided. All dates are expected and depend on the sighting of the moon.

What is Qurbani?

Qurbani is the sacrifice of an animal offered during the days of Eid al-Adha. It remembers the willingness of Prophet Ibrahim (AS), who was prepared to sacrifice what he loved most in obedience to Allah, and it is an act of worship tied to the Hajj season. The sacrifice is not only a ritual: the meat is meant to be shared, so that the celebration reaches beyond the household to relatives, neighbours and the poor.

Eid al-Adha itself is the festival of sacrifice, and Qurbani is offered on its days, after the Eid prayer. The Day of Arafah, the day before Eid, is expected on Saturday 15 May 2027, and Eid al-Adha is expected on Sunday 16 May 2027. Both dates are expected and depend on the sighting of the moon, so follow your local mosque or authority's announcement for the confirmed dates.

When is Qurbani in 2027?

Qurbani is offered on the 10th, 11th and 12th of Dhul-Hijjah, and only after the Eid prayer. Eid al-Adha is expected on Sunday 16 May 2027, which is the 10th of Dhul-Hijjah, so the days of Qurbani in 2027 begin on that day and run through the 12th. The timing matters: slaughter before the Eid prayer does not count as Qurbani, however early it is done.

The schools of thought differ slightly on the last day. The Shafi'i school extends the days of Qurbani to the 13th of Dhul-Hijjah, while the majority view ends the time at sunset on the 12th. In practice, most people arrange their Qurbani for the first day, the day of Eid itself, and treat the following days as a fallback if the first day is not possible. Whatever day you choose, confirm the Eid date with your local mosque first, since all of these dates are expected and depend on the sighting of the moon.

Who has to offer Qurbani?

Whether Qurbani is obligatory depends on the school of thought you follow. In the Hanafi school it is wajib, meaning obligatory, for a sane, adult, resident Muslim who holds the nisab threshold. In the Shafi'i, Maliki and Hanbali schools it is a strongly emphasized Sunnah, known as Sunnah mu'akkadah — an act the Prophet practised regularly and that Muslims are strongly encouraged to perform, though it is not classed as obligatory.

Qurbani is owed per person, not per family. Each person who meets the conditions offers their own Qurbani, so a household with more than one qualifying adult has more than one Qurbani to arrange. Children are not expected to offer Qurbani themselves, and the obligation does not fall on someone who is travelling or who does not hold the nisab.

The nisab is the zakat nisab: 85 grams of gold or 595 grams of silver, with 612.36 grams of silver in the Hanafi conversion. This guide states the gram weights only and gives no fixed cash figure, because the cash value of gold and silver changes. To work out whether you hold the nisab, ask your local scholars or mosque for the current value in your area.

Which animals can be offered?

The animals offered for Qurbani are sheep, goats, cows, buffalo and camels. A sheep or a goat counts for one person only: one animal, one Qurbani. A cow, a buffalo or a camel is larger, so it can be shared by up to seven people, with each person taking one share. Sharing a large animal is a common way for families and groups to arrange Qurbani together, and each share counts as one person's Qurbani.

The commonly cited minimum ages for the animals are: sheep 6 months, provided the animal is well-grown; goats 1 year; cows 2 years; and camels 5 years. The animal must be healthy and free of major defects, in general terms — an animal that is clearly sick, lame or otherwise unfit is not suitable. If you are unsure whether an animal qualifies, ask your local scholars before you buy.

How is the meat divided?

The recommended way to divide Qurbani meat is in three parts: one part for the family, one part for relatives, friends and neighbours, and one part for the poor. This three-way split is the most widely taught way of sharing the meat, and it reflects the purpose of Qurbani — that the celebration reaches beyond the household and that people in need share in the festival.

It is important to be clear about the status of this division: it is recommended, not obligatory. Scholars differ on the details of how the meat should be divided, and there is no single binding rule that every Muslim must follow. What matters is that the meat is shared, and that a portion reaches people who need it. If your local scholars or community follow a particular way of dividing the meat, follow their guidance.
