How to use this portal

Start with the table below. Pick the job you are interested in and the experience level that matches you, and read the band for that row. The band is shown as a multiple of a typical local monthly wage — for example, a band of 0.9 to 1.2 means the job typically pays between nine tenths and one and a fifth of the typical wage in the area. The bands are broad on purpose: they show the shape of pay for a role, not a promise about one particular job.

Then use the calculator above. Enter the monthly offer you have, and the typical monthly wage where you live — a number you can estimate from job listings, friends in similar work, or your own experience. The calculator works out the ratio of your offer to the typical wage and tells you whether it sits below the band, inside it, or above it. Copy the result if you want to keep it. Nothing you enter leaves your phone, and nothing is sent to a server.

An experienced developer at 1.3 to 1.9 earns roughly one and a third to almost twice the typical wage. Using multiples keeps the comparison honest across countries and cities, because the actual money amount changes everywhere while the shape of pay between roles is steadier.

Three things to remember when you read the table. First, the bands are wide because real pay is wide: two people with the same title can earn very different amounts. Second, the bands describe full-time work; part-time pay scales down with the hours. Third, the bands are illustrative — they show typical patterns, not a figure any employer must match. If your offer sits inside the band, it is in the usual range for that role and level. If it sits below, it may still be fair for a small employer or a quiet city, but you should know it before you accept. If it sits above, it is generous for the role — or the job may ask more of you than usual.

What changes pay most

Three things move pay more than anything else. The first is the skill itself: work that takes years to learn and few people can do usually pays more than work anyone can start tomorrow. That is why a developer or a nurse sits higher in the table than a shop assistant — not because one job matters more, but because the training is longer and the supply of workers is smaller.

The second is the city. The same job in a big, expensive city usually pays more than in a small town, but the extra pay often goes straight into rent and travel, so the higher number is not always a better life. The third is experience, and it works in steps rather than a smooth climb: the first year or two usually brings the biggest rise, then pay grows more slowly until a promotion or a move to a new employer lifts it again.

Other things matter too: the size of the employer, whether the job includes nights or weekends, and how urgently the employer needs someone. A small shop may pay less but offer steady hours; a large company may pay more but expect more. When you compare two offers, compare all of it — pay, hours, travel, and what the job asks of you — not just the headline number.

Gross vs net: reading a salary offer

Every salary offer has two numbers, and the difference between them causes more confusion than anything else in job hunting. The gross figure is the full amount before anything is taken off. The net figure is what actually reaches you after tax, insurance, pension, or any other deduction. An offer that quotes only the gross figure can look much better than it is, and an offer that quotes only the net figure can look worse than it is.

Before you compare two offers, make sure both numbers are the same kind: gross with gross, net with net. Ask the employer which figure they are quoting if the ad does not say. Also check what is included: some offers bundle overtime, holiday pay, or a transport allowance into the headline figure, while others list them separately. A monthly figure and a yearly figure are easy to mix up too — always work out the monthly amount you will actually receive, and the hours you will work for it. The calculator on this page compares monthly amounts, so if your offer is yearly, divide it by twelve first.

Negotiating with the range in hand

A range is useful only if you use it, and the time to use it is before you accept, not after. Start by finding where your offer sits in the band for your role and level. If it sits below the band, you have a clear, polite case: the usual range for this role at my level is higher, and I would like to discuss it. Name a number inside the band, not above it, and give one reason — your experience, a skill the job needs, or the hours involved.

If the offer sits inside the band, you can still negotiate, but the room is smaller: ask about the top of the band, or about benefits instead of pay. If the employer cannot move on pay at all, ask about the other things that have real value: extra leave, training, a review date, or help with travel. Keep the tone calm and friendly; you are starting a working relationship, not winning an argument. And know your walk-away point before the conversation starts. If the offer is below the band and the employer will not move, it is better to say no politely than to accept a job that leaves you resentful in the first month.

A worked example: two offers, side by side

Take two offers for the same person: a designer with three years of experience, living in a city where the typical monthly wage is 1000 in local units. The first offer is from a small studio: 1100 a month, a short walk from home, normal hours, no overtime. The second is from a large agency: 1500 a month, an hour of travel each way, and regular late nights.

The band for an experienced designer is 1.1 to 1.6 times the typical wage, so the first offer sits at 1.1 — the bottom of the band — and the second sits at 1.5 — near the top. On pay alone, the agency wins. But the comparison is not finished. The studio saves two hours of travel every day, which is roughly forty hours a month of your life back. The agency adds late nights, which cost rest and health. The studio may offer slower growth; the agency may offer faster learning and a stronger name on your record.

The calculator on this page would place the first offer inside the band at its lower edge, and the second inside the band near its top — both are fair pay for the role. The right choice depends on what matters more to you right now: time, money, or learning. That is why the portal shows bands instead of single numbers: a fair salary is a range, and the best offer is the one that fits your whole life, not just the highest figure.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is comparing gross with net — two offers that look far apart can be almost the same once deductions are counted. The second is ignoring benefits: paid leave, health cover, a pension, or a travel allowance can be worth a large share of the pay, and a job with a lower headline figure but real benefits can leave you better off.

The third is trusting one number from one website. Salary figures online are often old, often from another city, and often mixed together from jobs with the same title but very different duties. The fourth is forgetting overtime: a job that pays a little more but expects many unpaid extra hours can pay less per hour than a simpler job. The fifth is comparing your pay with a friend in a different city or a different industry — the numbers are not comparable, and the comparison only causes unhappiness. The sixth is accepting the first offer without asking whether there is room to move. Many employers expect a short negotiation, and a polite question costs nothing.

What this portal cannot do

This portal shows broad illustrative bands, not live salary data. It cannot look up what a particular employer pays, it cannot tell you what you personally should earn, and it cannot predict whether an offer will rise. The bands are patterns, not promises: a real offer below the band may still be fair for a small employer or a quiet town, and a real offer above the band may come with demands that make it less attractive than it looks.

The calculator compares the numbers you enter against those bands — it does not fetch real salaries from anywhere, and nothing you enter leaves your phone. Use the portal to understand the shape of pay and to prepare for a negotiation, then check current job listings and ask real employers for real numbers before you decide.

Questions people ask

What is a fair salary for my job?

A fair salary is one that sits inside the usual range for your role, your experience, and your city — and that fits the hours and demands of the job. Use the bands on this page to see the usual range, then check current job listings in your area, because real pay varies widely from place to place.

Why does the same job pay differently in two cities?

Pay follows the cost of living and the local demand for workers. A big, expensive city usually pays more, but rent and travel often take the extra money back. A smaller town may pay less but cost much less to live in, so the lower number can still leave you better off.

Gross salary or net salary — which matters?

Both matter, but the net figure is what reaches you each month, so it is the one to live on. Always compare gross with gross and net with net, and ask the employer which figure they are quoting if the offer does not say.

How do I negotiate a better salary?

Find where your offer sits in the band for your role and level, then ask politely for a number inside the band, with one clear reason. If pay cannot move, ask about leave, training, a review date, or travel help. Keep it calm and friendly, and know your walk-away point before you start.

Are these salary figures exact?

No. The bands on this page are broad and illustrative only, shown as multiples of a typical local wage. They are not live salary data and not a promise of what any job pays. Real pay varies widely by city, employer and experience, so check current job listings before you decide.

What benefits should I count as pay?

Count anything with real money value: paid leave, health cover, a pension, a travel allowance, meals, or training the employer pays for. Add their rough value to the salary before you compare two offers, because a lower headline figure with strong benefits can be worth more than a higher figure with none.
