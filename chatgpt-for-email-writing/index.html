How to Write Better Emails with ChatGPT

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

ChatGPT can turn a rough email idea into a clear, polite message in seconds. This guide shows you a prompt pattern that works for any email, then applies it to five common email types, each with a before-and-after example.

Most weak emails fail in the same ways: they bury the point, they ramble, or they strike the wrong tone. ChatGPT is good at fixing exactly those problems, because it can reshape a draft around one clear purpose.

The trick is giving the AI what it needs: who the email is for, what you want, and how you want to sound. This guide shows you the pattern, then walks through five email types you will actually send.

Never paste private details into a chatbot when drafting an email. Keep names, addresses, account numbers, and sensitive work details out of your prompts, and add them to the finished email yourself. Always read the final draft before sending: AI can invent details or strike a tone you did not intend.

The Prompt Pattern Behind Every Good Email

Every good email prompt has five parts. Who is the email for? What do you want from them? Why does it matter, in one line? How should it sound: formal, friendly, or firm? And how long should it be? Answer those five questions in your prompt and the draft comes back close to finished.

A pattern that works for most emails is: "Write a [tone] email to [person] asking for [thing]. Explain why I need it by [date], keep it under [length], and end with a clear question." The five email types below each use this pattern, shaped for the situation, so you can copy the pattern and fill in your own details.

The Request Email

Prompt pattern: "Write a polite request email to [person] asking for [thing]. Explain why I need it by [date], keep it under 80 words, and end with a clear question."

Before: "Hi, I was wondering if maybe you could possibly send me the report when you get a chance? No rush at all, but it would be great whenever. Thanks so much, sorry to bother you."

After: "Hi Sarah, could you please send me the sales report by Thursday? I need the figures for a meeting on Friday morning. If anything is missing, let me know and I will follow up. Thank you."

The after version names the person, the thing, and the date, and it asks one clear question. Politeness comes from clarity, not from apologising. The reader knows exactly what to do and when, and the email takes ten seconds to answer.

The Follow-Up Email

Prompt pattern: "Write a short follow-up email to [person] about [topic]. Remind them of my first message on [date], restate the question in one line, and ask for a reply by [date]. Keep the tone friendly."

Before: "Just following up on my previous email. Let me know. Thanks."

After: "Hi James, I am following up on my message from Monday about the venue booking. Could you confirm whether the hall is free on the 14th? A reply by Wednesday would help me plan. Thank you."

A good follow-up restates the question, because the reader may not remember your first message. One clear ask beats a vague nudge, and a deadline stated as a help to your planning sounds firm without sounding pushy.

The Apology Email

Prompt pattern: "Write a short apology email to [person] about [mistake]. Take responsibility, say what went wrong in one sentence, and explain what I will do to fix it. Keep it sincere and under 80 words."

Before: "Sorry about the delay, things have been really busy and it was out of my hands. Hope you understand."

After: "Hi Maria, I am sorry the delivery arrived two days late. I misjudged the packing time, and that is my fault. I have sent a replacement today, and it will reach you by Friday. Thank you for your patience."

The after version names the mistake, owns it, and gives a fix. Blaming circumstances makes an apology sound hollow, while a short, honest account of what went wrong and what happens next rebuilds trust.

The Thank-You Email

Prompt pattern: "Write a thank-you email to [person] for [specific help]. Name what they did, say what it made possible, and keep it warm and under 60 words."

Before: "Thanks for your help. Really appreciate it."

After: "Hi David, thank you for walking me through the new system yesterday. Your examples made the whole process clear, and I finished my first report this morning. I really appreciate the time you gave me."

Naming the specific help turns a generic thanks into a message people remember. One concrete detail is worth more than a long list of polite words, and it shows the reader that you noticed what they actually did.

The Cold Outreach Email

Prompt pattern: "Write a short cold email to [person] at [company] introducing [what I offer]. Open with one line about their work, explain the benefit in two sentences, and end with one simple question. Keep it under 100 words."

Before: "Dear Sir or Madam, I am writing to introduce our company, a leading provider of innovative solutions. We offer a wide range of services and would love to discuss how we can help your business grow. Please let me know a good time to talk."

After: "Hi Anna, I enjoyed your recent post about the spring market. I run a small bakery nearby and would love to supply fresh bread for your cafe's weekend menu. Would a short chat next week work for you?"

The after version opens with something real about the reader, names one clear offer, and asks one simple question. Generic praise and grand claims get deleted; a specific, human opening gets read.

Controlling Tone and Length

Tone is the easiest thing to control in a prompt. Ask for formal when you write to a manager or a client, friendly when you write to a colleague, warm when you thank someone, and firm when you need a clear answer. If the draft sounds wrong, say so: "Make this warmer" or "Make this more direct" usually fixes it in one step.

Length is just as easy. Ask for "under 60 words" for a quick note, "in three sentences" for a simple request, or "as a short list" when the email carries several points. If the first draft is too long, ask for a shorter version rather than starting over: the AI keeps the structure and trims the words.

Before You Hit Send

Run a quick check before sending any AI-drafted email. Is it addressed to the right person? Does the subject line say what the email is about? Are the facts right: names, dates, and figures? Does the tone match the relationship? If the email mentions an attachment, is the attachment actually there?

Finally, read the email once as the person receiving it. Would you know what to do after reading it? Would you feel respected? If the answer to both questions is yes, the email is ready. If not, one more round with the AI, asking for the specific change you want, usually fixes it.

Questions people ask

What should I put in an email prompt?

Say who the email is for, what you want from them, and the tone and length you want. Add the key facts the email must contain, and the AI will shape them into a clear message.

How long should my emails be?

As short as the message allows. Most everyday emails work best in three to five sentences: one to say why you are writing, one or two for the details, and one for the next step.

Can ChatGPT write a formal email?

Yes. Ask for a formal tone and name the situation, such as writing to a manager or a client. Read the result and adjust any phrase that sounds stiff or unnatural to you.

Should I let AI write an apology email?

AI can help you find calm, clear words, but the apology itself must be genuine. Read the draft carefully, make sure it takes responsibility, and send only what you truly mean.

Is it safe to paste an email thread into ChatGPT?

Remove names, addresses, and sensitive details first. Paste only the part you need help with, and describe the rest in general terms.
