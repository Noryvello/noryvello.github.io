How to Write a CV With No Experience — The First-Job CV That Works

By the Noryvello team · Published 21 November 2026 · Updated 21 November 2026

A CV with no work experience is not an empty CV — it is a CV that proves itself differently. This guide shows you the skills-first format, how to turn school and life into proof, and a one-page sample outline you can copy.

Never fake a job or a skill you cannot show. One honest proof beats ten invented lines — and an invented line can cost you the job when it is found out.

Why the usual CV advice fails first-timers

Most CV advice is written for people who already have jobs. It tells you to list your work history in reverse order, to show promotions, to quantify your achievements in your last role. If you have never had a role, that advice leaves you staring at a blank page — and the blank page feels like proof that you have nothing to offer. It is not proof of that. It is proof that the advice was written for someone else.

The first thing to understand is that employers hiring first-timers know you have no work history. They are not expecting five years of experience from a school leaver or a new graduate. What they are looking for is simpler: can this person learn, can they be relied on, and can they show it? A CV that answers those three questions — even without a single job on it — does its work. A CV that apologises for having no experience, or pads itself with invented jobs, does not.

The second thing is that you have more proof than you think. School, college, volunteering, family responsibilities, hobbies, part-time chores that turned into real skills — all of it counts, if you present it as proof rather than as a list. The rest of this guide shows you how: a format built for first-timers, a method for turning your life into evidence, and a sample outline you can copy and fill in.

The functional format in plain words

The usual CV format is chronological: jobs first, newest at the top. For a first-timer, that format is the wrong tool — it puts the emptiest part of your story first. The functional format turns the CV around. It leads with what you can do, and puts dates and places second. That is why it works for first jobs: it shows your strengths before anyone notices what is missing.

A functional CV has five parts, in this order. First, your name and contact details at the top — nothing fancy, just how to reach you. Second, a short summary line, two or three sentences about what you offer and what you are looking for. Third, a skills section: three or four skill groups, each with one line of proof. Fourth, your education, with the subjects and results that matter for the job. Fifth, anything else that proves reliability: volunteering, projects, clubs, awards, or a short line about languages.

Two rules hold the whole thing together. Keep it to one page — a first CV that runs to two pages is almost always padded, and a hiring manager will notice. And put the most relevant skill group first. If the job is in a shop, lead with people skills and reliability. If it is an office job, lead with writing and organisation. The format is the same; the order changes with the job. That small change — reordering for each application — is the difference between a CV that gets read and one that gets skimmed.

Turning school and life into proof (4 worked examples)

The hardest part of a first CV is turning things you have done into proof an employer can trust. The trick is a simple sentence shape: what you did, what it took, and what came of it. Here are four worked examples, each showing how an ordinary experience becomes a CV line.

Example 1 — school project. "Organised a group of five for a term project on local history; divided the research, set the deadlines, and presented the findings to the class." That one line proves teamwork, planning, and speaking in public — three things an employer wants, all from a school assignment. The project itself does not matter. The proof does.

Example 2 — volunteering. "Helped at a weekend food stall for six months, serving customers, handling money, and keeping the stall tidy during busy hours." That proves reliability (six months, every weekend), customer contact, and care with money — and it is honest, checkable, and easy to talk about in an interview.

Example 3 — looking after family. "Looked after two younger siblings after school, including meals, homework help, and getting them to activities on time." Many first-timers leave this off, thinking it is not a job. It is proof of responsibility, time management, and patience — and an employer who reads it will understand exactly what it took.

Example 4 — a hobby with discipline. "Practised piano for four years, passed grade five, and performed at two school concerts." That proves commitment over time and the ability to perform under pressure. Any hobby with a record — sport, coding, drawing, a language — works the same way. The hobby is not the point. The years of showing up are.

The summary line

The summary line sits at the top of your CV, just under your name, and it is the first thing an employer reads. It has one job: to say, in two or three sentences, what you offer and what you are looking for. It is not a life story, and it is not the place for adjectives about yourself. "Hardworking and passionate" says nothing — everyone writes it, and nobody believes it. Proof says everything.

A good first-timer summary follows a simple shape: who you are, what you can show, and what you want. For example: "Recent school leaver with strong written English and six months of weekend volunteering in a busy community cafe. Looking for a first role in retail or hospitality where I can learn fast and work hard." That is two sentences, and it gives an employer three facts: your situation, your proof, and your direction.

Write the summary last, after the rest of the CV is done — it is easier to summarise a page you can see than a page you are still building. And change it for each job. The cafe version and the office version of your summary should not be the same, because the proof that matters is different. One summary per application, written for that job, is worth more than one perfect summary sent everywhere.

The one-page sample CV outline

Here is the full outline, ready to copy and fill in. Keep each section short — the whole CV should fit on one page — and replace every bracketed line with your own true detail.

Your Name
Phone number · Town or city · One line about how to reach you

Summary
Two or three sentences: who you are, what you can show, what you are looking for.

Skills
People and communication — one line of proof, such as volunteering, helping customers, or presenting in class.
Organisation and reliability — one line of proof, such as running a club, managing a schedule, or looking after family.
Practical skills — one line of proof, such as computer skills, a language, driving, or a craft.

Education
School or college name, years attended, subjects studied and results. Include any project or prize that shows effort.

Experience and activities
Volunteering, school projects, clubs, part-time work, family responsibilities — each as one line with proof, in the shape shown in the examples above.

Interests
One short line, only if the interest shows something real: a sport you play regularly, a skill you practise, a language you are learning.

Read the finished page out loud before you send it. If a line sounds like it could belong to anyone, it needs one true detail. If a line sounds like bragging, it needs one fact. And if the page spills onto two pages, cut the weakest line in each section until it fits — a tight one page always beats a loose two.

What never to fake

There are two things you must never put on a CV: a job you never had, and a skill you cannot show. Both are tempting when the page feels empty, and both are worse than leaving the space blank. An invented job is found out quickly — a reference check, a question in an interview, a detail that does not add up — and when it is found out, the application is over. Not just for that job, either: word travels, and a reputation for inventing things follows you.

An invented skill is found out more slowly, which makes it more dangerous. If you claim a language you cannot speak or a program you cannot use, you may get the job — and then spend every day afraid of being asked to do the thing you claimed. That is a miserable way to start a working life, and it ends the same way as the invented job, just later.

The honest alternative is stronger than the fake one. Say what you have, and say it well. "No work experience yet" is not a weakness in a first CV — it is the truth, and employers hiring first-timers expect it. What they do not expect, and do not forgive, is invention. One honest proof beats ten invented lines, every time.

Questions people ask

What should I put on a CV with no experience?

Lead with skills, not jobs: three or four skill groups, each with one line of proof from school, volunteering, family life, or a hobby. Then education, then activities. Employers hiring first-timers look for proof you can learn and be relied on, not for a job history you do not have.

What is a functional CV format?

A functional CV leads with what you can do instead of where you have worked. It has five parts: contact details, a short summary, a skills section with proof, education, and activities. It is the best format for a first job because it shows your strengths before anyone notices the missing work history.

How long should a first CV be?

One page. A first CV that runs to two pages is almost always padded, and a hiring manager will notice. If your CV spills over, cut the weakest line in each section until it fits — a tight one page always beats a loose two.

Can I put school projects on my CV?

Yes — a school project is real proof if you describe it right. Say what you did, what it took, and what came of it: organising a group, meeting a deadline, presenting findings. The project itself does not matter; the proof of teamwork and planning does.

Should I write a different CV for each job?

Yes, at least the summary and the order of your skill groups. Lead with the skills that matter for that job — people skills for a shop role, writing and organisation for an office role. One summary per application, written for that job, is worth more than one CV sent everywhere.

Is it OK to say I have no experience on my CV?

Yes — it is the truth, and employers hiring first-timers expect it. What matters is what you show instead: skills with proof, education, and reliability. Never invent a job or a skill to fill the gap; an invented line costs you the job when it is found out.
