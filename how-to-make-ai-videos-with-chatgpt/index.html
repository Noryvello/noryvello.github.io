How to Make AI Videos with ChatGPT: From Idea to Finished Clip

By the Noryvello team · Published 20 November 2026 · Updated 20 November 2026

ChatGPT cannot render video itself, but it can plan one: the idea, the script, the shot list, and the video prompts. A text-to-video tool then turns those prompts into moving clips, and you assemble the clips into a finished video. This guide walks through the whole workflow step by step, with a worked 15-second birthday clip at the end.

Making a video with AI sounds like one job, but it is really two jobs done by two different tools. The first job is thinking: what is the video about, who is it for, what happens in it, and what should each shot look like. The second job is rendering: turning a written description into moving pictures. ChatGPT is good at the first job and cannot do the second at all, which is why the workflow in this guide splits the work that way.

The split is what makes the whole thing practical. Instead of staring at a blank prompt box in a video generator and hoping for the best, you arrive with a plan: a short script, a list of shots, and a ready prompt for each shot. The video tool then does what it is best at, which is generating short clips from clear descriptions. You stay in charge of the idea, the order, and the final cut.

Buttons, menus, and features inside ChatGPT and video tools change over time, so the exact steps on your screen may look a little different from the ones described here. The in-app help or shortcut list is the authority, and you should check the tool's own site for current pricing.

The workflow in one paragraph

The workflow has six steps, and each one hands something concrete to the next. First you settle the idea and the audience. Then you ask ChatGPT to write a short script. Then you turn the script into a shot list, one line per shot. Then you ask ChatGPT to write a video prompt for each shot, with the camera, the motion, and the light spelled out. Then you generate the clips in a text-to-video tool, one prompt at a time. Finally you assemble the clips, add a voiceover and music, and watch the whole thing through before you share it.

Notice what ChatGPT never does in this chain: it never makes a picture move. If you ask it to create a video, it will tell you it cannot, and then it will offer to help you plan one instead. That offer is the whole method. You are using a writing tool for writing and a video tool for video, and each one stays inside what it is good at.

Step 1 — the idea and who the video is for

Every good short video starts with two sentences you could say out loud: what the video is, and who it is for. A birthday clip for a friend is a different video from a product clip for a shop, even if both are fifteen seconds long. The audience decides the tone, the pace, and what counts as a good ending, so settling it first saves you from rewriting the script later.

Ask ChatGPT to help you sharpen the idea before you write anything else. A useful first message names the topic, the audience, the length, and the feeling you want: for example, a cheerful fifteen-second birthday clip for a friend who loves baking, warm and funny rather than formal. ChatGPT can then suggest two or three angles — a joke opening, a surprise reveal, a simple wish — and you pick the one that feels right. Keep the idea small. One joke, one message, or one moment is plenty for a first video, and a small idea is much easier to turn into shots.

At this stage, also decide how long the finished video will be. Short is kinder to everyone: the generator, the viewer, and you. A fifteen-second video needs about three shots, which is a comfortable first project. A one-minute video needs ten or more, which is a bigger job and a harder one to keep consistent. Start short, finish it, and make the next one longer only when the short ones feel easy.

Step 2 — ask ChatGPT for a script

With the idea settled, ask ChatGPT to write the script. A script for a short AI video is not a screenplay: it is a short list of what happens, in order, with the words anyone says written out in full. Give ChatGPT the length, the audience, and the tone, and ask for the script in sections with timings, so you can see how the seconds add up.

A script for a fifteen-second birthday clip might look like this: the first five seconds show a kitchen table with a cake and candles, the next five show a hand lighting the last candle, and the last five show the candles being blown out while a short birthday message appears. That is three beats, each one a single clear action. If a beat needs two sentences to explain, it is probably two beats, and splitting it now makes the shot list easier.

Read the script out loud before you move on. Anything that sounds awkward when spoken will sound worse with a voiceover on top, and anything you cannot picture clearly will be hard to prompt later. Ask ChatGPT to rewrite any line that feels stiff or vague, and keep rewriting until every beat is something you can actually see. The script is the foundation of the whole video, so the time you spend here pays for itself at every later step.

Step 3 — turn the script into a shot list

A shot list turns the script into a plan for the generator. Each line of the list is one shot: what the camera sees, what moves, and how long the shot lasts. One script beat usually becomes one shot, so a fifteen-second video with three beats becomes a three-shot list. Ask ChatGPT to convert the script for you, one numbered line per shot, with the subject, the setting, the action, and the length of each shot.

The shot list is also where you decide the look of the video. Ask yourself three questions and write the answers at the top of the list: what style is it, what does the main subject look like, and what is the light like. A warm cartoon style with a round red teapot in golden evening light is a look you can repeat. A look you cannot describe in one line is a look the generator cannot repeat either, and your shots will drift apart.

Keep each shot to one action and one camera move. A shot where a cat walks across a room while the camera slowly follows it is one idea. A shot where a cat walks across a room, jumps on a table, knocks over a cup, and the camera spins around is four ideas fighting in one clip, and the generator will usually fail at most of them. If a shot feels crowded, split it into two shots and let the cut do the work.

Step 4 — ask ChatGPT to write video prompts

Now ask ChatGPT to turn each shot into a video prompt. A video prompt is different from a photo prompt because it has to describe movement and time, not just a frozen picture. The formula that works is: subject, then motion, then camera, then light, then mood, then length. Ask ChatGPT to follow that order for every shot, and to keep each prompt to two or three sentences.

Here is the shape of a good one, written for a birthday clip: a small round chocolate cake with five lit candles sits on a wooden kitchen table in warm evening light. The flames flicker gently and a hand reaches in from the side holding a match. The camera moves slowly closer, the mood is warm and cheerful, and the clip lasts five seconds. Every part of that prompt does a job: the subject and setting fix the picture, the motion words say what changes, the camera line says how the view moves, and the mood and length set the rest.

Two habits make these prompts much stronger. First, repeat the character and style words in every prompt, word for word. If the cake is a small round chocolate cake in shot one, it must be a small round chocolate cake in shot three, or the generator will invent a different cake. Second, ask ChatGPT to keep each prompt short. A long prompt full of extra detail gives the generator more to get wrong, and the most common failure in AI video is a prompt that asks for too much at once.

Step 5 — generate the clips in a text-to-video tool

With the prompts ready, open a text-to-video tool and generate the clips one at a time, starting with the first shot. Paste the prompt, choose the length the shot needs, and generate. Watch the result before you move on: check that the subject looks right, the motion is the motion you asked for, and nothing strange has appeared, like an extra hand or a face that melts halfway through.

Expect to regenerate. Most shots take two or three tries before one is worth keeping, and that is normal, not a sign that you are doing it wrong. When a clip fails, change one thing in the prompt before you try again: simplify the motion, shorten the sentence, or remove the detail that went wrong. Changing everything at once teaches you nothing, because you cannot tell which change fixed it.

Save each keeper as you go, and give the files clear names — shot one, shot two, shot three — so the assembly step is easy. If a shot refuses to work after several tries, simplify it instead of fighting it: a stiller shot with less motion often looks better than an ambitious shot that falls apart. A calm, clean clip always beats a wild, broken one, and the viewer will never know what you originally planned.

Step 6 — assemble the clips and add voiceover and music

Assembly is where the video becomes a video instead of a pile of clips. Put the shots in order, trim the start and end of each one so the joins feel clean, and watch the whole sequence through without sound first. The picture should tell the story on its own: if it does not, the problem is usually a missing shot or a shot in the wrong place, and it is cheaper to fix it now than after the sound is added.

Then add the voiceover, if your video has one. You can record your own voice, or use a text-to-speech voice from the words in your script. Keep the words short and spoken, not written: short sentences, plain words, and one idea per line. If the voiceover runs longer than the pictures, shorten the words rather than stretching the clips, because stretched clips look slow and tired.

Music comes last, and it should sit quietly under the voice, not fight it. Pick one simple track that matches the mood — warm and light for a birthday clip, calm and steady for an explainer — and keep it low enough that every word stays clear. Then watch the finished video twice: once for the story, once for the mistakes. Check the joins, the sound levels, and the ending, and fix anything that pulls you out of the moment before you share it.

A worked example: a 15-second birthday clip, prompt by prompt

Here is the whole workflow applied to one small project: a fifteen-second birthday clip for a friend who loves baking. The idea is simple — a cake, a candle, a wish — and the audience is one person, which keeps the tone warm and personal instead of formal.

The first ChatGPT message sets the job: write a fifteen-second birthday video script for a friend who loves baking, warm and funny, in three beats of five seconds each, with a short spoken line at the end. The script that comes back has three beats: a chocolate cake with candles on a kitchen table, a hand lighting the last candle, and the candles being blown out while the words happy birthday appear. The spoken line is short: happy birthday, and may your year be as sweet as this cake.

The shot list turns those beats into three shots, each five seconds long, all in the same warm cartoon style with the same small round chocolate cake. The video prompts, written by ChatGPT from the shot list, are: shot one, a small round chocolate cake with five lit candles on a wooden kitchen table in warm evening light, flames flickering gently, camera slowly moving closer, warm and cheerful, five seconds. Shot two, a hand reaches in from the side holding a match and lights the last candle, the flame catches and steadies, camera still, warm and cheerful, five seconds. Shot three, the candles are blown out, thin smoke curls upward, and the words happy birthday appear in soft letters, camera slowly pulling back, warm and cheerful, five seconds.

Each clip is generated separately, the keepers are named shot one, shot two, and shot three, and they are joined in order. The spoken line is recorded over the last five seconds, a light cheerful track plays quietly underneath, and the finished video is watched twice before it is sent. The whole project, from the first ChatGPT message to the finished clip, is one idea carried carefully through six small steps, and that is the entire method.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is a prompt that is too long. A video prompt with five sentences, three camera moves, and four actions asks the generator to juggle too much, and the usual result is a clip where half the instructions are ignored and the other half go wrong. Keep prompts to two or three sentences, one action, and one camera move, and let the cut between shots do the heavy lifting.

The second mistake is too many scene changes inside one clip. A single clip should stay in one place with one subject. If your prompt moves from a kitchen to a garden and back again, split it into separate shots. The generator is good at one continuous moment and bad at travel, and a clean cut between two good shots always looks better than one confused clip.

The third mistake is changing the character halfway through. If the cake is chocolate in shot one and vanilla in shot three, or the cat is grey in one shot and ginger in the next, the viewer notices at once, even if they cannot say what is wrong. Repeat the exact same describing words for every character and object in every prompt, and check each clip against the shot list before you keep it. Consistency is a habit, not a talent, and it is the habit that separates a finished video from a pile of pretty clips.

Questions people ask

Can ChatGPT make videos?

No, ChatGPT cannot render video. It can plan a video for you — the idea, the script, the shot list, and the video prompts — and a separate text-to-video tool turns those prompts into moving clips.

How do I get ChatGPT to write video prompts?

Give it the shot list and ask for one prompt per shot, in the order subject, motion, camera, light, mood, and length. Ask it to keep each prompt to two or three sentences, and to repeat the character and style words exactly in every prompt.

What do I ask ChatGPT first?

Start with the idea: the topic, the audience, the length, and the feeling you want. Ask it to suggest two or three angles, pick one, and only then ask for a script. A clear idea makes every later step easier.

How long should each clip be?

Short clips work best, usually about five seconds each. A fifteen-second video needs about three shots, which is a comfortable first project, and short clips are easier to keep consistent than long ones.

How do I keep the same character across clips?

Repeat the exact same describing words for the character in every prompt, word for word, and check each clip against your shot list before you keep it. If a detail changes in the prompt, the generator will change it in the picture.

Which video tool should I use after ChatGPT?

Any text-to-video tool that accepts written prompts will work with this workflow, because the prompts are plain descriptions, not tied to one tool. Pick one you are comfortable with, check its own site for current pricing, and generate one shot at a time.
