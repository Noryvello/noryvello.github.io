Law Firm Client Intake Guide

This guide explains what happens when a law firm takes on a new client, from the first call to the signed fee agreement. It is general information only, not legal advice, and no client data is collected or sent anywhere on this page.

What this guide covers and who needs it

Client intake is the name for what happens when a law firm takes on a new client. It starts with the first call or message and ends when the firm has the information it needs, has checked that it can act for you, and has agreed the terms in writing. Intake sounds like paperwork, and some of it is, but it is really about trust: the firm needs to know who you are and what you need, and you need to know who will handle your matter, what it will cost, and how you will be kept informed.

This guide is written for both sides of the desk. If you are a client, it explains what a firm will ask you, why it asks, and what to expect at each step, so the first meeting feels calm rather than confusing. If you run a small firm, it explains the steps of a tidy intake process and the checks that protect both the firm and the client. The steps are the same in both cases, because good intake serves everyone in the room.

Intake is also the point at which a firm decides whether it can take your case at all. A firm may decline because it already acts for the other side, because the matter is outside its work, or because it does not have the capacity to do the job well. A polite no at intake is far better than a bad yes. This guide is general information only, not legal advice, and no client data is collected or sent anywhere on this page.

How intake works, step by step

Intake usually begins with a short first contact: a call, a message or a form on the firm's website. The firm asks for your name, your contact details, and a short description of the matter, and it checks the most basic question — can we help with this kind of matter at all. If the answer is yes, the firm books a first meeting and tells you what to bring. If the answer is no, a good firm says so plainly and, where it can, points you toward the kind of help you need.

The second step is the conflict check. A firm cannot act for you if it already acts for the other side in the same matter, or if taking your case would clash with work it already does. The firm checks its records against the names you give, including the other side's name, and it does this before the first meeting goes deep into your facts. The check protects you as much as the firm: a lawyer with divided loyalties cannot serve either side properly. If a conflict is found, the firm will tell you and will not take the case.

The third step is the first meeting itself, followed by the fee agreement. In the meeting, the lawyer listens to your account, asks questions, looks at your documents, and explains your options. If you both decide to go ahead, the firm sends you a written agreement covering the scope of the work, the fee and how it is charged, how you will be kept informed, and how the work can be ended by either side. Read it carefully before you sign, and ask about anything you do not understand. The agreement is the foundation of the whole relationship.

The steps of client intake, in order:

  1. First contact: who you are, what the matter is, can the firm help
  2. Conflict check: the firm confirms it can act for you
  3. First meeting: your account, your documents, your options
  4. Fee agreement: scope, fee, updates, and how to end the work
  5. File opened: your papers filed, your dates listed, work begins

Costs, time and what you need before you start

Intake itself usually costs nothing beyond the first meeting, and the first meeting may be free, fixed-fee or hourly, depending on the firm and the place. What matters is that the cost is agreed in writing before the meeting, so there are no surprises. The wider cost — the fee for the work itself — is set out in the fee agreement, and it should explain how the fee is charged, whether it is fixed, hourly or based on the outcome, and what else you may have to pay, such as court fees or the cost of an expert. Ask for the full picture before you sign.

In time, intake takes a short call, a meeting of about an hour, and a day or two for the agreement to be prepared and signed. Before the meeting, gather the documents that matter: the contract, the letter, the notice, the messages, in date order, and write a short timeline of what happened. Bring identification, because the firm will need to confirm who you are, and bring a list of your questions. Preparation is what turns a first meeting from a slow fact-finding exercise into a useful conversation.

What to have ready before you start:

  • A short description of your matter, in one or two sentences
  • The key documents, in date order
  • A timeline of events and a list of your questions
  • Identification, and the other side's full name for the conflict check
  • The fee for the first meeting confirmed in writing

Worked example: a first meeting about a small business contract

Consider a small example. Priya runs a small catering business and has a dispute with a venue that cancelled her contract two weeks before a large event. She calls a law firm that handles small business matters. On the call she gives her name, the venue's name, and a one-sentence account of the problem. The firm checks for a conflict, finds none, and books a meeting for Thursday. The firm tells her the fee for the meeting in writing and asks her to bring the contract, the cancellation message and her timeline.

Before Thursday, Priya writes a timeline: the contract date, the payments made, the cancellation message, and the costs she has already paid for food and staff. She prints the contract and the messages, and she writes down five questions, including what the next step would cost. At the meeting, the lawyer listens, reads the contract, and explains Priya's options: negotiate a settlement, send a formal demand, or bring a claim. The lawyer explains what each would cost and how long it might take.

Priya decides to go ahead with a demand letter first. The firm sends her a fee agreement the next day, covering the scope of the work, a fixed fee for the letter, and how she will be kept informed. She reads it, asks one question about the fee, signs it, and the firm opens her file and starts work. From first call to signed agreement took four days, and every step was explained before it happened. That is what good intake feels like.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The first common mistake, on the client side, is arriving without the documents or the timeline. A first meeting spent hunting for facts is a meeting wasted, and it often means a second meeting to cover what the first one missed. The second mistake is not reading the fee agreement. The agreement is the document that governs the whole relationship, and signing it unread is how surprises happen. Read it, ask questions, and keep a copy.

The third mistake, on the firm side, is skipping the conflict check or doing it late. A conflict found after work has begun wastes everyone's time and can force the firm to stop acting. The fourth mistake is vague fee language: an agreement that does not say how the fee is charged, or what else the client may have to pay, is an argument waiting to happen. The fifth mistake is poor communication after intake. A client who hears nothing for weeks assumes nothing is happening. Agree at intake how often you will be updated, and keep to it.

A practical checklist you can follow

Use this checklist as a client preparing for intake, or as a small firm setting up its own process. It covers the steps in order, from first contact to the opened file.

  • First contact made: matter described, firm confirms it can help
  • Conflict check done before the meeting goes deep
  • First meeting booked, with fee and format confirmed in writing
  • Documents gathered in date order, timeline written, questions listed
  • Identification ready, other side's name given for the check
  • First meeting held: options explained, next step agreed
  • Fee agreement read, understood, signed, and a copy kept
  • File opened: papers filed, dates listed, update routine agreed

Tips from real practice

The best tip from real practice, for clients, is to treat intake as the start of a working relationship, not a formality. The way a firm handles intake tells you how it will handle your case: clear, prompt and honest at the start usually means clear, prompt and honest later. If the firm is vague about fees, slow to answer, or unwilling to explain the process, that is useful information, and you are free to go elsewhere before you sign anything.

For firms, the best tip is to make intake the same every time. A written intake checklist, a standard conflict check, and a plain-words fee agreement turn intake from a chore into a strength. Clients notice when a firm is organized, and an organized intake produces an organized file. The second tip is to confirm everything in writing: the meeting, the fee, the scope, the next step. Written confirmations prevent most of the misunderstandings that damage client relationships, and they take minutes to send.

Questions people ask

What is client intake at a law firm?

Client intake is the process of taking on a new client: the first contact, a conflict check, a first meeting, and a written fee agreement. It ends when the firm has the information it needs and both sides have agreed the terms in writing. Good intake protects the client and the firm alike.

What information will a law firm ask me for?

The firm will ask for your name and contact details, a description of the matter, the other side's name for the conflict check, and the key documents. It will also ask for identification. Bring the contract, the letter or the notice, and a short timeline of what happened, in date order.

What is a conflict check?

A conflict check is the firm's check that it can act for you without a clash. A firm cannot act for you if it already acts for the other side in the same matter. The check is done against the names you give, before the first meeting goes deep into your facts, and it protects you as much as the firm.

What should a fee agreement say?

It should say what work the firm will do, how the fee is charged, what else you may have to pay, how you will be kept informed, and how either side can end the work. Read it carefully before you sign, ask about anything you do not understand, and keep a copy.

What should I bring to a first meeting?

Bring the key documents in date order, a short written timeline, a list of your questions, and identification. Do not bring every paper you own — bring the ones that matter. If the firm asked for something specific, bring that first.

Can I change lawyers after intake?

Yes, you can change lawyers, though you may have to pay for work already done and the new firm will need time to read the file. Tell the first firm in writing, ask for your file and documents back, and make sure the new firm has the dates and deadlines. Changing early is easier than changing late.
