AI Glossary: 40 Terms Explained in Plain English

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

This glossary explains 40 AI terms in plain English, each in one or two short sentences. The terms are listed in alphabetical order, so you can look up any word you meet while using AI tools. Use the letter list below to find the term you need.

AI has grown its own vocabulary quickly, and the words can feel like a wall between you and the tools. Terms like token, hallucination, and fine-tuning appear in help pages and articles with little explanation, as if everyone already knows what they mean. This glossary removes that wall by giving each term a short, plain definition you can understand on the first read.

You do not need to memorise all 40 terms. Most people only need a handful to use AI tools well, and the rest become useful as you try more advanced features. Read the glossary from the top to build a general picture, or look up single terms whenever a word puzzles you.

AI words change meaning as the field moves fast. A term defined here in plain words may carry a narrower technical meaning in research, so treat these definitions as starting points, not textbook entries. If a term matters for an important decision, check how the tool or service you use defines it.

How to Use This Glossary

Each entry gives the term in bold, followed by a definition in one or two plain sentences. Where a term is often confused with another, the definition says how they differ. The terms run from A to Z, split into two parts so the page is easy to scroll.

If you are completely new to AI, start with the five terms in the section near the end of this page. They are the words you will meet most often, and understanding them first makes the rest of the glossary easier to read.

The Glossary: A to M

Algorithm. A set of step-by-step instructions for solving a problem or completing a task. A recipe is an everyday algorithm; an AI model follows algorithms to turn inputs into outputs.

Artificial Intelligence (AI). Software that performs tasks which usually need human intelligence, such as understanding language, recognising images, or making predictions. Most AI today is narrow, meaning it is good at one kind of task.

Bias. A systematic slant in an AI system, often picked up from its training data. Bias can make a model treat some groups unfairly, which is why checking outputs matters.

Chatbot. A program that holds a conversation in written or spoken words. Modern chatbots use large language models to answer questions, draft text, and help with everyday tasks.

Context Window. The amount of text an AI model can consider at once, measured in tokens. Once a conversation grows longer than the context window, the model stops seeing the earliest messages.

Corpus. A large collection of texts used to train or test a language model. A corpus can include books, articles, and web pages.

Data Set. A structured collection of examples used to train, test, or measure an AI model. The quality of the data set shapes the quality of the model.

Deep Learning. A branch of machine learning that uses neural networks with many layers. It powers image recognition, speech recognition, and modern language models.

Diffusion. The technique behind most AI image generators. A model starts from visual noise and gradually removes it, guided by a text prompt, until a clear image appears.

Embedding. A way of turning words or images into lists of numbers that capture meaning. Words with similar meanings end up with similar embeddings, which helps models compare them.

Fine-Tuning. Additional training that adapts a general model to a specific task or style. A model fine-tuned on medical notes, for example, writes better medical summaries.

Generative AI. AI that creates new content, such as text, images, music, or video, instead of only sorting or predicting. Chatbots and image generators are common examples.

Hallucination. A confident-sounding answer from an AI model that is false or made up. Hallucinations look like real facts, so important claims should always be checked.

Inference. The step where a trained model produces an answer from new input. Training builds the model; inference is the model at work.

Input. Whatever you give an AI model to work with, such as a question, a document, or a photo. Clear inputs usually lead to better outputs.

Large Language Model (LLM). An AI model trained on huge amounts of text, able to understand and generate human language. ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini are built on large language models.

Machine Learning. A way of building AI in which a system learns patterns from examples instead of following hand-written rules. The model improves as it sees more data.

Model. The trained program itself, the result of training on data. When people say an AI model, they mean the finished system that takes inputs and produces outputs.

Multimodal. Able to work with more than one kind of input, such as text and images together. A multimodal chatbot can describe a photo you upload and answer questions about it.

The Glossary: N to Z

Natural Language Processing (NLP). The field of AI concerned with understanding and generating human language. Translation, spam filtering, and voice assistants all rely on NLP.

Neural Network. A computing structure loosely inspired by the brain, made of layers of simple units that pass signals to each other. Neural networks learn by adjusting the strength of the connections between units.

Output. Whatever an AI model produces, such as an answer, an image, or a summary. Outputs should be reviewed before they are used, especially for important work.

Parameter. A number inside a model that the training process adjusts. Large models have billions of parameters, and their values encode what the model has learned.

Pattern. A regularity a model notices in its training data, such as words that often appear together. Models use patterns to predict what should come next.

Prompt. The instruction or question you give an AI system. A clear, specific prompt usually produces a better answer than a vague one.

Prompt Engineering. The practice of wording prompts carefully to get better results from an AI model. It includes giving context, examples, and clear limits.

Reinforcement Learning. A training method in which a model learns by trial and error, receiving rewards for good actions. It is often used after other training to shape a model's behaviour.

Supervised Learning. Training in which a model learns from labelled examples, such as photos tagged with what they show. The labels tell the model what the right answer looks like.

Temperature. A setting that controls how random a model's outputs are. A low temperature gives steady, predictable answers, while a high temperature gives more varied and creative ones.

Text-to-Image. Generating an image from a written description. You describe the picture you want, and the model creates it from the words.

Text-to-Speech. Turning written words into spoken audio. Modern text-to-speech voices sound close to human speech and can read articles, scripts, or stories aloud.

Token. A small piece of text, often part of a word, that a language model reads and writes. A rough rule is that one token is about four characters of English text.

Training. The process of teaching a model by showing it examples and adjusting it until its outputs improve. Training can take weeks of computing on large data sets.

Training Data. The examples a model learns from during training. What a model knows, and what it gets wrong, usually traces back to its training data.

Transformer. The neural network design behind modern language models, introduced in a 2017 research paper. It lets a model weigh the importance of different words in a sentence.

Unsupervised Learning. Training in which a model finds patterns in data without labels telling it the right answers. It is used to group similar items or spot unusual ones.

Vector. A list of numbers that represents a word, image, or other item inside a model. Vectors let models measure how similar two things are.

Voice Mode. A feature that lets you talk to a chatbot by speaking and hear its replies aloud. It is useful for hands-free questions and for practising a language.

Weights. The adjustable numbers inside a neural network that determine how strongly signals pass between units. Training is mostly the process of setting good weights.

Zero-Shot Prompting. Asking a model to do a task without giving any examples first. The model relies on what it learned during training to work out what you want.

Terms That Sound Alike

Some AI words are easy to mix up because they describe ideas that sit close together. Artificial intelligence is the broad field; machine learning is one way of building AI, and deep learning is one branch of machine learning that uses layered neural networks. Think of them as boxes inside boxes: deep learning sits inside machine learning, which sits inside artificial intelligence.

Prompt and prompt engineering are another close pair. A prompt is a single instruction you give an AI tool. Prompt engineering is the skill of writing prompts well, including giving context, examples, and limits. Training and fine-tuning differ in a similar way: training builds a model from scratch, while fine-tuning takes a finished model and adapts it to a specific job. Finally, a token is not a word: it is a smaller piece of text, often part of a word, and several tokens can make up one long word.

Five Terms Worth Learning First

If you learn only five terms from this page, make them these. Prompt is the word for your instruction, and clear prompts are the main skill in using AI well. Hallucination is the word for a confident but false answer, and knowing it reminds you to check important claims. Token explains why long conversations and long documents have limits. Training data explains why a model knows some things and gets others wrong. Model is the word for the trained system itself, the thing that turns your prompt into an answer. With these five words, most AI help pages start to make sense.

Questions people ask

What is the most important AI term to learn first?

Prompt, because it is the word for the instruction you give an AI tool. Once you understand that a prompt is simply your request, the rest of the vocabulary falls into place more easily.

What is the difference between AI and machine learning?

Artificial intelligence is the broad idea of software doing tasks that need human-like intelligence. Machine learning is one way of building AI, in which a system learns patterns from examples instead of following hand-written rules.

What does hallucination mean in AI?

It means an AI model giving a confident-sounding answer that is false or made up. Hallucinations look like real facts, so any important claim from an AI tool should be checked against a trusted source.

Do I need to know all 40 terms to use AI tools?

No. Most people only need a few terms, such as prompt, token, and hallucination, to use chatbots and image generators well. The rest become useful when you explore advanced features or read deeper guides.

Why do AI terms change so often?

The field moves quickly, and new techniques get new names as they appear. Some words also shift meaning as tools improve, which is why plain guides like this one are worth revisiting.
