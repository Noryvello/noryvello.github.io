What a proposal must do

A proposal has one job: to make the client feel confident that you understand their need and can deliver what they asked for. It is not a sales letter about how wonderful you are, and it is not a contract, though it can become the basis of one. The best proposals are short, specific and written in the client's own terms, so the client can see their own problem reflected back at them.

Every proposal needs four things: the client's need, restated so they know you heard them; the scope, so they know exactly what they are getting; the timeline, so they know when; and the terms, so there are no surprises later. Leave any one of these out and the client has to guess, and guessing is where misunderstandings start.

The four parts

Understanding the brief comes first, and it is the part that separates a good proposal from a generic one. Restate the client's need in their own words, and name the outcome they want, not just the task they asked for. If the client asked for a website, the need might be more enquiries; if they asked for a report, the need might be a decision. Show that you see the difference.

Scope lists each deliverable in plain words, so there is no argument later about what was included. Timeline gives milestones and dates, and says what you need from the client and when. Terms states how you charge, when payment is due, how many revisions are included, and who owns the finished work. Use the builder at the top of the page to draft all four from your own notes.

Writing the scope so it protects you

The scope protects both sides, but only if it is specific. A line that says you will deliver a report is not a scope; a line that says you will deliver a report of a stated length, covering named topics, in a named format, by a named date, is. The more specific the scope, the fewer arguments about whether something was included.

State what is not included as well. If the client might reasonably expect something extra — extra rounds of changes, extra formats, ongoing support — say so plainly, and say how it would be handled if they want it. Clients respect clarity, and it is far easier to agree these things before the work starts than after it.

Timelines clients trust

A timeline clients trust has milestones, not just a final date. Break the work into two or three stages, give each one a date, and say what the client will see at each stage. That way the client can follow progress, and you both know early if something is slipping.

Be honest about what you need from the client and when. If you need material, feedback or a decision by a certain date, say so in the proposal, and say what happens to the timeline if it arrives late. Most delays come from the client's side, and naming that in advance is fair to both of you.

Terms worth stating plainly

Terms are the part freelancers most often skip, and the part they most often regret skipping. State how you charge, whether by project, by day or by hour, and when payment is due. State how many rounds of revision are included, and what happens if the client wants more. State who owns the finished work and when ownership passes to the client. None of this needs legal language; plain words, agreed in writing, are what matter.

If anything in the terms feels uncertain, get the wording checked before you send the proposal. A short check now is far cheaper than a dispute later, and a client who sees clear terms will trust you more, not less.

After you send it

Sending the proposal is only half the job. Follow up once, a few days after sending, with a short message that asks whether the client has any questions. If you hear nothing, follow up once more, and then move on: chasing a client who is not replying rarely wins the work and always costs you time.

When a client says yes, confirm the scope, timeline and terms in writing before you start any work, and agree a start date and the first milestone. Use the follow-up checklist at the top of the page to keep track of each step, so nothing slips through the gap between yes and starting.

Questions people ask

How long should a freelance proposal be?

As long as it needs to be, and no longer. Most proposals fit on one or two pages: the client's need, the scope, the timeline and the terms. If the project is large, add detail to the scope, not to the introduction.

Should I include a price in my proposal?

State how you charge and what the price covers, so the client knows exactly what they are paying for. If the price depends on choices the client has not made yet, say so plainly and give the options rather than guessing.

What if the client asks for changes to the proposal?

Welcome them. A client asking for changes is a client who is engaged. Update the scope and timeline to match, confirm the new version in writing, and check that the terms still fit before you agree.

How soon should I follow up after sending a proposal?

A few days is about right for most clients. One short message asking whether they have any questions is enough. If you hear nothing, follow up once more after a similar gap, and then move on.

What if I hear nothing back at all?

Follow up twice, then let it go. Silence usually means the client chose someone else, put the project on hold, or lost the budget, and none of those is something a third message will fix. Keep the proposal as a template for the next one.
