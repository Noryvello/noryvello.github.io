Why Seasons Change, Explained

Seasons change because the Earth's axis is tilted, so each hemisphere leans towards the Sun for part of the year and away from it for the rest. This guide explains the tilt, the solstices and the equinoxes, and the picker shows how the seasons run in opposite directions in the two hemispheres.

The tilt that makes the seasons

The Earth's axis is tilted by about twenty-three and a half degrees, and it keeps pointing in the same direction as the Earth travels around the Sun. That means each hemisphere leans towards the Sun for half the year and away from it for the other half. When your hemisphere leans towards the Sun, the Sun rises higher in the sky, its rays strike more directly, and the days are longer. That is summer. When your hemisphere leans away, the Sun stays lower, its rays spread out more thinly, and the days are shorter. That is winter.

A common misunderstanding is that seasons are caused by the Earth being closer to the Sun in summer. They are not. The Earth's distance from the Sun changes only slightly through the year, and the change is far too small to make the seasons. In fact, the Earth is slightly closer to the Sun in early January, in the middle of the northern winter. The tilt, not the distance, is what makes the seasons.

Solstices: the longest and shortest days

A solstice is the point in the year when one hemisphere leans most towards the Sun. There are two solstices each year. The June solstice brings the longest day of the year to the northern hemisphere and the shortest day to the southern hemisphere. The December solstice does the opposite: the longest day in the south and the shortest day in the north.

At a solstice, the Sun reaches its highest or lowest noon position of the year. After the June solstice, the days in the northern hemisphere slowly shorten again, and after the December solstice they slowly lengthen. The solstices mark the turning points of the year, and many cultures have marked them with festivals for thousands of years.

Equinoxes: day and night in balance

An equinox is the point in the year when neither hemisphere leans towards the Sun. The Sun rises due east and sets due west, and day and night are roughly equal in length all over the world. There are two equinoxes each year, one in March and one in September.

The March equinox marks the start of spring in the northern hemisphere and the start of autumn in the southern hemisphere. The September equinox marks the start of autumn in the north and spring in the south. The word equinox comes from the idea of equal night, and it is the moment when the two hemispheres are on level terms.

Why the hemispheres have opposite seasons

Because the tilt points one hemisphere towards the Sun while the other leans away, the seasons run in opposite directions. When it is summer in the northern hemisphere, it is winter in the southern hemisphere, and when it is spring in the north, it is autumn in the south. The picker above shows this directly: pick a season in one hemisphere and then the same season in the other, and you will see the months are about six months apart.

This is why a traveller flying from Europe to Australia in December swaps winter for summer. It is also why the school year, the farming year and the holiday calendar differ between the hemispheres. The tilt is the same everywhere, but its effect depends on which side of the equator you are on.

What the tilt means near the equator and the poles

Near the equator, the Sun stays high all year and the tilt makes little difference, so the seasons are mild and the days stay close to twelve hours long. The year is often described in terms of wet and dry periods rather than four seasons.

Near the poles, the tilt has its strongest effect. In summer the Sun can stay above the horizon for weeks at a time, and in winter it can stay below it for just as long. Between these extremes, in the mid-latitudes where most people live, the four seasons are at their clearest, with the longest days in summer and the shortest in winter.

Seasons and everyday life

The seasons shape far more than the weather. Farming follows them, with planting in spring and harvest in autumn. The school year follows them, with long breaks in the summer. Clothing, food, sport and holidays all shift with the seasons, and many festivals are tied to the solstices and equinoxes, the turning points of the year.

Because the hemispheres have opposite seasons, the same date can mean very different things in different places. A December holiday means cold weather and short days in the north and warm weather and long days in the south. That difference is a direct result of the tilt, and it is a useful reminder that the seasons are not a property of the date, but of where you stand on a tilted planet.

Questions people ask

What causes the seasons?

The tilt of the Earth's axis. Each hemisphere leans towards the Sun for half the year and away from it for the other half, which changes how high the Sun rises and how long the days are.

Why is it summer in Australia when it is winter in Europe?

Because Australia is in the southern hemisphere and Europe is in the northern hemisphere. When the north leans towards the Sun, the south leans away, so the seasons run in opposite directions.

What is a solstice?

The point in the year when one hemisphere leans most towards the Sun. The June solstice brings the longest day to the north and the shortest to the south, and the December solstice does the opposite.

What is an equinox?

The point in the year when neither hemisphere leans towards the Sun, so day and night are roughly equal in length all over the world. There are two equinoxes each year, in March and September.

Are seasons caused by the Earth being closer to the Sun?

No. The Earth's distance from the Sun changes only slightly through the year, and it is slightly closer in early January, in the middle of the northern winter. The tilt is what makes the seasons.
