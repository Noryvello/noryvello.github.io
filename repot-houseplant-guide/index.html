How to Repot a Houseplant

Repotting gives a houseplant fresh soil and room to grow, and it is a simple job once you know the steps. This guide shows when to repot, how to choose the right pot size, and how to look after the plant afterwards.

This page is general plant-care guidance only. Some plants have specific needs, so check the care label or a plant guide for your species, and wash your hands after handling soil.

When does a plant need a bigger pot?

Most houseplants need repotting every one to two years, but the plant itself will tell you when it is time. Roots growing out of the drainage holes, soil that dries out within a day of watering, and a plant that looks top-heavy or stops growing are all signs the roots have run out of room. If you gently slide the plant out of its pot and see more roots than soil, it is time.

The best time to repot is when the plant is about to start growing again, in spring or early summer. The plant recovers faster when it is actively growing. Avoid repotting in the middle of winter, when growth has slowed, unless the plant is clearly struggling. A plant that has just been bought can usually wait — give it a few weeks to settle into your home first.

How to repot, step by step

  1. Water the plant a day before. Damp soil holds together and slides out of the pot more easily than dry soil.
  2. Prepare the new pot. Choose a pot one size up with drainage holes, and cover the holes with a small piece of broken pot or mesh so soil does not wash out.
  3. Add fresh potting mix. Put a layer of fresh mix in the bottom of the new pot, enough that the plant will sit at the same height as before.
  4. Remove the plant gently. Tip the pot sideways and ease the plant out, supporting the stem. Squeeze a plastic pot to loosen the root ball.
  5. Loosen the roots. Gently tease out roots that are circling the bottom, and trim any that look black or mushy.
  6. Set the plant in place. Centre the plant in the new pot at the same depth it was before, and fill around it with fresh mix.
  7. Firm the soil lightly. Press the mix down gently with your fingers, leaving a small gap below the rim for watering.
  8. Water thoroughly. Water until it runs from the drainage holes, then let the pot drain fully before putting it back.

Choosing the right pot

Go one size up, not three. A pot that is only a little bigger gives the roots room to grow without leaving a large amount of wet soil around them. Soil that stays wet for too long can rot the roots, which is the most common mistake when repotting. The pot-size picker above works out the right range from your current pot size.

Always choose a pot with drainage holes. A pot without drainage traps water at the bottom, and the roots sit in it. If you love a pot without holes, use it as a decorative cover and keep the plant in a plain pot with holes inside it. Tip out any water that collects in the cover after watering.

Aftercare: the first few weeks

Repotting is a shock to a plant, even when it is done well. Expect a few days of drooping while the roots settle. Keep the plant in bright, indirect light and out of harsh direct sun for a week or two, and do not feed it until it shows signs of new growth. Water when the top of the soil feels dry, and remember that a bigger pot dries more slowly than the old one.

Some leaves may yellow and drop in the first week. A leaf or two is normal; a plant that loses many leaves or wilts badly may need a closer look. Check that the soil is not waterlogged and that the plant is not sitting in direct sun or next to a heater. Most plants settle within a few weeks and reward you with fresh growth.

Common repotting mistakes

The most common mistake is a pot that is far too big. It feels generous, but the extra soil holds water the roots cannot reach, and the roots can rot. The second mistake is burying the plant deeper than it was before. Keep the soil level the same as it was in the old pot — burying the stem can cause it to rot.

The third mistake is repotting too often. A plant that has just been repotted does not need another move for a year or more. Let it fill its new pot before you think about the next one. Patience is part of good plant care.

Questions people ask

How do I know my plant needs repotting?

Roots growing out of the drainage holes, soil that dries out within a day of watering, and a plant that has stopped growing are the clearest signs. If you slide the plant out of its pot and see more roots than soil, it is time for a bigger pot.

How much bigger should the new pot be?

About 2 to 5 cm wider than the current pot — one size up. A much bigger pot holds too much wet soil around the roots, which can rot them. The pot-size picker above works out the right range for your plant.

When is the best time to repot a houseplant?

Spring or early summer, when the plant is about to start growing again, is the best time. The plant recovers faster while it is actively growing. Avoid repotting in the middle of winter unless the plant is clearly struggling.

Should I water a plant after repotting?

Yes. Water thoroughly after repotting, until water runs from the drainage holes, then let the pot drain fully. After that, water only when the top of the soil feels dry — a bigger pot dries more slowly than the old one.

Why is my plant drooping after repotting?

Some drooping in the first few days is normal while the roots settle. Keep the plant in bright, indirect light and water when the top of the soil feels dry. If many leaves yellow or the plant wilts badly, check that the soil is not waterlogged.
