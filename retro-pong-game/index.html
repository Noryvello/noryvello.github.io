A short history of Pong

Pong was released in November 1972, and it was the first video game to become a commercial success. It was built by a small American company called Atari, and the game itself was designed by an engineer named Allan Alcorn as a training exercise. The brief was simple: make a game with two paddles and a ball. Alcorn added the details that made it feel alive — the ball sped up during long rallies, and hitting it with the edge of the paddle sent it off at an angle.

The first Pong machine was installed in a bar in California, and within days the bar called Atari to say the machine had broken down. It had not broken down. The coin box was so full of quarters that the machine had jammed. That story has become part of gaming history, and it tells you everything about how new the idea was: people had never played a video game before, and they could not stop.

Pong was deliberately simple. Two paddles, one square ball, a score at the top, and a sound when the ball bounced. There was no story, no character, and no way to lose except by missing the ball. That simplicity was the point. Anyone could understand it in five seconds, and anyone could get better at it in five minutes. Every video game since — every shooter, racer, and puzzle — descends from that little square ball bouncing between two paddles.

How to play Retro Pong

Your paddle is on the right side of the table, and the computer controls the paddle on the left. The ball serves from the centre, and your job is to return it past the computer's paddle. Every time the ball passes the computer's paddle, you score a point; every time it passes yours, the computer scores. The first player to reach 7 points wins the match.

The ball speeds up a little with every paddle hit, so rallies get faster and more dangerous the longer they last. A rally that starts gently can end with the ball flying across the table at more than twice its starting speed. The rally counter at the top of the table shows how many returns the current rally has lasted, and your longest rally is saved on your device as your best.

Before you start, pick a difficulty on the Start screen: Easy, Normal, or Hard. The difficulty changes how fast the computer paddle moves, which is explained in the section below. You cannot change the difficulty in the middle of a match — finish the match first, then pick a new one on the Start screen. Each match lasts a few minutes, and a new match is one tap away when the last one ends.

Controls on phone and desktop

On a phone, the easiest way to play is to drag. Put your finger on the right side of the table and slide it up or down — the paddle follows your finger, sitting a little above it so your thumb never covers the ball. If you would rather use buttons, the big UP and DOWN buttons under the table move the paddle while you hold them. Both ways work, and you can switch between them mid-rally.

How the computer difficulty works

The three difficulty levels change one thing only: how fast the computer paddle can move. On Easy, the computer moves slowly — about three quarters of the ball's starting speed — and it waits until the ball is clearly above or below it before it starts moving. It misses often, especially on fast balls and sharp angles. Easy is the right choice for a first game, or for a relaxed match.

On Normal, the computer moves at roughly the ball's starting speed and reacts sooner. It returns most balls that come straight at it, but it can still be beaten with a well-placed angle or a fast rally, because the ball speeds up while the computer does not. Normal is the fairest match: the computer is beatable, but it punishes lazy play.

On Hard, the computer moves almost twice as fast as the ball's starting speed, reacts to tiny differences, and even aims slightly ahead of where the ball is going. It rarely misses a straight ball. The only way to beat it is with angles — hit the ball with the edge of your paddle so it flies to a corner the computer cannot reach in time. Hard is a genuine challenge, and winning a match on Hard is something to be proud of.

Scoring, rallies and the match

Scoring in Pong is as simple as the game itself. One point for every ball that passes your opponent's paddle. The first player to 7 points wins the match, and there is no need to win by two — 7 to 6 is a win. After each point there is a short pause, then the ball serves again from the centre toward the player who just lost the point.

The rally counter is the quiet star of the game. It counts every return in the current rally, and it resets when someone scores. A rally of 10 returns is a good rally; a rally of 20 is a great one. Your longest rally is saved on your device, along with the number of matches you have won. The score bar at the top of the table always shows your points, the computer's points, the current rally, and your best rally.

There is no time limit on a match. A quick match can be over in two minutes; a tight match on Hard can last five. If you need to stop, the Pause button freezes everything, and you can come back to the same match whenever you like.

Five tips that really work

1. Aim with the edge. Hitting the ball with the centre of your paddle sends it straight back. Hitting it with the top or bottom edge sends it at a sharp angle toward a corner. Angles win points; straight returns just keep the rally going.

2. Return to the middle. After every return, drift your paddle back toward the centre of the table. A paddle waiting in the middle can reach a ball going either way; a paddle stuck at the top cannot reach a ball at the bottom.

3. Watch the ball, not the score. The score bar is a distraction during a fast rally. Keep your eyes on the ball and on your paddle, and check the score only between points.

4. Slow the rally down when you are in trouble. If the ball is flying and you are barely keeping up, return it softly with the centre of the paddle. A soft return resets the pace and gives you a second to recover.

5. Use the walls. The ball bounces off the top and bottom walls at the same angle it hits them. A ball hit at an angle toward a wall will come back at an angle too — aim for the wall on your return and the computer has to guess where the ball will go.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is chasing the ball. New players move the paddle to wherever the ball is right now, instead of where it is going to be. The ball moves faster than the paddle, so chasing it means you are always a step behind. Instead, watch the ball's direction and move to where it will arrive.

The second mistake is standing still in the middle and hoping. A paddle that never moves returns only the balls that come straight at it, and the computer will quickly learn to aim at the corners. Keep your paddle moving, even a little, so you are always ready to react.

The third mistake is hitting every ball as hard as possible. In Pong there is no power button — the angle of the hit decides everything, and a wild edge hit can send the ball past your own paddle on the return. Aim first, then worry about speed. And the fourth mistake is forgetting that the ball speeds up. A rally that felt comfortable at the start is twice as fast after ten returns, so leave yourself room to react.

Why old games still feel good

Pong is more than fifty years old, and it still works, because it never tried to be more than it is. Two paddles, one ball, first to seven. The rules fit in a sentence, the skill takes weeks, and every point is decided by timing and judgement rather than by luck or by something you bought. There is nothing to unlock, nothing to wait for, and no advertisement between you and the next match.

Old games respect your time. A match of Pong takes a few minutes, and you can play one while waiting for a bus and stop without losing anything, because the only progress is your best rally and your match wins, and both are saved on your own device. The game asks for nothing and gives you a fair contest every time.
