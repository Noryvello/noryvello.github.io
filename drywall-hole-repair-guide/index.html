How to Repair a Hole in Drywall

Small holes need filler, bigger ones need a patch, and the method depends on the size of the hole. Pick your hole size below for the right steps, and use the materials checklist to gather everything first.

Before you start

Look at the hole properly before you touch it. How big is it, are the edges clean or ragged, and is the wall dry? A nail hole and a hole made by a door handle need different methods, and a damp wall needs the damp fixed first, not a patch. If the wall is damp, soft or smells musty, stop: the repair will fail until the cause of the damp is dealt with.

Clear the area and lay a drop cloth. Filler and sanding dust get everywhere, and a few minutes of protection saves a long clean-up. If you need to cut the wall at all, check first for pipes and cables behind it. Never cut into a wall blind, and if the hole is near a socket, a switch or a pipe, get advice before you start.

Patch, fill and sand

Whatever the size, the rhythm is the same: prepare the hole, bridge it if needed, fill in thin coats, let each coat dry, then sand smooth. Thin coats are the secret. One thick coat shrinks, cracks and takes far too long to dry, while two thin coats dry faster and finish flatter.

  1. Trim ragged edges with a utility knife so the hole has clean, firm edges.
  2. For a medium or large hole, fit a patch or a backing piece so the filler has something to hold on to.
  3. Press filler into the hole with a putty knife, then scrape it flat, feathering the edges out onto the wall.
  4. Let the filler dry fully, following the time on the product instructions.
  5. Apply a second thin coat if the first has shrunk or shows a dip, and let it dry.
  6. Sand gently with fine sandpaper until the repair is smooth and level with the wall.
  7. Wipe the dust away with a damp cloth, prime the repair, then paint.

Getting an invisible finish

The difference between a repair you can see and one you cannot is in the finishing. Feather the filler out well beyond the hole, so there is no hard edge where the filler stops and the wall starts. Sand with light pressure and check your work by running your hand over it and by looking along the wall in raking light, which shows every bump.

Priming is the step people skip, and it is the step that matters most for the finish. Filler soaks up paint differently from the wall around it, so an unprimed repair shows as a dull patch even after two coats of paint. One coat of primer over the repair, feathered out at the edges, makes the paint sit evenly and the repair disappear.

Mistakes to avoid

Do not fill a hole in one thick coat: it shrinks as it dries, cracks, and leaves a dip that needs doing again. Do not sand before the filler is fully dry, or you will pull it out of the hole. Do not skip the primer, and do not paint over dust: wipe the sanded repair with a damp cloth and let it dry before you prime.

Do not ignore what made the hole. A door handle that keeps hitting the wall will make the same hole again, so fit a door stop. A hole that keeps cracking may mean the wall is moving, or the patch underneath has failed, and it is worth finding out why before you fill it a third time.

When the damage is bigger than a hole

Some damage is beyond a patch. If the drywall is soft, crumbling or damp over a wide area, or if the hole is near wiring or plumbing, the job needs a professional. The same goes for ceilings, where a repair has to hold against gravity, and for walls that have been damaged by water. A patch is for holes; a damaged wall needs proper repair.

Questions people ask

How long does filler take to dry?

Follow the time on the product instructions, since it varies by product and by how thick the coat is. Thin coats dry faster than thick ones, and the filler should be fully dry and hard before you sand it or apply another coat.

Can I repair a hole without a patch?

Small holes, like nail and screw holes, need only filler. Medium and large holes need a patch or a backing piece, because filler on its own has nothing to hold on to and will crack or fall through.

Why does my repair show through the paint?

Filler absorbs paint differently from the wall around it, so an unprimed repair shows as a dull patch. Prime the repair first, feathering the primer out at the edges, and the paint will sit evenly over it.

What if the hole keeps cracking after I fill it?

The wall may be moving, the patch underneath may have failed, or the filler may have been applied too thickly. Open the repair up, check the patch, and refill in thin coats. If it cracks again, the cause may need a professional to look at.

Do I need to prime before painting a repair?

Yes. Primer seals the filler so the paint sits evenly, and without it the repair will show through as a dull patch even after two coats of paint. One coat of primer over the repair is enough.
