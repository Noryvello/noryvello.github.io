Upcoming Solar Eclipses

The next six total and annular solar eclipses run from 6 February 2027 to 25 November 2030, and the best known of them is the total eclipse of 2 August 2027, which crosses North Africa and the Middle East. This page lists all six eclipses with their dates, types, maximum durations and the countries their central paths cross, using NASA's eclipse predictions.

A solar eclipse happens when the Moon moves between the Earth and the Sun and blocks some or all of the sunlight. Total and annular eclipses are the rarest and most dramatic kind: during a total eclipse the Moon covers the Sun completely, and during an annular eclipse it covers the middle of the Sun and leaves a bright ring of fire around its edge. Because the Moon's shadow is narrow, each eclipse is total or annular only along a thin band across the Earth — the central path — while a much wider area sees it as a partial eclipse.

This page lists the next six total and annular solar eclipses, from February 2027 to November 2030. Every duration given here is the maximum value from NASA's eclipse predictions — the length of totality or annularity at the best point along the path, not the length you would see from every spot under it. Partial eclipses happen more often, but this list covers total and annular eclipses only, because those are the ones people travel to see.

The 2027 eclipses

2027 brings two of the six eclipses, and the second of them is the one eclipse-watchers talk about most. The year opens with an annular eclipse on 6 February 2027, Saros 131, with a maximum ring lasting 7 minutes 51 seconds. Its annular path crosses Chile and Argentina before running out over the Atlantic, and a partial eclipse is visible across South America, Antarctica, and western and southern Africa.

Six months later, on 2 August 2027, comes a total eclipse, Saros 136, with a maximum totality of 6 minutes 23 seconds. Its total path is a long one: it crosses Morocco, Spain, Algeria, Libya, Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Yemen and Somalia, so the eclipse is total from parts of southern Europe, North Africa and the Middle East. Anyone planning to watch it should read the guide to the 2 August 2027 eclipse on this site and check the countdown page for the date.

The 2028 eclipses

2028 also brings two eclipses. On 26 January 2028 an annular eclipse, Saros 141, produces a ring lasting up to 10 minutes 27 seconds — the longest maximum duration in this list. Its annular path crosses Ecuador, Peru, Brazil and Suriname in South America, then reaches Spain and Portugal in Europe. On 22 July 2028 a total eclipse, Saros 146, brings up to 5 minutes 10 seconds of totality to Australia — including Sydney — and to New Zealand's South Island, so the year's second eclipse belongs to the southern hemisphere.

The 2030 eclipses

After 2028 there is a gap of almost two years before the next total or annular eclipse. On 1 June 2030 an annular eclipse, Saros 128, gives a ring of up to 5 minutes 21 seconds along a path that crosses Algeria, Tunisia, Greece, Turkey, Russia, northern China and Japan. The sixth eclipse in this list falls on 25 November 2030: a total eclipse, Saros 133, with a maximum totality of 3 minutes 44 seconds, whose total path crosses Namibia, Botswana and South Africa before reaching southern Australia. Between them, the six eclipses of 2027–2030 touch every inhabited continent except North America — a reminder that the Moon's shadow falls where it falls, and that the best eclipse of the decade may be the one nearest to you.

The three types of solar eclipse

Total. During a total eclipse the Moon fully covers the Sun, so the Sun's bright disk is hidden completely for a few minutes and the sky darkens. Totality is visible only from inside the narrow path of totality; outside that path the same eclipse appears as a partial eclipse.

Annular. During an annular eclipse the Moon covers the middle of the Sun and leaves a bright ring of fire around its edge. The Sun is never fully covered, so the sky does not darken the way it does during totality, and the ring is visible only from inside the annular path.

Partial. During a partial eclipse only part of the Sun is covered. A partial eclipse is visible over a much wider area than a total or annular eclipse, and every total or annular eclipse is seen as a partial eclipse outside its central path.

How to watch an eclipse safely

Never look at the Sun without certified eclipse glasses or a proper solar filter. During a partial phase, and during the whole of an annular eclipse, the Sun's visible surface is never fully covered, so unprotected viewing can damage your eyes. The only time it is safe to look at the Sun without a filter is during the few minutes of totality, from inside the path of totality, and only while the Sun's bright surface is fully covered.

If you want to photograph an eclipse, the photography guide on this site explains filters and camera settings, and the eclipse guide explains the 2 August 2027 eclipse in detail. Read both before you travel: the right glasses cost little, and the wrong shortcut can cost your eyesight.

Questions people ask

When is the next solar eclipse?

The next total or annular solar eclipse is the annular eclipse of 6 February 2027, with a ring lasting up to 7 minutes 51 seconds over Chile, Argentina and the Atlantic. The next total eclipse follows on 2 August 2027, with up to 6 minutes 23 seconds of totality across Morocco, Spain, Algeria, Libya, Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Yemen and Somalia.

What is the difference between a total and an annular eclipse?

During a total eclipse the Moon fully covers the Sun, hiding its bright disk completely for a few minutes. During an annular eclipse the Moon covers only the middle of the Sun and leaves a bright ring of fire around its edge, so the Sun is never fully covered.

Where can I see the 2 August 2027 eclipse?

The total path of the 2 August 2027 eclipse crosses Morocco, Spain, Algeria, Libya, Egypt, Saudi Arabia, Yemen and Somalia. You need to be inside that path to see totality; outside it, the eclipse appears as a partial eclipse.

When is the next eclipse after 2028?

After the total eclipse of 22 July 2028, the next total or annular eclipse is the annular eclipse of 1 June 2030, with a ring of up to 5 minutes 21 seconds over Algeria, Tunisia, Greece, Turkey, Russia, northern China and Japan. A total eclipse follows on 25 November 2030.

Is it safe to look at a solar eclipse?

Only with certified eclipse glasses or a proper solar filter. Never look at the Sun without protection during a partial phase or an annular eclipse. The only unprotected view that is safe is during totality itself, from inside the path of totality, while the Sun's bright surface is fully covered.
