Space weather, explained simply

Space weather is the changing condition of the space around Earth, and it is driven by the Sun. The Sun is not a steady lamp: it breathes out a stream of charged particles called the solar wind, it throws off bursts of energy called solar flares, and every so often it hurls a huge cloud of charged gas into space. When those things reach Earth, they interact with our planet's magnetic field, and that interaction is space weather.

Earth weather and space weather are different things that happen in different places. Earth weather is the wind, rain and temperature in the air around us, driven by the Sun heating the ground and the oceans. Space weather happens far above the air we breathe, where there is almost no air at all, and it is driven by the Sun's magnetism. A stormy day on Earth says nothing about space weather, and a solar storm does not change the weather outside your window.

Space weather matters because modern life depends on technology that lives in space or depends on signals from space. Satellites carry our communications, our navigation and our weather pictures. Power grids span whole continents, and radio signals cross the oceans. All of them can be affected when the Sun is active, which is why space agencies and forecast centres watch the Sun all day, every day.

The Sun's moods: flares, wind and storms

The solar wind is always blowing. It is a stream of charged particles flowing out from the Sun in all directions, travelling at about 400 kilometres per second, and it takes roughly four days to reach Earth. The wind is not steady: it gusts and calms, and fast streams of wind can catch up with slow ones and pile up into shock waves that hit Earth like a wave breaking on a shore.

Solar flares are sudden bursts of energy from the Sun's surface, caused by the snapping and reconnecting of magnetic field lines. Flares are ranked by strength in classes called A, B, C, M and X, with X the strongest. A large flare sends out a burst of X-rays and ultraviolet light that reaches Earth in about eight minutes, travelling at the speed of light, and it can disturb the upper atmosphere and disrupt radio signals.

The biggest events are coronal mass ejections, or CMEs: huge clouds of charged gas thrown off the Sun, sometimes carrying billions of tonnes of material. A CME takes one to three days to reach Earth, which gives forecasters time to warn that a geomagnetic storm is on its way. When a strong CME hits Earth's magnetic field, it can shake the whole magnetic bubble around our planet, and that shaking is what causes the strongest effects and the brightest auroras.

How space weather is measured

Space weather is measured by spacecraft that watch the Sun and by instruments on the ground that watch Earth's magnetic field. Satellites parked between the Earth and the Sun measure the solar wind as it passes, giving about an hour of warning before it reaches us. Other satellites watch the Sun's surface for flares and CMEs, and telescopes on the ground track sunspots, the dark patches that mark where the Sun's magnetism is strongest.

The Sun goes through a cycle of about eleven years, from a quiet period with few sunspots to an active period with many. At solar maximum, flares and CMEs are more common, and space weather is busier. At solar minimum, the Sun is quieter, though the solar wind never stops. Forecasters watch the cycle, but they cannot predict exactly when a big flare will happen, any more than a weather forecaster can predict the exact minute a thunderstorm will start.

The strength of a geomagnetic storm is measured on a scale called the Kp index, which runs from 0 to 9. A Kp of 5 or more means a geomagnetic storm is under way, and the higher the number, the stronger the storm. The Kp index is the number you will see most often in space weather forecasts, and it is the one that tells you whether auroras might be visible further from the poles than usual.

What space weather affects

Satellites feel space weather first. A geomagnetic storm heats and puffs up the upper atmosphere, which drags low satellites down faster than expected and changes their orbits. Charged particles can damage satellite electronics, and a strong storm can confuse a satellite's sensors or, in rare cases, knock it out altogether. Satellite operators watch the forecasts and can put their spacecraft into a safe mode when a big storm is coming.

Power grids are affected in a different way. A geomagnetic storm makes the Earth's magnetic field change quickly, and a changing magnetic field drives electric currents through long conductors, including power lines. In March 1989, a strong storm knocked out power across Quebec in Canada for about nine hours, and it damaged transformers as far away as the United States. Grid operators now watch space weather forecasts and can take steps to protect their networks.

Radio and navigation are affected too. Solar flares can black out high-frequency radio for hours, which matters to aircraft flying over the oceans and the poles, and to ships at sea. The same disturbances can degrade GPS accuracy, because the signals from navigation satellites must pass through the disturbed upper atmosphere. Airlines sometimes reroute polar flights during big storms, both for radio and for the extra radiation at high altitudes.

Auroras: the beautiful side of space weather

Auroras are the most beautiful effect of space weather, and the one most people can see for themselves. When charged particles from the Sun reach Earth, our planet's magnetic field funnels them towards the north and south poles, where they collide with gases high in the atmosphere. Those collisions make the gases glow, painting curtains and arcs of light across the sky.

The colours tell you which gas is glowing. Green, the most common colour, comes from oxygen about a hundred kilometres up. Red also comes from oxygen, but higher up, and it appears during stronger storms. Purple and blue come from nitrogen. The shapes change constantly, rippling and folding like curtains in a wind, and a good display can last for hours.

Auroras are normally seen near the poles, in places like northern Scandinavia, Canada, Alaska and Antarctica, and in the southern ocean around Tasmania and New Zealand. During a strong geomagnetic storm, the aurora can spread far further towards the equator than usual, which is why a big storm can bring the lights to places that almost never see them. The best way to know whether auroras might be visible from where you live is to check the official forecast, which predicts how far the lights are likely to reach.

Checking the official forecast

The official space weather forecasts come from forecast centres that watch the Sun full time. The best known is the Space Weather Prediction Center run by NOAA, the American weather agency, which publishes forecasts, alerts and a Kp index prediction. ESA, the European Space Agency, runs its own space weather service, and several countries have national centres as well. These are the only sources that can tell you what space weather is doing right now.

A space weather forecast looks different from an Earth weather forecast. Instead of rain and temperature, it shows the solar wind speed, the Kp index, the chance of flares and the expected arrival time of any CME heading our way. A forecast that says a CME will arrive tomorrow is a prediction with real uncertainty in it, because CMEs can speed up or slow down on their journey, so check again closer to the time.

When you read a forecast, look for the Kp prediction first, because it tells you how strong any geomagnetic storm is expected to be. Then look for the aurora outlook, which tells you how far from the poles the lights might reach. And remember that a forecast is a forecast: the Sun can surprise the forecasters, which is why the official centres update their predictions several times a day.

Common mistakes about space weather

The most common mistake is confusing space weather with Earth weather. A solar storm does not bring rain, wind or cold, and a hurricane says nothing about the Sun. The two kinds of weather happen in different places, are measured in different ways, and are forecast by different people. A space weather forecast will never tell you whether to carry an umbrella.

The second mistake is thinking that one solar flare means disaster. Most flares are small and harmless, and Earth's magnetic field shields us from the particles they throw out. The flares that matter are the large ones aimed at Earth, and those are rare. Forecasters issue alerts for them, and the alerts are the right thing to watch, not the raw number of flares.

The third mistake is trusting a dramatic post on social media. Space weather attracts alarming headlines, from claims that a solar storm will end the internet to predictions of a blackout that never happens. The Carrington Event of 1859, the largest solar storm ever recorded, did disrupt telegraph systems and paint auroras near the equator, but it happened in a world without modern grids and satellites. A storm of that size today would cause real problems, which is exactly why forecasters watch the Sun, but the day-to-day reality of space weather is quieter than the headlines suggest.

Questions people ask

What is space weather?

Space weather is the changing condition of the space around Earth, driven by the Sun. The Sun breathes out a stream of charged particles called the solar wind, throws off bursts of energy called solar flares, and sometimes hurls huge clouds of charged gas into space. When those things reach Earth, they interact with our planet's magnetic field, and that interaction can affect satellites, power grids, radio signals and the auroras.

How is space weather measured?

Instruments on the ground measure changes in Earth's magnetic field. The strength of a geomagnetic storm is given on the Kp index, a scale from 0 to 9, where a Kp of 5 or more means a storm is under way.

What does space weather affect?

Space weather affects satellites, power grids, radio and navigation. Storms can drag low satellites down, damage their electronics, and disturb GPS signals. A strong storm can drive currents through power lines: in March 1989 a storm knocked out power across Quebec in Canada for about nine hours. Solar flares can also black out high-frequency radio for hours.

What causes auroras?

Auroras are caused by charged particles from the Sun reaching Earth and being funnelled by our planet's magnetic field towards the poles. There they collide with gases high in the atmosphere, which makes the gases glow. Green comes from oxygen, red also from oxygen higher up, and purple and blue from nitrogen. Strong storms push the auroras further from the poles than usual.

Where can I check the space weather forecast?

Check an official space weather forecast centre, such as the Space Weather Prediction Center run by NOAA, or the space weather service run by ESA. These centres watch the Sun full time and publish forecasts, alerts and Kp index predictions. They update several times a day, so check again close to the time if you are planning to watch for auroras.

Should I worry about solar flares?

No, not in everyday life. Most solar flares are small and harmless, and Earth's magnetic field shields us from their particles. The large flares aimed at Earth are rare, and forecasters issue alerts for them so that satellite operators, airlines and grid operators can prepare. The day-to-day reality of space weather is much quieter than the alarming headlines suggest.
