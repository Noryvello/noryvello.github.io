Frosting Quantity Calculator

Enter your cake size, the number of layers and how thick you want the frosting, and this calculator tells you how much frosting you need, in grams and in millilitres. It shows every step of the working, so you can see exactly how the answer was reached.

How the calculator works

The calculator works out the total area of cake that needs frosting, then multiplies that area by the thickness you choose. The frosted area has three parts: the top of the cake, the sides all the way around, and the filling between the layers. A two-layer cake has one layer of filling; a three-layer cake has two. Each part is worked out from the size you enter, and the steps below the result show each number so you can check the maths.

Two assumptions are built into the working, and both are shown in the steps. First, each baked layer is taken as about 5 cm tall, which is the usual height of a standard cake layer, and that height is used to work out the side area. Second, buttercream is treated as weighing about 1 gram per millilitre, which is close enough for planning. If your layers are much taller or shorter than 5 cm, or you are using a very light frosting, treat the result as a guide and make a little extra.

How to use the result

Make the frosting to the weight shown, or a little more if you plan to pipe swirls, roses or a thick border, because piping uses noticeably more frosting than a flat spread. If your recipe makes frosting by weight, use the gram figure directly. If it makes frosting by volume, use the millilitre figure. Either way, weigh or measure the finished frosting before you start, so you know whether you have enough before the cake is half covered.

Work in two coats for the neatest finish. Spread a thin crumb coat first, just enough to trap the loose crumbs, then chill the cake for about 20 to 30 minutes until the coat is firm. Then spread the final coat over it. The crumb coat uses only a small amount of the total, and it is already included in the calculator result, because the thickness you pick covers the whole finish.

Choosing a thickness

A thin coat of about 2 mm suits a cake where the sponge is the star and the frosting is a light finish, such as a simple sponge or a cake with fresh fruit on top. A medium coat of about 4 mm is the usual choice for a celebration cake: thick enough to look smooth and generous, thin enough that the frosting does not overpower the cake. A thick coat of about 6 mm suits a rich cake that is meant to be very frosted, or a cake that will be piped with deep swirls.

Thickness also affects how the cake eats. A very thick coat on a light sponge can feel heavy, while a thin coat on a dense fruit cake can look patchy. If you are unsure, choose the medium coat and adjust next time based on how this cake turns out. The calculator makes it easy to compare: run it once for each thickness and see how much the amount changes.

This calculator gives an estimate for planning, not an exact amount. Layer height, frosting density and decorating style all change how much you actually use, so make a little extra and keep the cake chilled if the frosting contains cream cheese or fresh cream.

Honest limits

The calculator covers the top, sides and layers of a plain round or square cake. It does not allow for drip decoration down the sides, piped borders, flowers, or a thick layer of filling that is much thicker than the outside coat. If you plan heavy decoration, add roughly a quarter more frosting than the calculator suggests. It also assumes the cake is a regular shape with straight sides; a shaped or sculpted cake needs to be judged by eye.

Different frostings also behave differently. Buttercream, ganache and cream cheese frosting all spread at about the same thickness, so the volume figure works for all of them. Whipped cream is much lighter and softer, so it needs a thicker layer to cover a cake well, and it is best added close to serving time. Whatever frosting you use, the working shown by the calculator gives you a solid starting point and a number you can adjust from experience.

Questions people ask

How does the calculator work out the amount?

It works out the area to be frosted, the top, the sides and the layers in between, then multiplies that area by the thickness you pick. The result is a volume, and the weight follows by treating buttercream as about 1 gram per millilitre.

Why does the calculator assume each layer is 5 cm tall?

A standard baked cake layer is about 5 cm tall, so the calculator uses that to work out the side area. If your layers are much taller or shorter, the result will be a little off, so make a small extra batch to be safe.

Should I make extra frosting?

Yes, a little extra is wise. Piping, swirls and a crumb coat all use more frosting than a flat spread, and it is much easier to have a little left over than to run out halfway through decorating.

Does this work for ganache or cream cheese frosting?

The volume is about the same for any spreadable frosting, because the amount depends on the cake area and the thickness. The weight is an estimate based on buttercream, so very light frostings may weigh a little less.

What is a crumb coat?

A crumb coat is a very thin first layer of frosting that traps loose crumbs so they do not show in the final coat. Chill the cake after the crumb coat, then spread the final layer over it.
