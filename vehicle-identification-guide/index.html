Vehicle Identification Guide

This guide teaches you to identify any vehicle step by step, from its badges and body shape to its VIN and model year. It shows you what to look for and what to check before you buy a used car.

This guide teaches identification only. It does not look up live registration records, and it cannot tell you a vehicle's history. For an official check, use your country's vehicle registry or transport authority.

What identifying a vehicle means

Identifying a vehicle means answering three questions: what is it, when was it made, and is it what the seller claims? The first two questions are answered by reading the car itself — its badges, its body shape, its plates and its VIN. The third question is answered by checking that all of those things agree with each other and with the documents.

You do not need special tools or training to do this. You need to know where to look and what the marks mean. A badge tells you the maker and the model. A body shape tells you the type. A VIN tells you the factory, the year and the car's unique number. A plate tells you where and roughly when the car was registered, in countries whose plate system works that way.

This guide walks through each of those marks in turn, with worked examples that use clearly labelled example plates and VINs. Nothing on this page is a real person's plate or a real vehicle on the road. When you finish, you will be able to stand in front of any car and read it like a label — and you will know exactly what to check before you hand over money for a used one.

Badges and model names: reading the outside of a car

Start with the badges. Every car carries the maker's badge on the front and the back — the familiar emblem of the brand. The model name is usually on the back, on the left or the right of the boot lid, and sometimes on the front wings. A car that says Corolla on the back and Toyota on the front is a Toyota Corolla, and the trim badge — letters like SE, GT or Sport — tells you which version of the model it is.

Badges can be removed, changed or added, so treat them as a first clue rather than proof. A car with a sports badge but ordinary wheels, ordinary brakes and an ordinary interior is probably a standard car with a badge added later. Check the badges against the rest of the car: the engine size, the wheels, the seats and the dashboard should all match the version the badge claims.

Also look at the small details that badges cannot fake easily. Headlight shape, grille design, door handles and mirror shape all change between model generations, and they are expensive to change. If the badge says one generation and the lights say another, something has been changed — and you should ask why before you go any further.

Body styles: hatchback, saloon, SUV and the rest

Body style is the shape of the car, and it is the fastest way to narrow down what you are looking at. A hatchback has a rear door that opens upwards and includes the rear window, with the luggage space and the cabin in one area. A saloon, also called a sedan in some countries, has a separate boot with its own lid, and the cabin and the boot are separate spaces.

An estate, also called a station wagon, is a saloon stretched out: the roof continues to the back, giving a long luggage area with an opening rear door. An SUV is a tall car with a high driving position, high ground clearance and a hatchback-style rear door. A coupe is a car with a sloping roofline and two doors, or four doors with a coupe-like roof. A convertible has a roof that folds away, and a pickup has an open load bed at the back.

Body style matters because it tells you what the car is for and what to expect. A hatchback is usually a small or medium family car. A saloon is usually a medium or large family car. An SUV is built for space and height, not for speed. When a seller describes a car, the body style should match the description — a car described as a saloon that is clearly a hatchback is a warning sign.

The VIN: what each part means

The VIN, or Vehicle Identification Number, is the car's fingerprint. It is 17 characters long — letters and numbers, never the letters I, O or Q, because they look too much like numbers. Every car made since 1981 has one, and no two cars share the same VIN. It is stamped into the car's body in several places, and it is printed on the registration document.

The 17 characters fall into six groups, each with its own meaning:

PositionsPartWhat it means
1–3World Manufacturer IdentifierWho made the car and in which country
4–8Vehicle Descriptor SectionModel, body type and engine type
9Check digitA maths check on the other characters
10Model yearThe model year, as a letter or number code
11Assembly plantWhich factory built the car
12–17Serial numberThe car's unique number in that production run

Here is a worked example. EXAMPLE ONLY — this is a textbook example VIN used in guides, not a real vehicle on the road: 1HGBH41JXMN109186. The first three characters, 1HG, mean a Honda passenger car built in the United States. Characters 4 to 8, BH41J, describe the model and body. The ninth character, X, is the check digit. The tenth character, M, is the model-year code for 1991. The eleventh character, N, is the assembly plant. The last six digits, 109186, are the serial number.

You do not need to memorise the codes for every maker. What matters is the structure: the first three characters tell you the maker and country, the tenth tells you the year, and the whole string must match everywhere it appears on the car and on the documents. If it does not match, stop and ask why.

Where to find the VIN

The VIN appears in several places on every car, and knowing where to look is half the skill. The easiest place is the dashboard on the driver's side, visible through the windscreen from outside the car. Stand at the front of the car and look through the bottom corner of the windscreen on the driver's side — a small plate with the VIN is usually there.

The second place is the driver's door jamb: open the driver's door and look at the edge of the door frame or the door itself, where a sticker or plate carries the VIN along with other information such as tyre pressures and the build date. The third place is the engine bay, where the VIN is stamped into the bodywork, often on the inner wing or the bulkhead.

The VIN is also printed on the registration document and usually on insurance papers. When you check a car, compare the VIN in at least two places on the car with the VIN on the documents. All of them must match exactly. A VIN that does not match is one of the clearest warning signs in car buying, and it is a reason to walk away until the seller can explain it.

Model-year clues and the year code

The tenth character of the VIN is the model-year code, and it follows a fixed cycle. Letters and numbers take turns: 2010 is A, 2011 is B, 2012 is C, 2013 is D, 2014 is E, 2015 is F, 2016 is G, 2017 is H, 2018 is J, 2019 is K, 2020 is L, 2021 is M, 2022 is N, 2023 is P, 2024 is R, 2025 is S, and 2026 is T. The letters I, O, Q, U and Z are never used as year codes.

The code repeats every thirty years, so A means 2010 and also 1980. In practice this is rarely confusing, because a 1980 car and a 2010 car look nothing alike. The year code tells you the model year, which is not always the same as the year the car was built or first registered — a car with a 2024 model year may have been built in late 2023, and that is normal.

Other clues confirm the year. Number plates follow a pattern in many countries that shows the year of registration: for example, a made-up example plate in a letter-based system might read ABC 123, while a made-up example in a year-based system might read XX00 XXX. These are invented examples to show the format, not real plates. Headlight shape, grille design and dashboard layout also change with each model generation, and a car whose styling matches its year code is a car whose story holds together.

Facelift or new model — and checking a used car before you buy

Makers update cars in two ways. A facelift keeps the same body shell but changes the lights, bumpers, grille and interior trim, usually halfway through a model's life. A new model is a fresh design: a new body shell, new proportions, and often a new platform underneath. Telling them apart matters, because a facelift car and a new-model car of the same badge can be very different cars to drive and to own.

To tell a facelift from a new model, look at the shape of the car, not the details. If the roofline, the door shape and the window line are the same as the earlier car, it is a facelift. If those have changed, it is a new model. Lights and bumpers are easy to change; the shape of the doors is not. The VIN's model-year code and the vehicle descriptor section will also confirm which generation the car belongs to.

Before you buy a used car, work through this checklist. First, read the VIN in two places on the car and compare it with the registration document — all must match. Second, check the badges against the car: the model, the trim and the engine should all agree. Third, look for signs of tampering around the VIN plates: scratched metal, mismatched screws, fresh paint over the VIN area, or a plate that looks newer than the car. Fourth, ask for the documents: the registration document, the service history, records of previous owners, and any inspection or roadworthiness certificate your country requires. Fifth, check the story: does the mileage fit the car's age and condition, and does the seller's account of the car's history match what the car shows? If anything does not add up, walk away. There is always another car, and a bad purchase is far more expensive than a wasted trip.

Questions people ask

What is a VIN?

A VIN, or Vehicle Identification Number, is a 17-character code that is unique to one vehicle, like a fingerprint. It tells you who made the car, where it was built, its model year, and its serial number. Every car made since 1981 has one.

Where do I find the VIN?

The easiest place is the dashboard on the driver's side, visible through the windscreen from outside. It is also on the driver's door jamb, stamped in the engine bay, and printed on the registration document. Check at least two places and compare them with the documents.

How do I tell the model year?

Read the tenth character of the VIN. It follows a fixed cycle: A is 2010, B is 2011, and so on through T for 2026, skipping the letters I, O, Q, U and Z. The code repeats every thirty years, so A also means 1980.

What is a facelift?

A facelift is a mid-life update to a car that keeps the same body shell but changes the lights, bumpers, grille and interior trim. A new model is a fresh design with a new body shell. Look at the roofline and door shape: if they are unchanged, it is a facelift.

How do I check a used car is what the seller claims?

Read the VIN in two places on the car and compare it with the registration document. Check the badges against the car's engine, wheels and interior. Look for tampering around the VIN plates. Ask for the registration document, service history and any inspection certificate, and check that the story fits the car.

Can I identify a car from a photo?

Often, yes, for the make, model and rough generation: badges, body shape, headlights and grille are all visible in a photo. But you cannot verify a car from a photo alone — the VIN, the documents and the physical condition need to be checked in person before you buy.
