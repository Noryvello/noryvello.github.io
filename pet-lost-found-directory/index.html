Pet Lost & Found Directory

When a pet goes missing, the first hour matters more than the first day — and the right first steps bring more pets home than any single poster or post. This guide covers the first-hour plan, where to post, what a good post includes, what to do if you find a pet, and the scams to avoid.

What this is

A pet lost and found directory is not a list of lost pets — no web page can keep one, and any page that tried would be out of date within the hour. What this guide is, instead, is the plan: what to do in the first hour when a pet goes missing, where to post, what a good post includes, what to do if you find a pet, and how to avoid the scams that target worried owners.

Most lost pets are found close to home, and most are found quickly — but only when the owner acts fast and acts in the right order. The first hour matters more than the first day, and a calm plan beats a panicked search. This guide gives you that plan, step by step.

This page lists no lost pets, no found pets and no finders, because invented listings would help nobody and could hurt real searches. Everything here is about what to do and where to look, so that when it matters most, you are not starting from nothing.

The first hour

Start inside. Check every room, cupboard, wardrobe and hiding place in your home — cats in particular can sleep through a house search and appear hours later, perfectly fine, in a drawer you were certain you checked. Check the garden, the shed, the garage and the car, including under and behind things.

Then search the streets nearby, on foot, calling calmly and often. A frightened pet may not come to a shout, but may come to a familiar voice speaking normally. Check under parked cars, behind bins, in neighbours' gardens — the small, sheltered places a scared animal chooses. Bring a lead, a carrier, and a favourite treat or toy.

While you search, get the word out. Post in local community groups and lost-pet groups for your area with a clear photo, and call the nearest shelters and vets so they know to look out for your pet. Knock on neighbours' doors too — a knock reaches people who never see your post, and a neighbour may have seen your pet an hour ago.

Do not wait a day to act. Every hour that passes widens the area your pet may have covered, and the first hour is when they are most likely still close. If your pet is microchipped, call the chip company to flag them as lost and to check that your contact details are up to date — a chip with an old phone number helps nobody.

Where to post

Post first in the places closest to home: local community groups, neighbourhood groups, and lost-pet groups for your town or area. These are read by the people most likely to see your pet — your neighbours, dog walkers, delivery drivers, the people who walk the same streets every day.

Then contact the organisations: nearby animal shelters, rescue centres and veterinary clinics. Give each one a clear description and a photo, and your phone number. Shelters take in found pets every day, and a pet matching your description may already be there — call, do not just post and hope.

Put up posters where people walk: near parks, schools, shops and bus stops, and on lamp posts along the routes dog walkers use. A poster needs a big photo, a few words, and a phone number readable from a distance. Rain will destroy a paper poster in a day, so cover it or use a plastic sleeve.

Widen the net gradually. If a day passes with no news, post in groups for the wider area, and keep re-posting: a post from Monday is invisible by Wednesday. And keep checking back with the shelters yourself — a pet can be taken in days after going missing, and the shelter may not connect it to your call.

Writing a post that works

A good lost-pet post has five things: a clear recent photo, your pet's name, a short description, where and when they went missing, and how to reach you. The photo matters most — a sharp picture of your pet's face, taken recently, in good light. A blurry photo from three years ago will not help anyone recognise them.

The description should cover breed or type, colour, size and any distinctive markings: 'small black and white cat, white paws, torn left ear' is a description someone can match. Add whether your pet is friendly or shy, and whether they wear a collar — and if they do, what colour it is.

Say exactly where and when they went missing: the street, the nearest landmark, the time. 'Near the park' is too vague; 'Willow Lane, by the school gate, around 4 in the afternoon' is a place someone can check. And give a phone number, not just a message — a finder with your pet in their arms will call, not wait for a reply.

One deliberate omission: leave out one small identifying detail — a mark, a habit, a particular spot — so you can check anyone who claims to have found your pet. Ask them to describe it. A genuine finder will know it; a scammer will not.

If you find a pet

If you find a pet, keep them safe first: a quiet room, water, and a lead or carrier if you have one. Approach calmly — a lost pet is frightened, and a frightened animal may snap. Do not chase; let them come to you, with food if that helps.

Check for a collar tag, and read it carefully — it may carry a phone number. Then take the pet to a vet or shelter to scan for a microchip. Scanning is free, takes a minute, and is the fastest way home: a chip with up-to-date details reunites pets and owners the same day.

Post in local groups with a photo and where you found them, and tell nearby shelters and vets. Ask anyone claiming the pet to describe the detail you left out of your post, and to show you a photo of the pet at home. A genuine owner will have photos; a false claimant will not.

If nobody claims the pet, the shelter or rescue centre can advise on next steps. Do not give the pet away to the first person who asks — a found pet belongs to someone, and the right ending is a reunion, not a rehoming. And if you cannot keep the pet safe overnight, the shelter can.

Scams to avoid

Losing a pet makes people vulnerable, and scammers know it. The most common scam is the demand for money before you see your pet: someone messages to say they found your pet, then asks for a reward, a transport fee, or a payment before they will meet you. Never pay anyone before you see your pet, in person, with your own eyes.

A second scam asks you to share a code sent to your phone. The scammer uses your number to set up an account somewhere, the code arrives on your phone, and they ask you to read it out 'to prove you are the owner'. Never share a code from your phone with anyone, for any reason.

Watch for callers who refuse to meet in person, refuse to send a photo, or cannot describe your pet beyond what was in your post. That is why you left a detail out of the post: ask them to describe it. A genuine finder can; a scammer guesses.

Meet in a public place, in daylight, and take someone with you. If anything feels wrong, it probably is — trust that feeling, and involve the police if someone is threatening you or demanding money. Your pet matters, and so does your safety.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is no photo in the post. A post without a photo is a post nobody can act on — nobody can recognise a pet from a description alone. Always lead with the clearest recent photo you have.

The second is waiting a day to act. The first hour is when your pet is closest and the trail is freshest. Posting tomorrow, searching tomorrow, calling the shelter tomorrow — each delay widens the search and lowers the odds.

The third is posting once and stopping. Social media moves fast: a post from Monday is buried by Wednesday. Re-post every day or two, refresh the posters, and keep calling the shelters. Pets have been found weeks and months after going missing, by owners who kept going.

The fourth is searching too far, too fast. Most lost pets are found within a short distance of home, often hiding. Search close first, and search thoroughly — under cars, behind bins, in garages and sheds — before widening the net.

The fifth is forgetting the microchip details. A chip only works if the phone number on it is yours and current. Check it now, before you need it — not in the hour your pet goes missing.

Never pay a 'finder' upfront — genuine finders return pets without payment, and anyone demanding money before you see your pet is almost certainly running a scam. This page lists no lost pets and no finders; it teaches you how to act, and where to post, when it matters most.

Questions people ask

What do I do first when my pet goes missing?

Search your home and garden first, then the streets nearby, calling calmly and checking sheds, garages and under parked cars. Then post in local groups and call nearby shelters and vets — all within the first hour, while your pet is most likely still close.

Where should I post?

Post in local community groups, lost-pet groups for your area, and with nearby shelters and vets, and put up posters where people walk dogs. Tell neighbours directly too — a knock on the door reaches people who never see your post.

What should the post include?

A clear recent photo, your pet's name, breed or description, colour and size, where and when they went missing, and how to reach you. Leave out one small identifying detail so you can check anyone who claims to have found them.

What if I find a pet?

Keep them safe, check for a collar tag, and take them to a vet or shelter to scan for a microchip — it is free and it is the fastest way home. Post in local groups with a photo, but ask anyone claiming the pet to describe the detail you left out.

How do I avoid scams?

Never pay anyone before you see your pet, never share codes sent to your phone, and be wary of anyone who refuses to meet in person or send a photo. A genuine finder wants the pet home, not your money.

How long should I keep looking?

Keep looking and re-posting for weeks, not days — pets have been found months after going missing. Refresh your posts, widen the search area gradually, and keep checking with shelters, because a pet can turn up far from home.
