Origami Learning Platform

Origami turns a single square of paper into animals, flowers and boxes, using nothing but folds. This guide gives you a learning path from your first easy folds to harder models, with every fold described step by step in words.

This guide uses words, not diagrams, so read each step slowly and fold along with it. Crease firmly, take your time, and start with the easy folds before moving on. There is no rush and no wrong first try.

How this learning path works

Origami looks difficult from the outside, but it is really a small set of folds repeated in different orders. This guide teaches those folds in the order you need them, in three levels. Level one is easy folds that teach you to crease, line up edges and follow steps. Level two adds the folds that make animals and flowers. Level three puts those skills together in harder models that take patience and precision.

You do not need talent to start origami. You need a square of paper, a flat surface and a little patience. Every folder starts with the same first folds, and every expert still folds them. The path below is designed so that each model teaches you something the next one needs, and nothing in level two assumes a fold you did not meet in level one.

Work through the levels in order, and fold each model more than once. The first fold teaches you the steps. The second fold teaches you the feel of the paper. The third fold is the one you will be proud of.

Paper: sizes and types

Origami paper is usually a square, and the standard size is 15 centimetres on each side. That size suits most models: big enough to fold comfortably, small enough to hold in one hand. Larger squares, 20 or 25 centimetres, are easier for beginners and for complex models, because the folds are bigger and more forgiving. Smaller squares, 10 centimetres or less, suit simple models and make neat decorations.

Start with ordinary origami paper, often called kami, in 15 centimetre squares. It is thin enough to fold many layers, crisp enough to hold a crease, and coloured on one side, which helps you see which face is which as you fold. Copy paper cut into a square is fine for practice, though it is thicker and resists very small folds.

To cut a square from a rectangular sheet, fold one corner of the paper across to the opposite edge, line it up neatly, and cut off the strip that sticks out. The folded part is a square. Check it by folding it in half both ways: if the edges meet, the square is true.

The basic folds every model uses

All origami is built from a few basic folds. Learn these five by name and by feel, and you will be able to follow almost any written instruction.

  • Valley fold: fold the paper towards you, so the crease sits at the bottom like a valley. Most folds in origami are valley folds.

Practise each fold on a spare square before you need it in a model. Fold slowly, line up the edges before you press, and run your fingernail along the crease to make it sharp. A sharp crease is the difference between a model that holds its shape and one that slowly opens up.

Level 1: your first folds

These three models are the traditional first folds. Each one takes a few minutes, and each one teaches a skill the next level needs.

The paper hat. Take a rectangular sheet, such as a newspaper page, and fold it in half so the folded edge is at the top. Fold the two top corners down to the middle, so they meet at the centre and form a triangle with two flaps below. Fold the bottom flap on the front up over the triangle, then turn the paper over and fold the other flap up. Open the hat and wear it. This fold teaches you to line up corners and to fold flaps neatly.

The paper boat. Start with the paper hat you just made. Open the hat and flatten it into a diamond shape, tucking the two side points together. Fold the bottom corner of the diamond up on the front, then turn it over and fold the other bottom corner up. You have a triangle again. Open it and flatten it into a diamond a second time. Now hold the two side points and pull them gently apart, and the boat opens up. Press the bottom flat and you have a boat that floats.

The paper cup. Take a square and fold it in half diagonally to make a triangle, with the long edge at the bottom. Fold the left corner across to a point about two-thirds along the right edge, then fold the right corner across to the left edge in the same way. Fold the top flaps down on both sides, open the cup, and press the bottom flat. This fold teaches you to judge a fold by eye instead of by the centre line.

Level 2: animals and flowers

Level two models use the folds from level one plus the reverse fold and the petal fold. Take your time, and do not worry if the first try looks rough.

The jumping frog. Take a rectangle, twice as long as it is wide. Fold the top down in a triangle both ways to make a crease pattern, then collapse the top into a triangle by pushing the sides in. This is the same move as the boat. Fold the two bottom corners of the triangle up to the top point to make a diamond. Fold the sides of the diamond in to the centre line. Fold the bottom edge up to the middle, then fold it back on itself to make the spring. Turn the frog over, press the spring down with a finger and let go, and it jumps.

The tulip. Take a square and fold it in half diagonally both ways, then unfold it, so the creases cross in the middle. Fold the square in half one way, then the other, and unfold again. Now push the sides in along the creases so the paper collapses into a smaller triangle with four layers. Fold the bottom corners of the triangle up to the top point on both sides to make a diamond. Fold the left and right edges to the centre, tuck one edge inside the other to lock it, blow gently into the small hole at the bottom to inflate the flower, and fold the four top points outwards to open the petals.

The crane. The crane is the most famous origami model, and it is easier than it looks once you know the steps. Start with a square, coloured side down. Fold it diagonally both ways and unfold. Turn it over, fold it in half both ways and unfold. Push the corners together along the creases so the paper collapses into a small square with four layers. This is the square base. Fold the lower edges of the diamond to the centre line on both sides, fold the top down, and unfold. Lift the bottom point up and flatten it, folding the sides in as you go: this is the petal fold, and it makes a long diamond. Repeat on the other side. You now have a long, narrow diamond. Fold the lower edges to the centre again on both sides to make it narrower, then fold the whole shape in half lengthwise. Reverse fold one end to make the head and neck, reverse fold the other end to make the tail, and pull the wings out to the sides. The crane is finished.

Level 3: harder models

Level three models ask for patience and precision, and they reward both. They use the same folds you already know, but they use more of them, in smaller spaces, and they punish a sloppy crease.

The dragon is a step up from the crane. It starts with the same bird base, then adds reverse folds for the horns, the legs and the tail, and a series of small folds along the back to suggest scales. Use a large square, at least 20 centimetres, and take your time with each fold. A dragon takes half an hour or more, and the result is worth it.

The modular cube is a different kind of challenge: you fold six identical units, then slot them together without glue to make a cube. Each unit is simple, but the assembly needs care, because the units hold each other in place by friction and by the pockets you fold into them. Modular origami teaches precision, because a unit that is even slightly off will not fit.

At this level, paper choice matters. Thin, crisp paper folds more layers without tearing, and a larger sheet gives your fingers room to work. If a model frustrates you, put it down and come back tomorrow. Origami is patient, and so should you be.

Reading origami instructions anywhere

Origami instructions use a small set of symbols, and once you know them you can follow diagrams in any book or on any page, in any language.

  • A dashed line means a valley fold: fold towards you along the line.

Written instructions use the same ideas in words: fold, unfold, turn over, reverse, squash and petal. If a step confuses you, read the next step as well, because it often shows what the fold is for. And remember that every folder reads a new set of instructions slowly the first time.

Practice tips: how to improve

Origami improves with practice, and the practice is simple. Fold the same model several times in a row. The first fold teaches you the steps, the second teaches you the paper, and the third is the one you keep.

  • Fold on a hard, flat surface, not on your lap. A table gives you a firm crease.

A daily habit of ten minutes beats a long session once a month. Keep a pack of paper by your chair, and fold a crane or a frog while you listen to something. The hands learn faster than the head, and soon the folds will come without thinking.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

Every folder makes the same mistakes at first. Here are the common ones and what to do about them.

  • The model will not stay closed: the creases are too soft. Refold each crease firmly, and use thinner paper.

There is no failed origami, only practice. A crane that leans is still a crane, and the next one will stand straighter. Fold, unfold, and fold again.

Questions people ask

What paper should I use for origami?

Start with ordinary origami paper, often called kami, in 15 centimetre squares. It is thin enough to fold many layers, crisp enough to hold a crease, and coloured on one side so you can see which face is which. Copy paper cut into a square is fine for practice, and larger squares of 20 centimetres or more make complex models easier.

Is origami hard for beginners?

No. Origami is a small set of folds repeated in different orders, and the first models take only a few minutes. A paper hat, a boat and a cup teach the basic folds, and every harder model builds on them. Start with the easy folds, fold each model more than once, and the harder ones will follow naturally.

What is the easiest origami model to start with?

The paper hat is the easiest first model: fold a rectangle in half, fold the two top corners to the middle, and fold the bottom flaps up. The paper boat and the paper cup are the natural next steps, and each one takes only a few minutes. All three use the same basic folds.

Do I need scissors or glue for origami?

Traditional origami uses no scissors and no glue: one square of paper and folding alone. Some modern models use a cut or a drop of glue, but the models in this guide need neither. You may need scissors once, to cut a square from a rectangular sheet of copy paper, and that is all.

How long does it take to fold a paper crane?

A first crane takes about fifteen to twenty minutes, because you are learning the steps as you go. Once you know the steps, a crane takes about five minutes. The crane uses the square base and the petal fold, which are both taught in this guide, so fold those first and the crane will follow.

Can children learn origami?

Yes. Children from about five or six years old can fold the paper hat, the boat and the cup, and older children can fold the frog and the tulip. Origami is good for fine motor skills and for patience, and it needs nothing more than paper and a table. Start with large paper, because the folds are easier for small hands.
