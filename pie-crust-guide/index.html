Pie Crust Guide

Pick your filling and crust style and this guide gives you the right crust, its ingredient ratios by weight, numbered method steps and blind-baking guidance. It covers fruit, custard, savoury and cream fillings, so the base comes out crisp instead of soggy.

How to use this guide

Choose your filling first, then the crust style you want, and press the button. You will get a crust recommendation for that combination, the ingredient amounts for a single 23 cm crust, numbered method steps, and a note on whether to blind bake. The amounts use the classic ratio of 3 parts flour to 2 parts fat to 1 part water by weight, adjusted for the crust style you pick. Weigh the ingredients rather than measuring by volume, because a cup of flour can vary a lot depending on how it is scooped.

If your pie needs a top crust as well, double the amounts. If your dish is larger or smaller than 23 cm, scale the amounts up or down in the same ratio and keep the 3-2-1 balance. The method stays the same whatever the size: keep everything cold, handle the dough as little as possible, and chill it before rolling and again before baking.

Choosing a crust for your filling

The filling decides the crust more than anything else. A fruit filling releases juice as it bakes, so it needs a sturdy shortcrust that can take a long bake without going soft, and it benefits from a lattice or a vented top that lets steam escape. A custard filling is wet from the start and bakes at a gentle temperature, so the base must be blind baked first or it will never set properly. A savoury filling is usually rich and heavy, so a plain shortcrust or a wholemeal crust holds up best and adds flavour of its own. A cream filling is added after baking, so the case must be fully baked and crisp before the filling goes anywhere near it.

Sweet crust is the right choice when the filling is delicate and sweet, such as a lemon tart or a berry tart with a light filling. It has sugar in the dough, which makes it crisper and more biscuit-like, but also more fragile, so it needs gentle handling and a good chill. Wholemeal crust brings a nutty flavour that suits savoury pies and rustic fruit pies, though it is a little less tender than a white-flour crust. If you are unsure, classic shortcrust is the safest all-round choice, because it works with almost every filling.

Keeping the pastry cold

Cold ingredients are the single most important part of good pastry. When cold butter is rubbed into flour, it stays in small pieces. In the oven those pieces melt and make steam, and the steam lifts the pastry into light, flaky layers. If the butter melts into the flour before baking, the pastry turns out dense and greasy instead of flaky. That is why the butter should go back in the fridge until the moment you use it, why the water should be ice cold, and why the dough should be chilled before rolling.

Your hands are warm, so handle the dough as little as possible. Rub the butter in with your fingertips, not your palms, and stop as soon as the mixture looks like coarse breadcrumbs with a few pea-sized pieces of butter still visible. Bring the dough together with a knife or a spoon before you touch it with your hands, and add the water a spoonful at a time. The dough should just hold together when pressed; it should not feel wet or sticky.

Rolling and lining the dish

Roll the chilled dough on a lightly floured surface, turning it a quarter turn after every few rolls so it stays even and does not stick. Roll from the centre outwards rather than back and forth, and aim for a thickness of about 3 mm, roughly the thickness of a coin. If the dough cracks at the edges, press the cracks together with your fingers and keep rolling. Small cracks are normal and easy to patch.

To line the dish, roll the pastry loosely around the rolling pin and unroll it over the dish, then ease it into the corners with your fingers. Do not stretch the pastry to make it fit, because stretched pastry shrinks back in the oven. Leave the extra pastry hanging over the edge until after baking, or trim it with a knife and crimp the edge with a fork or your fingers. Then chill the lined dish for at least 30 minutes before baking, because cold pastry holds its shape far better than warm pastry.

This page is general cooking guidance, not food-safety advice. Take care with hot baking trays and oven shelves, and keep raw pastry away from ready-to-eat food. If a filling contains raw egg or meat, make sure the pie is cooked through before serving.

Blind baking, step by step

Blind baking means baking the empty case before the filling goes in, and it is the best defence against a soggy bottom. Line the chilled case with baking paper, fill it with baking beans, dried rice or dried beans right up to the rim, and bake in a preheated oven at about 190 to 200 degrees Celsius until the edges are set and lightly coloured. Then lift out the paper and weights, prick the base lightly with a fork, and bake for a few more minutes until the base looks dry and lightly golden.

For a wet filling, blind bake the case until it is almost fully cooked, and brush the hot base with a little beaten egg as soon as it comes out of the oven. The egg sets on the hot pastry and forms a thin seal that keeps the filling from soaking in. For a cream filling, blind bake the case until it is fully golden and crisp, then let it cool completely before filling. A warm case will melt a cream filling and soften within minutes.

Fixing common crust problems

If the pastry tears while you roll it, patch it with a scrap of dough pressed over the tear and keep going; small repairs disappear in the oven. If the crust shrinks away from the dish, the pastry was probably stretched when it was fitted, or it was not chilled long enough. If the base is soggy, blind bake longer next time and bake the pie on a preheated tray on the lowest shelf. If the top browns before the filling is done, cover the pie loosely with foil for the rest of the bake.

A tough crust usually means the dough was overworked or too much water was added. Next time, handle the dough less, add water a spoonful at a time, and stop as soon as the dough holds together. A crumbly crust that falls apart usually means too little water or fat that was too warm. Pastry improves quickly with practice, and even an imperfect crust usually tastes good, so keep baking and adjust one thing at a time.

Questions people ask

What is the classic ratio for pie crust?

The classic shortcrust ratio is 3 parts flour to 2 parts fat to 1 part water, all by weight. For a single 23 cm crust that works out to about 150 g flour, 100 g cold butter and 50 g cold water, plus a pinch of salt.

Do I always need to blind bake a pie crust?

No. Blind baking is needed when the filling is wet, or when the filling is added after baking, or when the filling bakes for a shorter time than the crust needs. For a fruit pie with a long bake, the crust usually cooks through with the filling.

Why did my pie crust shrink?

Shrinking usually means the pastry was stretched when it was fitted into the dish, or it was not chilled long enough before baking. Rest the lined dish in the fridge for at least 30 minutes and let the pastry settle into the corners instead of pulling it.

Can I make pie crust without butter?

Yes. Lard, shortening or a firm vegetable fat can replace butter in the same ratio, and each gives a slightly different flavour and texture. Keep whichever fat you use cold, and handle the dough as little as possible.

How do I stop a soggy bottom?

Blind bake the base when the filling is wet, bake on a preheated tray or the lowest oven shelf, and avoid filling the case until the last moment. A thin brush of beaten egg over the hot base after blind baking also helps seal it.
