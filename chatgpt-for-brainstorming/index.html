Brainstorming with ChatGPT: Methods That Work

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

ChatGPT is a strong brainstorming partner because it can produce many ideas fast, take on a role, and push back on your assumptions. The trick is to give it a clear method instead of asking for ideas with no direction. This guide explains five methods that work, each with a worked example you can copy and adapt.

Most people brainstorm with ChatGPT by typing something vague, like asking for ideas for a party or a business name, and then feeling disappointed when the answers feel generic. The problem is rarely the tool. It is the prompt. A chatbot answers the question you actually asked, and a vague question gets a vague list. A method fixes that. It gives the conversation a shape: a goal, a limit, and a way to judge what comes back.

The five methods below all follow the same basic rhythm. First, tell ChatGPT what you are trying to do and who the ideas are for. Then ask for a set number of ideas. Then react: pick the ones you like, say why, and ask for more in that direction. Every worked example in this guide uses the same running task, planning a weekend launch event for a small neighbourhood bakery, so you can compare how each method changes the answers.

ChatGPT's ideas are starting points, not finished plans. It can repeat common ideas, invent facts about markets or rules, and miss local details it does not know. Check anything factual before you act on it, and keep private business details out of your prompts.

Start with a list dump

The list dump is the simplest method and the best place to begin. You ask for a large number of ideas on one topic, with no filtering at all. The point is quantity, not quality. When you ask for twenty ideas, the first five will be obvious, the next five will be decent, and the last few are where the surprising ones usually hide. That is the whole value of the method: it pushes past the easy answers.

Give the prompt a little context so the list is not random. Say who the ideas are for, what the occasion is, and how many you want. A worked example: "I run a small neighbourhood bakery and I am planning a weekend launch event for a new pastry stall. Give me twenty ideas for activities and attractions that would bring in families with young children. Keep each idea to one sentence." The reply might include a decorate-your-own-cookie table, a story corner, a mini baking contest, and a tasting trail. From there, you pick the three that fit your space and budget, and ask for detail on those.

Try SCAMPER-style prompts

SCAMPER is a classic creative technique that asks you to change an existing idea in seven ways: substitute, combine, adapt, modify, put to another use, eliminate, and reverse. With ChatGPT, you do not need to learn the full framework. You just ask it to take one idea and push it through those changes, one at a time, and see which version is better than the original.

Worked example: start with the bakery's plain idea, a tasting table with free samples. Ask: "Take the idea of a free tasting table at my bakery launch and apply SCAMPER to it. For each of the seven moves, give me one changed version of the idea." You might get a tasting table combined with a vote for the next seasonal pastry, a tasting table adapted for children with tiny portions, or the same table with the free samples eliminated and replaced by a small tasting ticket that includes a drink. Each change is a new idea, and you only keep the ones that survive.

Brainstorm in a role

Role-play brainstorming means asking ChatGPT to answer as a particular kind of person. A children's party planner thinks differently from a food critic, and both think differently from a tight-fisted shop owner. When you give the chatbot a role, it draws on the habits and priorities of that role, and the ideas change shape. This is the fastest way to escape a list that all sounds the same.

Worked example: "Pretend you are a children's party planner with ten years of experience. Suggest ten activities for a bakery launch event that will keep children aged four to eight happy for two hours while their parents shop." Then run the same question again with a different role: "Now pretend you are a local food blogger. Suggest ten ideas that would make the same event worth writing about." Compare the two lists. The party planner's ideas will lean toward games and mess, while the blogger's will lean toward photo-worthy moments. The best event usually mixes both.

Add constraints to sharpen ideas

It sounds backwards, but limits make brainstorming better. An open question invites the most common answers. A tight constraint forces the chatbot away from the obvious. Tell it the budget is tiny, the space is small, the weather might be bad, or the audience is hard to please, and watch the ideas get more specific and more useful.

Worked example: "I have a tiny budget and only a pavement table outside my bakery for the launch event. Suggest ten ideas that cost almost nothing, need no electricity, and can be set up by one person in under an hour." A constrained prompt like this produces ideas a general prompt would never reach: a chalk-drawn hopscotch path leading to the door, a guess-the-ingredient jar, a paper crown-making station using old baking paper, or a loyalty card stamped at each visit. Constraints turn a generic list into a plan you can actually carry out.

Try reverse brainstorming

Reverse brainstorming starts from the opposite end. Instead of asking how to succeed, ask how to fail spectacularly, then flip each failure into a fix. It works because people find it easier to list what could go wrong than to list what could go right, and the flipped list often contains ideas nobody would have thought of directly.

Worked example: "List ten ways to make a bakery launch event a complete disaster." The answers might include running out of food in the first hour, having no seating, playing music too loud for conversation, and forgetting to tell anyone the event is happening. Now flip each one: "Turn each disaster into a rule for making the event a success." Running out of food becomes a plan to bake in small batches through the day. No seating becomes a row of borrowed stools and a standing counter. Forgetting to tell anyone becomes a flyer in every neighbouring shop a week before. The failures become a checklist.

How to pick a method for your task

The five methods suit different moments. Use a list dump when you are starting from nothing and need raw material fast. Use SCAMPER when you already have one decent idea and want to improve it. Use role-play when your ideas feel flat or one-sided. Use constraints when the ideas are too grand for your real budget, space, or time. Use reverse brainstorming when you are worried about what could go wrong and want to plan around it.

You can also chain methods together in one conversation. Start with a list dump to get twenty ideas, pick the best three, run each through SCAMPER, then add your real constraints and ask which of the improved ideas still works. A short chain like this takes ten minutes and usually produces a better result than any single method on its own. Keep the conversation in one chat so ChatGPT remembers what you liked and what you rejected.

Turning a long list into a shortlist

Brainstorming only helps if it ends in a decision. Once you have a long list, ask ChatGPT to help you narrow it down instead of adding more ideas. Give it your real criteria: what you can afford, how much time you have, who is coming, and what success looks like. Then ask it to rank the ideas against those criteria and explain the ranking in one line each.

A useful closing prompt is: "Here are my top ten ideas. Rank them by how much fun they would be for families, how cheap they are to run, and how easy they are to set up. Give each idea a score out of ten for each of the three things, and tell me which three I should pick." The scores are not scientific, but they force a comparison, and the comparison is what helps you decide. After that, the choice is yours. ChatGPT can suggest, but only you know your shop, your customers, and your limits.

Questions people ask

How many ideas should I ask ChatGPT for at once?

Start with ten to twenty ideas. That is enough to see a range without drowning in a list you will never read. Once you have a shortlist of three or four favourites, ask for variations on those instead of asking for another long list.

What if all the ideas sound the same?

Change the angle instead of asking again in the same words. Add a constraint, give ChatGPT a role, or ask for the opposite of your first idea. If the list still feels flat, tell it which ideas you already like and which ones bore you, then ask for ideas that are nothing like the boring ones.

Can ChatGPT brainstorm for a business?

Yes, it is good at generating names, slogans, event ideas, and campaign angles. But it does not know your market, your customers, or your local rules, so treat its output as raw material. Check any factual claim before you use it, and keep private business details out of your prompts.

Do I need a special prompt format?

No. Plain sentences work well. What matters is giving ChatGPT a clear goal, some context about who the ideas are for, and a number or a limit so the list stays useful. A method like the ones in this guide matters far more than any special wording.

Should I use the first idea ChatGPT gives me?

Rarely. The first ideas in a list are usually the most obvious ones, which is why they appear first. The better ideas often come when you ask for a second round, combine two ideas, or ask ChatGPT to make the list stranger, cheaper, or simpler.
