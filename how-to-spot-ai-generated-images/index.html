How to Spot AI-Generated Images: 10 Telltale Signs

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

AI-generated images often give themselves away in small details: hands with the wrong number of fingers, garbled text, ears that do not match, and backgrounds that melt into nonsense. This guide walks through ten signs to check, in order, so you can judge a suspicious image in a minute or two. No single sign proves anything on its own, so the guide also covers how to check the source behind the picture.

Not long ago, an AI-generated image was easy to spot. Faces looked waxy, hands looked melted, and text in the background turned into strange symbols. The newest image tools are far better, and many of their pictures look like ordinary photographs at first glance. That is why a quick checklist helps: instead of trusting your first impression, you learn where to look.

The signs below work best together. One odd detail might be a compression artefact, a bad camera angle, or a genuine quirk of real life. Several odd details in the same image, especially the classic ones, are a much stronger signal. And when the pixels cannot settle the question, the source of the image usually can.

Be honest with yourself about the limits of this skill. A good AI image can pass every check on this page, and a real photo can fail one or two of them. These signs raise suspicion; they do not deliver proof. For anything important, check the source and the context, not just the picture.

Why AI images are getting harder to spot

Image generators learn from enormous collections of photographs, and each new version learns finer detail. Early tools struggled with faces and hands because those are complex and varied. Newer tools handle them far better, and they also handle light, reflections, and texture with growing skill. The result is that the obvious mistakes are rarer, and the remaining clues are subtler.

That does not mean spotting is hopeless. AI images are still built by predicting what pixels should come next, not by photographing a real scene. The generator has no understanding of how many fingers a hand has, what a word means, or how light falls across a room. It produces things that look right at a glance, and the errors show up when you look closely at exactly the places where real-world logic matters.

It also helps to know that social media compresses images, which can hide or mimic some of these signs. A heavily compressed real photo can look smeary in the background, and a compressed AI image can hide its mistakes. Where possible, look at the largest version of the image you can find before you judge it.

The 10 telltale signs, at a glance

Work through this list from the top. Check each sign against the image, and count how many look wrong. One or two odd details mean little on their own. Four or more should make you pause and check the source before you share or believe the picture.

  1. Hands and fingers: too many fingers, fingers that bend the wrong way, nails that merge into the skin, or hands that fade into sleeves and objects.
  2. Text and letters: words that look almost right but spell nothing, letters that repeat or melt together, signs and labels with garbled or mirrored characters.
  3. Ears and earrings: ears that do not match each other, earrings that hang in mid-air, or jewellery that blends into hair or clothing.
  4. Backgrounds that melt: people, furniture, or objects in the background that blur into strange shapes, extra limbs on bystanders, or faces that dissolve into the crowd.
  5. Lighting and shadows: shadows that fall in different directions, light on a face that does not match the light in the room, or reflections that show a different scene.
  6. Repeating patterns: tiles, bricks, fences, or fabric patterns that change size or shape as they run across the image, or that repeat in impossible ways.
  7. Too-perfect skin and teeth: skin with no pores, no blemishes, and no texture at all, or teeth that are too many, too even, or merged into a single block.
  8. Reflections that do not match: mirrors, windows, and puddles that show the wrong person, the wrong room, or a reflection that does not line up with the subject.
  9. Objects that blend or merge: a cup growing out of a table, glasses merging into a face, a chair leg that becomes a person's leg, or two objects sharing one edge.
  10. A feeling that something is off: the scene looks polished but slightly unreal, proportions feel wrong, or the mood of the image does not fit the story around it.

Hands and fingers: the classic giveaway

Hands remain one of the most reliable places to look, even as generators improve. A human hand has five fingers, each with joints that bend in one direction, and nails that sit on the fingertips. AI tools often get close and then fail on the details: a sixth finger tucked behind the others, a thumb on the wrong side, or fingers that are different lengths.

Look at every hand in the image, not just the obvious one. A hand resting on a table, holding a cup, or draped over a shoulder is a common failure point. Also look at how hands meet objects. A hand holding a phone should grip it; an AI hand often hovers near the phone, or the fingers pass through it.

Be fair about what you see. Real hands in motion blur, and a photo taken at a strange angle can make fingers look odd. One strange hand in an otherwise normal photo is a weak signal. Two strange hands, or a strange hand plus garbled text, is a much stronger one.

Text, logos, and small details

Generators do not read. They reproduce the shapes of letters without knowing what the letters mean, which is why text is still a rich source of clues. Look at shop signs, book covers, T-shirts, and labels. Real text spells real words. AI text often looks like a word from a distance and dissolves into nonsense up close.

Logos are another good check. A familiar brand logo in an AI image may have the wrong number of letters, a letter flipped around, or a symbol that is almost the right shape but not quite. The same goes for numbers: a clock face, a calendar, or a price tag in an AI image often shows digits that repeat or make no sense.

Small details elsewhere in the frame matter too. Count the legs on chairs, the spokes on a bicycle wheel, or the buttons on a shirt. AI tools are good at the overall shape and weak at keeping small repeated parts consistent, so a bicycle with a wheel that has no clear structure is worth a second look.

Light, shadows, and backgrounds

Light follows rules, and AI images sometimes break them. Pick the brightest light source in the scene, then check the shadows. In a real photo, shadows fall in one consistent direction. In an AI image, one person's shadow may point left while another's points right, or a shadow may be missing entirely.

Reflections deserve the same attention. A mirror should show the back of what the camera sees, and a window should reflect the room. AI reflections often show a different scene altogether, or a face that does not belong to anyone in the picture. Puddles and shiny floors are worth checking for the same reason.

Backgrounds are where generators cut corners. The main subject gets the most attention, and the background gets less. Look at the edges of the frame and the people behind the subject. Faces that melt, bodies that merge, and objects that fade into each other are all common background failures.

Check the source, not just the pixels

When the image itself cannot settle the question, the source often can. Ask where the image came from. A photo posted by a news organisation, a museum, or a photographer's own account is easier to trust than the same image reposted by an account you have never seen, with no caption and no link.

Look for the earliest version of the image. A reverse image search can show whether the picture appeared somewhere else first, with a different caption or a different story. If the same image turns up on several unrelated pages with different claims attached, at least some of those claims are wrong.

Read the account that posted it. A brand-new account, an account that posts only sensational images, or an account whose other posts are all AI pictures is a warning sign. Real photographers and real news sources usually have a history you can check, and their images fit that history.

What to do when you are not sure

If you cannot tell, the safest choice is to treat the image as unverified. Do not share it as fact, and do not build an argument on it. Sharing an uncertain image with a question attached is better than sharing it with a claim attached, but not sharing it at all is better still until you know more.

You can also ask for help. Describe what looks odd and ask others what they see, or look for fact-checking sites that may already have covered a viral image. Popular images get checked quickly, and a short search for the image's description often turns up an existing verdict.

Finally, remember that the goal is not to win a spotting contest. The goal is to avoid being misled, and to avoid misleading others. A cautious pause, a quick check of the signs, and a look at the source will catch most of the AI images you meet, and the ones that slip past are a reason for care, not for giving up on checking.

Questions people ask

Can a real photo fail these checks?

Yes. Motion blur, bad lighting, heavy compression, and unusual angles can make a real photo look strange. A single odd detail is weak evidence. Several odd details together, especially the classic ones like hands and text, are a much stronger signal.

Is there an app that can tell me if an image is AI-generated?

Some detection tools exist, and they can help, but they are not reliable enough to trust on their own. They give a probability, not a verdict, and they can be wrong in both directions. Use them as one more check alongside the signs in this guide, not as a replacement for them.

Why are hands so hard for AI to draw?

Hands are complex and varied. They bend in many ways, they are often partly hidden, and they appear at many angles. A generator predicts pixels rather than understanding anatomy, so it often gets the general shape right and the details wrong, especially the number and position of fingers.

Do AI images always have mistakes?

No. The best AI images can pass every check on this page. That is why the source matters as much as the pixels. A flawless-looking image from an unknown account, posted with a dramatic claim, still deserves a source check before you believe it.

What should I do if I shared an AI image by mistake?

Correct it openly. Delete the post or add a clear correction saying the image was AI-generated, and share the source of the correction if you have one. A quick, honest correction does far less damage than leaving a misleading image up.
