The landmarks by continent

The 22 landmarks on this page are spread across six continents. Here is what each continent holds, so you can plan your browsing — or your dreaming.

  • Asia, five landmarks. The Great Wall of China and the Taj Mahal in India are the two most famous, joined by Angkor Wat in Cambodia, the rose-red city of Petra in Jordan, and Borobudur in Indonesia, a 9th-century Buddhist temple built as a stepped pyramid.
  • Europe, six landmarks. The Eiffel Tower in Paris and the Colosseum in Rome lead the list, with Big Ben in London, the Sagrada Familia in Barcelona, the Acropolis in Athens, and Neuschwanstein Castle in Bavaria, the fairytale castle that inspired storybook drawings around the world.
  • Africa, three landmarks. The Pyramids of Giza in Egypt are the oldest wonder on this page, joined by Victoria Falls on the border of Zambia and Zimbabwe, and Table Mountain above Cape Town in South Africa.
  • North America, three landmarks. The Statue of Liberty in New York harbour, the Grand Canyon in Arizona, and Chichen Itza, the Maya city on Mexico's Yucatan peninsula.
  • South America, three landmarks. Machu Picchu high in the Andes of Peru, Christ the Redeemer above Rio de Janeiro in Brazil, and Iguazu Falls on the border of Argentina and Brazil.
  • Oceania, two landmarks. The Sydney Opera House in Australia, famous for its sail-like roofs, and Uluru, the giant sandstone rock rising from the desert in central Australia, sacred to the Anangu people.

The stories behind the famous ones

The Great Wall of China is not one wall but a chain of walls and forts built over centuries to guard China's northern borders. The first walls went up more than 2,000 years ago, and most of the wall visitors walk on today was built during the Ming dynasty, about 500 to 600 years ago. It is the longest structure ever built by human hands.

The Pyramids of Giza were built around 2560 BCE as tombs for pharaohs — more than 4,500 years ago. The Great Pyramid is the last surviving wonder of the ancient world, and it was the tallest building on Earth for more than 3,800 years.

The Taj Mahal in Agra is a white marble mausoleum built in the 1600s by the emperor Shah Jahan for his wife Mumtaz Mahal. It took more than twenty years to build, and its marble changes colour with the light — pink at dawn, white at noon, golden at sunset.

The Eiffel Tower was built in 1889 for the World's Fair in Paris, and at 330 metres it was the tallest structure in the world when it opened. Many Parisians disliked it at first, but it became the symbol of the city and is now one of the most visited landmarks on Earth.

Machu Picchu is a 15th-century Inca citadel high in the Andes, built of stone fitted together without mortar. It was never found by the Spanish conquerors, and the outside world only learned of it in 1911, when it was brought to attention by the explorer Hiram Bingham.

The Colosseum in Rome opened in 80 CE, and crowds of tens of thousands watched gladiators fight there. It held tens of thousands of spectators, and its ruins are still among the most visited sights in Europe.

Planning a visit: tickets, crowds and best time

The single best piece of advice for famous landmarks is to book ahead. The Eiffel Tower, the Colosseum, the Sagrada Familia and Neuschwanstein Castle all sell timed tickets online, and the ticket line at the gate is always longer than you expect. For Machu Picchu, entry tickets and the train should be booked months ahead, and Statue of Liberty crown tickets sell out weeks in advance.

The second-best advice is to go early. Most landmarks open in the morning, and the first hour is quieter, cooler and better for photographs than any other time of day. The Taj Mahal at sunrise and Angkor Wat at sunrise are famous for a reason — and the crowds arrive later.

Spring and autumn are the kindest seasons for most of the landmarks on this page: mild weather, smaller crowds and softer light. Summer brings heat and queues, and winter can bring closures or bad weather, though some places — the Pyramids, the Taj Mahal, Victoria Falls — are best in the cooler months from November to February.

Check the official site of each landmark before you go. Opening times, ticket prices and access change, and some landmarks close on certain days — the Taj Mahal, for example, is closed on Fridays. A five-minute check saves a wasted journey.

A worked example: one day, three landmarks — how to plan it

Imagine a day in Rome, where three great sights stand close together: the Colosseum, the Roman Forum and Palatine Hill. Here is how to see all three without rushing, using the planning method from this page.

  • The night before: book a timed morning ticket for the Colosseum online, and check the opening times on the official site. Pack water, good shoes and a hat — Rome's ancient stones are hard on feet and there is little shade.
  • Morning, at opening time: start at the Colosseum, when the light is good and the crowds are still thin. Give it two hours, and read the signs — the arena's story is better when you know what you are looking at.
  • Midday: walk next door to the Roman Forum, the ruined heart of ancient Rome, and then up Palatine Hill for the view over the Forum and the city. This is the hottest part of the day, so take it slowly and drink water.
  • Afternoon: rest. A long lunch, a shaded square, and time to look at your photographs. Trying to add a fourth or fifth sight in the afternoon is how a good day turns into a tired one.
  • Evening: walk back past the Colosseum at dusk, when it is lit up and the crowds have thinned. The same building looks like a different place in the evening light.

The pattern works anywhere: one big sight in the morning, nearby sights in the middle of the day, rest in the afternoon, and one evening view. Three landmarks, well seen, beat six landmarks glimpsed through a tired haze.

Common mistakes when visiting landmarks

  • Trying to see everything in one day. A famous landmark deserves hours, not minutes. Pick two or three and enjoy them properly, instead of collecting ten in a blur.
  • Ignoring opening times. Landmarks close on certain days and in certain seasons, and hours change. The Taj Mahal is closed on Fridays, and the Table Mountain cable car stops in strong wind — both are the kind of detail that ruins a day if you only learn it at the gate.
  • Not booking tickets. The most popular landmarks sell out. Machu Picchu tickets go months ahead, Statue of Liberty crown tickets go weeks ahead, and the Eiffel Tower's ticket line can eat half your morning.
  • Going at midday in summer. Heat, glare and crowds all peak at noon. Early morning and late afternoon are better in every way — cooler, quieter and prettier.
  • Forgetting the practical things. Water, good shoes, a hat and a charged phone matter more than a guidebook. The marble paths of the Acropolis are slippery, the climb at Borobudur is steep, and the spray at Victoria Falls will soak you — the tips on each card are there for a reason.
  • Standing too close to the edge. The Grand Canyon's South Rim is open all year and its views are breathtaking, but the edge is a real drop. Stay back from it, especially with children.

What this page cannot do

This page covers 22 landmarks — the most famous ones, but only a small share of the world's great places. There are thousands of castles, temples, mountains and old cities that are not here, and many of them are wonderful. Use this page as a starting point, not a complete list.

It cannot give you live information. Opening times, ticket prices and access change, and this page does not update them. Always check the official site of a landmark before you travel.

And it cannot book anything for you. Tickets, trains and tours are bought from the landmark itself or from a travel agent, never from this page. What this page can do is help you choose where to go, when to go, and what to watch out for — and then test how much you remember with the quiz.

Questions people ask

What is the most visited landmark in the world?

It depends on how you count visitors, but the Forbidden City in Beijing and the Eiffel Tower in Paris are among the most visited landmarks in the world, with millions of visitors every year.

How old is the Great Wall?

The first walls were built more than 2,000 years ago, and most of the wall visitors see today was built during the Ming dynasty, about 500-600 years ago.

When is the best time to visit the Eiffel Tower?

Spring and autumn are the best times to visit the Eiffel Tower — the weather is mild and the queues are shorter than in summer. Evenings are lovely too, when the light is soft. Book your tickets online in advance, because the ticket line at the tower is long.

Do I need tickets in advance?

For the most popular landmarks, yes. The Eiffel Tower, the Colosseum, the Statue of Liberty and Machu Picchu all reward booking ahead — some sell out weeks or months in advance. For outdoor landmarks like the Grand Canyon or Uluru, you can usually just turn up, but always check the official site first.

What is a UNESCO site?

A UNESCO World Heritage Site is a place listed by the United Nations cultural agency because it has outstanding value for all of humanity. Famous examples on this page include the Great Wall of China, the Pyramids of Giza and the Taj Mahal.
