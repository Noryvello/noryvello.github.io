Employee Onboarding Portal

Good onboarding turns a new hire's first weeks from a confusing scramble into a clear plan, with tasks, introductions, and documents laid out in order. This page gives you checklists for the first day, week, and month — for the manager and for the new hire — that you can tick off as you go.

Onboarding rules differ by country: contracts, tax forms, right-to-work checks, and probation rules are set by local law, not by this page. Check the requirements that apply where you hire, and get professional advice when you are unsure.

Why onboarding matters

A new hire's first weeks decide how quickly they become useful and how long they stay. Someone who arrives to a ready desk, a clear plan, and a named person to ask will be doing real work within days. Someone who arrives to no login, no plan, and nobody expecting them will spend their first week just trying to get started — and will remember the experience for a long time.

Onboarding is also where most early mistakes happen. A missed tax form, a skipped safety briefing, a contract signed late — each one is a small failure that is easy to prevent with a list and hard to fix afterwards. A checklist does not make onboarding impersonal; it makes it reliable, so that the personal parts — the welcome, the introductions, the conversations — have room to happen.

The lists on this page cover the first day, the first week, and the first month, from both sides: what the manager should arrange, and what the new hire should do. Tick each item off as it is done; your ticks are saved in this browser, on this device only, so you can work through the lists over days or weeks and come back without losing your place.

Before day one: the manager's list

Most onboarding failures happen before the new hire arrives. The desk is not ready, the accounts are not set up, or nobody told the team that someone new is starting. The list below covers the preparations that make the first day work. Do them in the week before the start date, not on the morning of it.

Send the new hire a short message before they start: the start time, the address or the video link, who to ask for, and what to bring. A new hire who knows these four things arrives calm; one who does not arrives worried. It costs one message.

The first day

The first day should feel welcoming, not overwhelming. The goal is not to teach everything; it is to make the new hire feel expected, equipped, and oriented. A short welcome, a tour of the tools and the team, and one small task they can complete — that is a good first day.

Resist the urge to fill the day with presentations. New hires remember very little of what they are told on day one, and a day of slides leaves them exhausted and no wiser. Short conversations, a few introductions, and time to set up their workspace will do more than any induction pack.

The first week

The first week is about learning how the team works. The new hire should meet the people they will work with most, understand the current projects, and start doing real tasks with support. By the end of the week they should know where to find answers, who to ask, and what their first month looks like.

Give feedback early and kindly. A short check-in each day — five minutes, in person or on a call — catches confusion while it is still small. Ask what is unclear, not just what is done: new hires often struggle silently rather than admit they are lost.

The first month

By the end of the first month, a new hire should be doing the core of their job with light supervision, and the manager should have a clear picture of how they are settling in. The month is also the time to confirm the formal side: the contract, the probation terms, and any training the role requires.

Do not wait for a formal review to find out how things are going. A short conversation at the end of week two and another at the end of week four will tell you more than any form. Ask the new hire what surprised them, what confused them, and what they would change about the onboarding itself — their answers are the best guide to improving it for the next person.

The new hire's own list

Onboarding is not something done to a new hire; it is something they take part in. This list is for the new hire: the steps that turn a good onboarding plan into a good start. Share it with them on day one, so they know what is expected and what to ask for.

The most important item is the simplest: ask questions. New hires who ask early learn faster than those who wait, and no reasonable team minds being asked. Write questions down as they come to mind, and bring them to the daily check-in, so nothing is forgotten.

Documents to collect

Every new hire brings paperwork, and every hire creates it. The list below covers the documents most employers need to collect or file. The exact list depends on your country's rules, so treat this as a starting point and check the local requirements that apply to you.

Collect documents early, and store them securely. Identity and tax documents contain personal details, and they deserve the same care as any other sensitive file: kept where only the people who need them can reach them, and never shared by an unsecured link.

Common onboarding mistakes

The most common mistake is starting late. If the accounts are not ready on day one, the new hire's first day is wasted waiting, and the team's first impression is disorganisation. Prepare in the week before, not on the morning of.

The second mistake is information overload. A new hire cannot absorb a month of knowledge in a day, and trying to teach everything at once teaches nothing. Spread learning over the first month, and let real tasks do most of the teaching.

The third mistake is no buddy. A new hire with no one person to ask will ask nobody, and will struggle quietly. A named buddy — not the manager, but a peer — is the single cheapest improvement most onboarding plans can make.

The fourth mistake is never asking how it went. The new hire's view of their first month is the best information you will ever get about your onboarding, and it is only available once. Ask for it, write it down, and use it to make the next person's start better.

Questions people ask

What should a manager prepare before a new hire's first day?

The signed contract, a ready desk and computer, working accounts for every tool the role needs, a planned first-week schedule, and a named buddy or mentor. The team should also know the start date and the new hire's name and role. Most onboarding failures happen before day one, so prepare in the week before, not on the morning of.

How long should onboarding last?

At least a month. The first day is about welcome and orientation, the first week about learning how the team works, and the first month about doing the core of the job with light supervision. Some roles need longer, especially where training or certification is involved. A single day of induction is not onboarding.

What is the most important part of a new hire's first day?

Feeling expected and equipped: a welcome, introductions to the team, a working desk and logins, and one small real task they can finish. Avoid filling the day with presentations — new hires remember very little of what they are told on day one, and short conversations teach more than slides.

Should a new hire have a buddy or mentor?

Yes. A named buddy — a peer, not the manager — is the single cheapest improvement most onboarding plans can make. The buddy answers the small questions a new hire does not want to ask their manager, and helps them learn how the team really works. Without a buddy, new hires often struggle quietly.

What documents does a new employer usually need to collect?

A signed employment contract, identity documents as local law requires, tax forms, bank details for pay, emergency contact details, and any licences or certificates the role requires. The exact list depends on your country's rules, so check the local requirements. Store these documents securely, as they contain personal details.

How do you know if your onboarding is working?

Ask the new hire at the end of the first week and the first month: what went well, what confused them, and what they would change. Their answers are the best guide to improving the process. You will also see it in how quickly new hires become useful and how long they stay.
