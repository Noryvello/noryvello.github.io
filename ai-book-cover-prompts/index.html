AI Book Cover Prompts: How to Design a Cover That Sells the Book

By the Noryvello team · Published 20 November 2026 · Updated 20 November 2026

A book cover is a tall rectangle that most readers first see as a tiny thumbnail, so it has a harder job than a picture: it must signal the genre, set the mood, and stay readable at the size of a postage stamp. This guide shows you the cover prompt formula, how to leave room for the title, and what to do after the image is generated.

Image tools change their menus and buttons often, so if a step below does not match what you see on screen, the tool's own help pages are the authority. Before you sell a book with an AI cover, check the tool's commercial-use terms.

What a book cover must do

A book cover has three jobs, and it must do all of them in the few seconds a reader gives it. The first job is to signal the genre: a reader should know, at a glance, whether the book is a romance, a thriller, a fantasy, or a self-help guide. The second is to set the mood — warm, tense, epic, calm — so the reader feels something before reading a word. The third is to stay readable as a tiny thumbnail, because that is how most readers will first see it.

The thumbnail job is the one most covers fail. On a shop page or a phone screen, a cover appears at the size of a postage stamp, and everything that made it beautiful at full size — the fine detail, the subtle texture, the small figures — turns to noise. A cover that only works large is not finished. The test is simple: shrink the image small, and ask whether the central image still reads and the genre still shows.

A cover is also a promise about the book inside. A thriller cover on a gentle romance misleads the reader who picks it up, and a romance cover on a thriller does the same in reverse. The prettiest cover in the world hurts the book if it promises the wrong thing, which is why genre comes first in the prompt formula below — before the mood, before the style, before everything else.

The good news is that a cover prompt is a small, learnable skill. It follows a formula, it uses a short list of genre signals, and it improves fast once you start testing thumbnails. The sections below walk through it in order, from genre to generation to what happens after.

Genre first — how covers signal romance, thriller, fantasy, mystery, and non-fiction

Every genre has a visual language that readers recognise instantly, and a cover prompt works by speaking that language plainly. You do not need to be subtle: the genre word goes first in the prompt, and the visual signals follow it.

  • Romance. Warm colours, soft light, and a tender detail — two figures close together, a hand held, a sunset shared. The mood is warm and tender, and the palette leans toward gold, rose, and cream.
  • Thriller. Dark, high contrast, and a lone figure or an unsettling object: a foggy street, a single lit window, a shadow that should not be there. The mood is tense, and the palette is dark with one sharp accent.
  • Fantasy. Epic scale and impossible light: a castle above the clouds, a glowing forest, a traveller small against a vast landscape. The mood is wondrous, and the palette is rich and deep.
  • Mystery. Shadows, a single clue, and muted tones: a half-open door, a letter on a table, a figure seen from behind. The mood is quiet and unsettling, and the palette is restrained.
  • Non-fiction. Clean, bold, and one clear idea: a single strong image, plenty of empty space, and a palette that says calm authority rather than drama.

When you are unsure of a genre's signals, look at the bestselling covers in that genre and note what they share — not to copy any one cover, but to learn the language. A cover that speaks the genre's language fluently will be read correctly by the readers who matter, and that is the whole job.

The prompt formula for covers

A cover prompt has five parts, in this order: genre, mood, central image, title space, style. The order matters because the model weighs the first words most, so the genre goes first and the style goes last. Five parts is enough: more than that and the prompt starts pulling in too many directions.

The genre is one word — romance, thriller, fantasy, mystery, non-fiction — followed by the words novel cover or book cover, which tell the model the shape and purpose of the image. The mood is one feeling phrase: warm and tender, tense and dark, epic and mysterious. The central image is the one strong thing the reader sees first: a couple silhouetted at sunset, a lone figure in fog, a castle above mist.

The title space is the part beginners forget, and it has its own section below: a plain area, usually the top third of the cover, left empty for the title. The style is one word — painterly, cinematic, watercolour, minimal flat — and it should match the genre: painterly suits fantasy and romance, cinematic suits thriller, minimal flat suits non-fiction.

Put together, the formula reads like this:

Romance novel cover, warm and tender mood, a couple silhouetted at sunset on a beach, empty sky at the top for the title, painterly style, tall portrait format

That is one sentence and five parts, with the format named at the end — tall portrait format tells the model the cover's tall rectangle shape. When a result disappoints you, change one part at a time and compare the results side by side.

Leaving room for the title

The title is the most important text on a cover, and it is the one thing image AI cannot be trusted to write. Image models learn the shapes of letters, not spelling, so a title in the prompt comes out garbled more often than not — almost right, which is worse than wrong, because it is easy to miss at a glance.

The fix is to leave room for the title and add it later in a design tool. In the prompt, ask for a plain area where the title will go: empty sky at the top for the title, plain dark area at the top for the title, lots of empty sky at the top. The top third of the cover is the usual place, because readers read top to bottom and the title is the first thing they should see.

That empty area must be genuinely empty — plain sky, plain shadow, plain colour — not a busy sky full of birds and clouds. If the model fills it with detail, the title will have nowhere clean to sit. Ask for empty and plain, and mean it; a busy top third defeats the purpose.

Adding the title in a design tool has a second benefit: control. You choose the font, the size, and the exact wording, and you can change the title without generating the cover again. A cover whose title lives in a design tool is a cover you can revise; a cover whose title is baked into the image is a cover you have to regenerate every time the title changes.

Colour and contrast for tiny thumbnails

A cover that looks beautiful at full size can fail completely as a thumbnail, and the reason is almost always colour and contrast. At the size of a postage stamp, fine detail disappears, subtle shades merge, and only the strongest shapes and the boldest contrasts survive.

The first rule is one strong focal point. A cover with one central image — one figure, one castle, one path — reads at any size. A cover with three competing subjects turns to noise when it shrinks, because the eye has nowhere to land. If you can describe the cover's image in five words, it has one focal point; if you need a paragraph, it has too many.

The second rule is high contrast between the subject and the background. A light figure against a dark sky, or a dark figure against a bright horizon, reads instantly. A mid-tone figure against a mid-tone background disappears. The thumbnail test is the judge: shrink the image small, and if the subject still stands out, the contrast is doing its job.

The third rule is few colours. Two or three main colours keep a cover bold and memorable; five or six turn it muddy at small sizes. And remember the title: light text needs a dark area behind it, and dark text needs a light one, so the empty title space and the text colour must be planned together, not separately.

Front cover vs full wrap

The front cover is the priority, and for a first book it is often all you need. It is the image readers see in a shop listing, on a phone screen, and on a recommendation page — and it is the image the prompt formula in this guide is built to produce. Get the front right first, and everything else follows from it.

A full wrap is the front, the spine, and the back as one continuous image, and it is a bigger job. The spine carries the title and the author name in a narrow vertical strip, and the back usually carries a blurb, a short author note, and a plain background. Prompting for all three at once is hard, because the model has to hold three different purposes in one image.

The practical approach is to generate the front cover first, then extend it. Use the front image as the basis, extend the background across the spine and back in a design tool, and keep the back simple — a plain continuation of the front's colours, with room for the blurb. The spine takes the title in a real font, vertically, which is a design-tool job, not a generation job.

Many first-time authors never need a full wrap at all: an ebook needs only the front, and a print book's wrap can come later, once the front is proven. Start with the front, test it as a thumbnail, and build the wrap only when the front is doing its job.

8 ready cover prompt patterns

Each pattern below follows the five-part formula and leaves the top of the cover empty for the title. Copy a pattern, change the central image to fit your book, and generate several versions before you judge it. Every pattern ends with the tall portrait format, because a cover is a tall rectangle.

  • Romance. Warm, tender, and golden.
    Romance novel cover, warm tender mood, a couple silhouetted at sunset on a quiet beach, empty warm sky at the top for the title, soft painterly style, tall portrait format
  • Thriller. Dark, tense, and lonely.
    Thriller novel cover, tense and dark mood, a lone figure walking away down a foggy street at night, single streetlight glow, empty dark sky at the top for the title, cinematic style, tall portrait format
  • Fantasy. Epic scale and glowing light.
    Fantasy novel cover, epic and mysterious mood, a castle on a cliff above glowing mist, a lone traveller on a path below, empty twilight sky at the top for the title, painterly style, tall portrait format
  • Mystery. Quiet, shadowed, and restrained.
    Mystery novel cover, quiet and unsettling mood, a single lit window in a dark old house, bare tree branches, empty dark sky at the top for the title, muted colours, tall portrait format
  • Science fiction. Vast, cold, and clean.
    Science fiction novel cover, vast and cold mood, a small spacecraft above a ringed planet, deep blue and silver palette, empty dark space at the top for the title, clean digital style, tall portrait format
  • Historical. Rich, warm, and lived-in.
    Historical novel cover, rich and warm mood, a cobbled street in an old European town at dusk, horse-drawn cart in the distance, empty dusky sky at the top for the title, painterly style, tall portrait format
  • Children's picture book. Bright, playful, and kind.
    Children's picture book cover, bright and playful mood, a friendly fox reading under a big oak tree, warm sunlight, empty soft sky at the top for the title, watercolour style, tall portrait format
  • Non-fiction self-help. Calm, clear, and spacious.
    Non-fiction self-help book cover, calm and clear mood, a single winding path through green hills toward morning light, lots of empty sky at the top for the title, minimal flat style, tall portrait format

Use these patterns as starting points. Change the central image, swap the mood, try a different style word — and always test the result as a thumbnail, because the thumbnail is where the cover earns its keep.

After generation

Generating the cover image is half the job. What you do next decides whether it becomes a real cover or stays a pretty picture. Start by generating several versions of the same prompt and picking the one with the strongest central image and the cleanest empty space at the top — the best version is rarely the first.

Next, add the title in a design tool. Pick a font that fits the genre — a serif for historical, a clean sans-serif for non-fiction, something with character for fantasy — and size the title big: it is the first thing a reader should read, and it must survive the thumbnail test. The author name goes smaller, usually at the bottom, where it does not compete with the title.

Then sort out the size. A cover is usually made at print size, with bleed — bleed means the image extends slightly past the edge where the page will be trimmed, so no white edge shows after cutting. The exact size depends on the book's trim size and the printer, so check your printer or publisher's size guide rather than guessing. Export at high resolution, because a cover that looks sharp on screen can still print soft.

Finally, test the finished cover as a thumbnail with the title on it. Shrink it to the size of a postage stamp and ask the three questions: does the genre show, does the central image read, can you read the title? If the answer to all three is yes, the cover is doing its job.

Copyright note

Before you sell a book with an AI cover, check the tool's commercial-use terms. Terms differ between tools, and they can change: some tools allow commercial use of generated images, some restrict it, and some attach conditions. The tool's own terms of use are the authority, and they are worth reading before the book goes on sale, not after.

Two habits keep you on safer ground. The first is to make something of your own: do not ask for a copy of another book's cover, a famous character, or a famous film's look, because a cover that copies another work can cause copyright trouble. The second is to keep a record of the tool you used and the prompt you wrote, so you can show how the cover was made if anyone ever asks.

This section is general information, not legal advice. Copyright and commercial-use rules vary, and a book you plan to sell widely deserves a careful read of the terms — and, for a commercial project, advice from someone qualified to give it. The cover is the face of your book; making sure you have the right to use it is part of finishing the job.

Questions people ask

Can AI design a real book cover?

Yes, as the artwork — the image, the mood, and the genre signals. The title and author name should be added later in a design tool, where the spelling and the font are under your control. Expect to clean up the image and test it as a thumbnail.

Why should I leave space for the title?

Because image AI misspells text: letters are shapes to the model, not spelling. Leaving the top of the cover empty lets you add the real title in a real font, and change it later without generating the cover again.

What makes a cover read well as a tiny thumbnail?

One strong focal point, high contrast between the subject and the background, and two or three main colours. Fine detail turns to noise at thumbnail size, so the central image must read in a glance.

Which style fits a thriller, romance, or fantasy cover?

A thriller suits a dark, high-contrast, cinematic style; a romance suits a warm, soft, painterly style; a fantasy suits epic scale with glowing light, also painterly. Match the style to the genre's mood.

Can I sell a book with an AI cover?

It depends on the tool you used, so check the tool's commercial-use terms before the book goes on sale, and do not copy another book's cover or a famous character. This is general information, not legal advice.

What size should a cover be?

It depends on the book's trim size and the printer, so check your printer or publisher's size guide. Make the cover at print size with bleed — the image extending slightly past the trim edge — and export at high resolution.
