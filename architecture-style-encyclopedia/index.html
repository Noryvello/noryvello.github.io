Architecture Style Encyclopedia

This encyclopedia explains the main house architecture styles in plain language, from Victorian and Colonial to modern and minimalist. Each style entry covers its key features, its history, and what to look for when you see one.

Styles overlap and vary by region, and many homes mix features from more than one style. Use this page as a guide to recognising styles, not as a rulebook.

How to read a house style

A house style is a set of features that belong together: a roof shape, a window shape, a porch, a material, a way of arranging rooms. Styles grow out of a time, a place, and a way of living, and they carry the marks of both. A steep roof sheds snow. A deep porch shades a hot climate. A tall window lights a room before electric light existed.

You do not need training to read a style. Start with the roof: is it steep or flat, gabled or hipped? Then look at the windows: are they tall and narrow, or wide and square? Then look at the entrance: is there a porch, a portico, or a plain door? Then look at the materials: brick, timber, stone, stucco, glass? Each answer narrows the style down, and by the fourth answer you usually know what you are looking at.

This encyclopedia covers the styles you are most likely to meet, grouped by era. For each style you will find its key features, a short history, and what to look for. Remember that styles mix: a house can be Colonial in shape and modern in its windows, and a new house can borrow an old style. The entries describe the classic form of each style; real houses vary.

Victorian and Gothic Revival

Victorian. The Victorian style takes its name from the reign of Queen Victoria in Britain, from 1837 to 1901, and it spread across the English-speaking world. Victorian houses are tall and richly decorated: steep roofs, bay windows, towers and turrets, patterned brickwork, and ornate woodwork around the porch and the eaves. Inside, the rooms are generous and the ceilings are high. What to look for: asymmetry — a Victorian house rarely has a plain, balanced front — and decoration on every surface, from the roofline to the door.

Gothic Revival. Gothic Revival came earlier, in the late 1700s and 1800s, and it borrowed from medieval churches and castles. Its marks are pointed-arch windows, steep gables, decorative bargeboards along the roof edges, and sometimes a small tower or a finial on the roof peak. Gothic Revival houses feel romantic and a little dramatic, and they were popular for country houses and cottages alike. What to look for: the pointed arch — if the windows come to a point at the top, the house is probably Gothic Revival.

Colonial, Georgian and Cape Cod

Colonial. Colonial houses are the plain, balanced homes built by European settlers in North America from the 1600s onwards, and the style has been revived many times since. A Colonial house is usually two storeys, built of brick or timber, with a symmetrical front: a central door, the same number of windows on each side, and a chimney at each end. What to look for: symmetry — if the front of the house could be folded in half and the two sides would match, it is probably Colonial.

Georgian. Georgian style takes its name from the British kings named George, and it covers the 1700s and early 1800s. Georgian houses are formal and balanced, like Colonial houses, but grander: taller ceilings, larger windows, decorative cornices, and often a portico over the door with columns. The style values order and proportion above all. What to look for: five windows across the front, a central door with a fanlight above it, and a calm, regular rhythm.

Cape Cod. Cape Cod houses began in New England in the 1600s and were revived in the 1900s as small, affordable family homes. A Cape Cod is a small, one-and-a-half-storey house with a steep pitched roof, a central door, and dormer windows in the roof. The rooms are compact and the plan is simple, which makes Cape Cods cheap to build and easy to heat. What to look for: the steep roof with dormers, and the small, neat, symmetrical front.

Craftsman and Tudor

Craftsman. The Craftsman style grew in the early 1900s as a reaction against fussy Victorian decoration. Craftsman houses celebrate honest work: low-pitched roofs with wide eaves, exposed beams, tapered columns on the porch, built-in cupboards and window seats, and natural materials such as wood and stone. The decoration comes from the craftsmanship itself rather than from applied ornament. What to look for: a deep front porch with thick, tapered columns, and brackets under the eaves.

Tudor. Tudor Revival, popular in the 1920s and 1930s, borrowed from English houses of the 1500s. Its marks are steep gables, half-timbering — dark wooden beams set into light plaster — tall, narrow windows with small panes, massive chimneys, and arched doorways. Tudor houses feel solid and storybook-like, and they were often built for wealthy suburbs. What to look for: the half-timbering on the upper floor and the tall, decorative chimney.

Mediterranean and Spanish Revival

Mediterranean. Mediterranean Revival, popular in warm climates in the 1920s and 1930s, borrowed from the houses of Spain, Italy and Greece. Its marks are stucco walls, red tile roofs, arched windows and doorways, wrought-iron balconies, and courtyards with fountains or tiled floors. The style suits hot weather: thick walls keep rooms cool, and shaded outdoor rooms extend the living space. What to look for: the red tile roof and the arches — together they are the clearest sign of the style.

Spanish Revival. Spanish Revival is the Spanish branch of the Mediterranean style, with a stronger emphasis on courtyards, towers, and ornate tilework. Spanish Revival houses often have a bell tower or a small turret, carved wooden doors, and colourful glazed tiles around the entrance or the fountain. The style was especially popular in California and Florida, where the climate matched the Spanish original. What to look for: a tower, a courtyard, and bright tilework around the door.

Modern, Mid-century modern and Art Deco

Modern. Modern architecture, which grew from the 1920s onwards, rejected decoration and historical styles altogether. Modern houses are built from concrete, steel and glass, with flat roofs, large windows, open floor plans, and plain, smooth walls. The idea is that a house should be honest about its materials and its structure, and that beauty comes from proportion and light rather than from ornament. What to look for: a flat roof, walls of glass, and no decorative moulding anywhere.

Mid-century modern. Mid-century modern is the warm, residential form of modernism, popular from the 1940s to the 1960s. Its marks are low, horizontal lines, large windows that open onto the garden, open-plan living, and natural materials such as wood and stone mixed with glass. Mid-century houses were designed for relaxed, informal living, and they are among the most sought-after homes today. What to look for: a low, flat or gently sloping roof, and a wall of glass facing the garden.

Art Deco. Art Deco, named after a 1925 exhibition in Paris, is the glamorous style of the 1920s and 1930s. Its marks are bold geometric shapes, stepped outlines, zigzags and sunburst patterns, smooth stucco walls, and decorative details in chrome, glass and coloured tile. Art Deco houses are rare, but the style is common in cinemas, hotels and apartment blocks of the period. What to look for: the stepped silhouette and the geometric decoration around the door and the windows.

Minimalist, Contemporary and Ranch

Minimalist. Minimalism takes the modern idea further: less is more. A minimalist house has plain walls, few materials, no ornament, and as little furniture as possible. The beauty comes from light, space and proportion, and from the quality of a small number of materials. Minimalist houses can feel calm and spacious, but they demand discipline: with nowhere to hide, every object and every detail is on show. What to look for: plain white or pale walls, hidden storage, and large, empty-feeling rooms.

Contemporary. Contemporary is not one style but the style of the present day: houses built now, using current materials and current ideas. Contemporary houses often mix materials — wood, glass, metal, concrete — and they often have large windows, open plans, and features such as solar panels and green roofs. Because the style is still changing, a contemporary house is best described by its features rather than by a single label. What to look for: a mix of materials, large windows, and a roofline that is neither flat nor traditionally pitched.

Ranch. The ranch house, popular in the United States from the 1940s onwards, is a long, low, single-storey home with a simple roofline and an open plan. Ranch houses were built for the suburbs: they are practical, affordable, and easy to live in, with the garage attached and the living rooms opening onto a patio or a garden. What to look for: the long, low shape, the attached garage, and the picture window at the front.

Farmhouse and Cottage styles

Farmhouse. The farmhouse style comes from working farms, and it values practicality above all. Farmhouse houses have a large kitchen, a deep porch, a simple pitched roof, and rooms arranged for daily work rather than for show. Modern farmhouse style keeps the practical plan and the natural materials — wood, stone, brick — and adds large windows and open living. What to look for: the big kitchen, the wraparound porch, and the simple, honest materials.

Cottage. Cottage style covers small, cosy houses of many kinds, from English country cottages to beach cottages. The shared marks are small scale, a pitched roof, a fireplace, and a garden close to the door. Cottages are built for comfort rather than display, and they are often the most loved houses in a neighbourhood. What to look for: the small size, the pitched roof, and the sense that the house fits its garden rather than dominating it.

When styles mix

Most houses are not pure examples of one style. A Victorian house may have a modern extension. A Colonial house may have Craftsman windows. A new house may borrow a Tudor chimney and a contemporary glass wall. This is normal, and it is part of what makes streets interesting: styles evolve by borrowing from each other. When styles mix, look for the dominant feature — the roof, the windows, or the porch — and use that as your starting point. A house with a steep Tudor roof and modern windows is still best described as Tudor Revival with modern updates. The style of a house is a guide to its history, not a box it must fit into.

How to spot a style and what it means for buyers

When you are looking at a house, the style tells you useful things. A Victorian house will have high ceilings and large rooms, but it may also have old wiring and single-glazed windows. A Craftsman house will have solid woodwork and built-in storage, but the rooms may be smaller than in a modern home. A modern house will have light and open space, but flat roofs need care and large windows need cleaning.

Style also affects upkeep. Ornate styles need more maintenance: woodwork to paint, decoration to repair, old materials to care for. Plain styles need less, but they show every mark and every repair. Before you buy, look at the features that define the style and ask what they will cost to maintain. A beautiful porch is a joy; a rotting porch is a bill.

Finally, remember that style is a matter of taste as well as history. The right house is the one you enjoy living in, whatever its style is called. Use this encyclopedia to understand what you are looking at, and then trust your own feeling about the home.

Questions people ask

What is the most common house style?

There is no single answer: the common style depends on the country, the region, and the age of the neighbourhood. In older areas you will find Victorian, Colonial and Georgian houses; in newer suburbs you will find ranch, contemporary and modern homes.

What is the difference between modern and contemporary?

Modern is a historical style from the 1920s onwards, with flat roofs, glass walls and no decoration. Contemporary means the style of the present day: houses built now, often mixing materials and current ideas.

What makes a house Victorian?

A Victorian house is tall and richly decorated, with steep roofs, bay windows, towers or turrets, patterned brickwork and ornate woodwork. The style comes from the reign of Queen Victoria, from 1837 to 1901.

What is a Craftsman house?

A Craftsman house celebrates honest work: low-pitched roofs with wide eaves, exposed beams, tapered porch columns, built-in cupboards and natural materials. The decoration comes from the craftsmanship itself.

Can a house be more than one style?

Yes, and most houses are. Styles mix all the time: a Victorian house may have a modern extension, and a new house may borrow a Tudor chimney. Look for the dominant feature — the roof, the windows or the porch — as your starting point.

Does style affect a home's value?

Style affects how appealing a home is and how much it costs to maintain, but value depends on many things: location, size, condition and the local market. A well-kept house in any style is worth more than a neglected one.
