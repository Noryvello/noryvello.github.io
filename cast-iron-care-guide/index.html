Cast Iron Care Guide

A cast iron pan can last a lifetime if you look after it, and the care is simpler than its reputation suggests. This guide walks through seasoning, daily cleaning and rust removal, step by step, with a checklist to keep your pan in good shape.

Cast iron earns its loyal following for good reasons. It holds heat evenly, it goes from hob to oven without complaint, and it gets better the more you cook with it. The catch is that it is bare metal with a thin layer of seasoning on top, so it needs a little more attention than a non-stick pan. Once the routine is a habit, it takes only a few minutes after each meal.

Cast iron is heavy and stays hot for a long time. Use oven gloves when handling a hot pan, let it cool before washing, and never plunge a hot pan into cold water. Keep the handle away from the edge of the hob so nobody knocks it.

What seasoning is and why it matters

Seasoning is a thin layer of oil that has been heated until it bonds to the iron. It does two jobs: it stops food from sticking, and it stops the bare metal from rusting. A well-seasoned pan looks dark and slightly glossy, and food slides across it with ease. A pan that has lost its seasoning looks dull and grey, and food starts to cling.

Seasoning builds up with use. Every time you fry with oil, you add a little more to the layer. That is why an old pan that has cooked a thousand meals is often better than a new one. Oven seasoning, described below, gives a new or restored pan its first coat, and cooking does the rest.

There is no single perfect oil for seasoning. What matters is a thin layer and enough heat. A neutral cooking oil with a high smoke point works well. Olive oil can leave a sticky layer if too much is used, so wipe it on thinly whatever oil you choose.

How to season cast iron

Season a new pan before its first use, and re-season a pan that looks dull, patchy or rusty. The whole job takes about an hour, most of it waiting for the oven.

  1. Wash the pan with hot water and a little soap, then dry it thoroughly. If there is rust, scrub it off first using the rust-removal steps below.
  2. Put the pan on a low heat for a few minutes to drive off every drop of water, then let it cool enough to handle.
  3. Rub a very thin layer of oil over the whole pan, inside and out, including the handle. Then wipe it all off with a clean cloth until the pan looks almost dry. This thinness is the whole secret.
  4. Put the pan upside down in a cold oven and heat the oven to a high temperature, above the oil's smoke point. A baking tray on the shelf below catches any drips.
  5. Leave the pan in the hot oven for about an hour, then turn the oven off and let the pan cool inside it.
  6. Repeat the oil and heating steps once or twice more if the pan is new or was badly rusted. Each round adds a little more seasoning.

If the pan comes out sticky, too much oil was left on. Do not worry: scrub it back with hot water and a brush, dry it, and repeat with a thinner layer. A good seasoning job feels dry and smooth, never tacky.

Daily cleaning

Everyday cleaning is quick. The goal is to remove food, dry the pan fully, and leave a whisper of oil behind.

  1. Let the pan cool until you can hold it comfortably, then wash it with hot water and a brush or sponge. A little mild soap is fine.
  2. For stuck-on food, pour in a little water and simmer it on the hob for a few minutes, then scrape gently with a wooden spoon. A handful of coarse salt and a paper towel also lifts stubborn bits.
  3. Rinse and dry the pan at once. Put it on a low heat for a few minutes so every drop of water evaporates.
  4. While the pan is warm, rub in a very thin layer of oil with a paper towel, wiping until it looks dry.
  5. Store the pan in a dry place. If you stack pans, put a paper towel between them to protect the seasoning.

Do not soak cast iron, and do not leave it in the sink. Water is the enemy of seasoning, and a long soak is the fastest way to invite rust. If you are in a hurry, at least rinse the pan, dry it on the heat and oil it lightly; a full wash can wait for a better moment.

Removing rust

Rust looks alarming, but it is almost always fixable. Surface rust is a thin orange film, and it comes off with scrubbing. Deep rust that has pitted the metal is harder, but even that is often worth saving if the pan is otherwise sound.

  1. Scrub the rusty areas with a stiff brush or a scouring pad until the orange is gone and bare grey metal shows. For heavy rust, a paste of coarse salt and water helps.
  2. Wash the pan with hot water and a little soap, then rinse well.
  3. Dry the pan on a low heat at once. Bare iron rusts quickly when wet, so do not leave it sitting damp.
  4. Season the pan in the oven using the steps above, repeating for two or three rounds so the restored surface gets a proper coat.

After rust removal, cook something fatty in the pan for its first few meals, such as frying vegetables in oil or making a batch of fried potatoes. That builds the seasoning back up faster than cooking watery or acidic food.

Mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is using too much oil when seasoning. A thick layer does not bond properly and turns sticky. Wipe the oil on, then wipe it off again until the pan looks dry. The layer that remains is exactly enough.

The second mistake is cooking acidic food in a new pan. Tomatoes, vinegar and wine can strip young seasoning. Once a pan has a solid layer built up over months of cooking, the odd tomato sauce is fine, but a freshly seasoned pan does better with frying and roasting first.

The third mistake is storing a pan while it is still damp. Even a little moisture trapped under a lid or in a cupboard starts rust. Dry with heat every time, and let the pan cool before you put it away. If you live somewhere humid, the thin oil layer after drying matters even more.

Finally, do not fear the pan. Cast iron is tough. A scratch in the seasoning heals with the next few cooks, a little rust scrubs off, and a pan that looks tired can be brought back with one oven seasoning. Cook with it often, keep it dry, and it will outlast most things in your kitchen.

Questions people ask

Can I use soap on cast iron?

A little mild soap and a soft sponge are fine for everyday cleaning. The old advice to never use soap came from harsh lye soaps that could strip seasoning. Modern dish soap is gentle, but rinse well and dry the pan at once.

How often should I season my pan?

Season a new or restored pan before first use, then cook with it. Each time you cook with oil, the seasoning builds a little more. Re-season in the oven only when the surface looks dull, patchy or rusty.

Why is my pan sticky after seasoning?

Too much oil was left on the surface. Seasoning needs a very thin layer, wiped until the pan looks almost dry. If it comes out sticky, scrub it back and repeat with less oil.

Can I put cast iron in the dishwasher?

No. Dishwasher detergent and long soaking strip seasoning and invite rust. Wash cast iron by hand, dry it with heat, and rub in a thin layer of oil before storing.

Is a rusty pan ruined?

Almost never. Surface rust comes off with scrubbing, and even a badly rusted pan can usually be restored with a good scrub, a rinse and a fresh round of seasoning. Only deep pitting or a crack means the pan is done.
