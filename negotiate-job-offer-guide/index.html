How to Negotiate a Job Offer

A job offer is usually a starting point for a conversation, not a final answer. This guide covers what can be discussed — pay, start date and duties — with a checklist and a script builder for your talking points.

Receiving a job offer is a good moment, and it is also a moment to slow down. Most offers leave room for discussion, and most employers expect it. Negotiating does not mean being difficult. It means asking, politely and clearly, whether the details of the offer can be adjusted to fit both sides. The conversation goes well when you know what matters to you, you have thought about what to ask, and you keep the tone warm throughout.

This guide is general information only and is not legal advice. Employment rules vary by place and contract, so check your offer documents carefully and seek professional advice if anything is unclear.

What can be negotiated

Pay is the most commonly discussed part of an offer, but it is far from the only one. The start date is often flexible, especially if you need time to finish your current role properly. Duties can sometimes be shaped, particularly where the role is new or the team is small. Other details — working hours, working location, time off, training, and the review schedule — may also be open to discussion, depending on the employer and the role.

Before you reply, decide what matters most to you. A short list of priorities, in order, keeps the conversation focused. If pay is the priority, lead with that. If the start date matters most because of a notice period or a personal commitment, say so plainly. Trying to negotiate everything at once weakens every point, so pick the one or two things that matter most and ask about those first.

Before you reply

Read the offer documents carefully, all the way through. Check the job title, the duties, the working hours, the location, the pay and how it is reviewed, the start date, and any conditions attached to the offer. Note anything that is unclear or that differs from what was discussed in the interview. A careful read now prevents misunderstandings later.

Look into the going rate for the role in general terms, so that any discussion about pay is grounded in what is reasonable. Then decide on your reply: accept, ask to discuss, or decline. If you want to discuss the details, it is polite to say that you are pleased with the offer and keen to accept, and that you would like to talk through a few points before you confirm. That framing keeps the tone warm and makes clear that you want the role.

How to say it

Keep the tone warm and the language simple. Start by thanking them for the offer and saying that you are pleased. Then raise your points one at a time, briefly and plainly. Then ask whether those points can be discussed. Short, clear sentences work better than long explanations, and they leave room for the other person to respond.

Whether to negotiate in writing or by phone depends on the situation. Writing gives you time to choose your words and leaves a clear record. A phone call allows a quicker back-and-forth and a warmer tone. Either way, ask for any agreed changes to be confirmed in writing before you accept, so that both sides have the same understanding.

After you agree

Once the details are settled, confirm your acceptance in writing and thank the employer. Keep a copy of the final offer documents. If a point could not be changed, note what was said about it, including any promise to revisit it later, and the date that was mentioned. Then focus on the start: handing over your current work properly, preparing for the first week, and arriving with a clear sense of what you agreed. A negotiation handled well leaves both sides feeling good about the decision, and that is the best possible start to a new role.

Questions people ask

Is it rude to negotiate a job offer?

No. Most employers expect some discussion, and asking politely about the details is normal. Keep the tone warm, thank them for the offer, and raise your points plainly and briefly.

What if the employer withdraws the offer?

That is very unlikely when the negotiation is polite and reasonable. Employers withdraw offers for serious reasons, not because a candidate asked a fair question. A calm, respectful tone protects you.

Should I negotiate by email or by phone?

Both work. Writing gives you time to choose your words and leaves a clear record. A phone call allows a quicker back-and-forth. Either way, ask for agreed changes to be confirmed in writing.

What else can I ask about besides pay?

The start date, the duties, working hours, working location, time off, training and the review schedule may all be open to discussion, depending on the employer and the role. Pick the one or two that matter most.

How long can I take to decide on an offer?

Check the deadline in the offer documents. If you need more time, ask politely and explain why. Most employers will allow a short extension when the request is reasonable.
