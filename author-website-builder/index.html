Author Website Builder

An author website has one job: to turn a curious reader into a reader of your books. This guide explains what the site needs — your books, your story and a way to stay in touch — and walks you through building it step by step, with a template outline you can follow.

This guide teaches you how to build an author site — it is not a site builder and it cannot build or host a site for you. Pick a platform, follow the steps, and launch something simple first; you can improve it after it exists.

What an author website needs

Every author site, whatever the genre, needs the same four things. It needs your books, presented so a visitor can find them and decide to read one. It needs your bio, written so a reader feels they know you a little. It needs a mailing list signup, so interested readers can hear from you again. And it needs a way to contact you, for readers, reviewers and event organisers. Everything else is optional.

Notice what is not on that list: a blog you update daily, a shop you run yourself, a forum, a complex design. Those can come later, if ever. A simple site with four good pages will serve most authors better than a large site that is half-finished. Readers come for the books; the site's job is to get out of the way and let them find one.

Choosing how to build it

Most authors should build on a hosted platform rather than coding a site from scratch. A hosted platform handles the technical work — hosting, security, updates — and gives you templates you can shape to your books. The three best-known options are all capable of a good author site, and the differences are about fit, not quality.

WordPress.com (wordpress.com) is the most flexible of the three, with the widest choice of designs and features, and it grows well if your site later becomes more ambitious. Squarespace (squarespace.com) is known for beautiful templates and a smooth editing experience, which suits authors who care about design and want little technical fuss. Wix (wix.com) offers a drag-and-drop editor that is easy to start with. Compare them by feature and by the pricing on their own sites, which changes over time, and pick the one whose editing style feels comfortable to you.

Whichever you choose, buy your own domain name — your name if you can get it — and use it from the start. A site on your own domain looks professional, survives a move between platforms, and is easy for readers to remember and share. The cost is small, and it is the single best investment in the site.

Your books page

The books page is the heart of the site, and it should answer three questions for every book: what is it, why would I enjoy it, and where can I get it? Show the cover, write a short description in your own voice, add one honest line from a review if you have one, and link to the retailers where readers can buy it. Keep the newest book first and the oldest last.

Write the descriptions as a reader would want to read them, not as a catalogue entry. A sentence about who the book is for is worth more than a list of its features. If a book belongs to a series, say so clearly and give the reading order — nothing frustrates a new reader more than starting at book three by accident.

Your bio page

A good author bio is short, warm and honest. Readers want to know who you are, what you write and why, and one human detail that makes you memorable. They do not need your full life story, your qualifications or a list of every publication. Two or three short paragraphs are enough for most authors, with a photograph that looks like you.

Write it in the third person if that feels more comfortable, or the first person if you want a closer tone — either works, as long as it sounds like you. End with an invitation: the mailing list, the newest book, or a way to get in touch. A bio that ends nowhere wastes the goodwill it just built.

Your mailing list

A mailing list is the most valuable part of an author site, because it is the one way to reach readers directly. Social media algorithms change, search rankings shift, but your list stays yours. Put the signup where visitors will see it — the home page and the end of the books page — and say plainly what subscribers will get: news of new books, occasional behind-the-scenes notes, nothing more.

Write to the list only when you have something worth saying: a new book, an event, a story readers would enjoy. A short message once a month beats a long one every week, and every message should feel like a letter from you, not a circular. Never add anyone without their permission, and make leaving easy — a small list of willing readers is worth far more than a large list of strangers.

Step-by-step: building the site

Start with the domain name and the platform account, then choose a simple template and change nothing about it except the colours and the fonts. Add the four pages — home, books, bio, contact — and write the content for each before you touch the design again. A site with finished words in a plain template is ready to launch; a site with beautiful design and empty pages is not.

  • Buy your domain name and open your platform account
  • Choose a simple template and change only colours and fonts
  • Write the four pages: home, books, bio and contact
  • Add the mailing list signup to the home page and books page
  • Check every page on your phone, fix anything broken, then launch
  • Tell your readers the site exists — email, social media, your books

Launch when the site is good enough, not when it is perfect. A live site with four good pages can be improved in public; a perfect site that never launches helps no reader. Set a launch date, keep it, and improve one thing a week after that.

Template outline: pages and sections

Copy this outline and fill it in with your own content. Home page: your name, one line about what you write, the newest book with its cover, and the mailing list signup. Books page: every book, newest first, each with cover, short description and retailer links. Bio page: two or three short paragraphs and a photograph. Contact page: a short note on how to reach you and what you welcome hearing about.

Optional pages, added only when you have real content for them: an events page listing readings and signings, a press page with a downloadable photo and a short bio for journalists, and a page for a series with its reading order. Do not create a page until you have something genuine to put on it — an empty page is worse than no page at all.

Keeping the site alive

A website that never changes slowly stops being visited, but keeping it alive takes less work than most authors fear. Update the home page when a new book comes out, add events as they are confirmed, and write to the mailing list when there is real news. A site updated four times a year is alive; a site updated never is a monument.

Once a year, walk through the whole site as a new reader would: click every link, check every page on your phone, and fix anything broken or out of date. That annual walkthrough takes an hour and prevents the slow decay that makes sites look abandoned. If a page no longer serves any purpose, delete it — a smaller, fresher site is always better than a larger, stale one.

Mistakes authors make with websites

The most common mistake is building a large site before there is content to fill it: ten empty pages, a blog with two posts, a shop with nothing in it. Start with four pages done well and grow from there. The second mistake is hiding the books — a home page that talks about the author for five paragraphs before mentioning a single book has the priorities backwards.

Other mistakes are just as common: a site that does not work on a phone, where most readers will find it; no mailing list signup, so interested readers leave and never return; and a contact page that gives no way to actually make contact. Check your site against this list before launch, and fix whatever you find — each fix takes minutes and each one matters.

  • Ten empty pages instead of four finished ones
  • Hiding the books behind long writing about the author
  • A site that breaks on a phone, where most readers find it
  • No mailing list signup, so interested readers never return
  • A contact page with no real way to make contact

Questions people ask

What pages does an author website need?

Four pages cover almost every author: home, books, bio and contact. Add a mailing list signup to the home and books pages. Only add more pages — events, press, a series page — when you have real content to put on them.

Which platform is best for an author website?

WordPress.com is the most flexible, Squarespace is known for beautiful templates and smooth editing, and Wix offers an easy drag-and-drop editor. Compare them by feature and by the pricing on their own sites, and pick the one whose editing style feels comfortable.

Do I need my own domain name?

Yes — your name if you can get it. A site on your own domain looks professional, is easy to remember and share, and survives a move between platforms. It is a small cost and the single best investment in the site.

How do I get readers onto my mailing list?

Put the signup where visitors will see it, say plainly what subscribers will get, and only write when you have something worth saying. Never add anyone without permission, and make leaving easy — a small willing list beats a large unwilling one.

How often should I update my author website?

Update it when there is real news: a new book, a new event, a new review worth quoting. A site updated four times a year is alive. Once a year, walk through the whole site as a new reader and fix anything broken or out of date.

Should my author website have a blog?

Only if you genuinely enjoy writing one and can keep it up. A blog with two posts from last year makes a site look abandoned. Most authors are better served by a mailing list and four good pages than by a blog they resent maintaining.
