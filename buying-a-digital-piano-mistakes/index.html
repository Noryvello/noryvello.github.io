7 Mistakes to Avoid When Buying a Digital Piano

The most common mistake when buying a digital piano is choosing too few keys or keys that feel nothing like a piano, which limits how far you can progress. This guide covers the seven mistakes beginners make most often, plus a keys and features checklist.

A digital piano is a wonderful way to start playing: it never needs tuning, it can be played quietly with headphones, and it fits in a room where an acoustic piano never could. But the range of models is wide, and the differences between them matter far more than the price tags suggest.

The two things that matter most are the keys — how many there are and how they feel — and the extras that make daily practice comfortable. Get those right and the piano will serve you for years; get them wrong and you will be shopping again far sooner than you planned.

This guide is general buying advice, not a product review. Features vary between models and makers, so check the details of any piano before you buy, and try the keys in person if you can.

The 7 mistakes to avoid

  1. Buying too few keys. Fewer keys limit the music you can play, and beginners outgrow a small keyboard sooner than they expect. Pieces you want to play will reach beyond the keys you have, and shifting octaves mid-piece is no substitute for having the full range. A full set of keys is the best choice for most beginners, and it is the one decision that is hardest to fix later without buying a new piano.
  2. Ignoring how the keys feel. Keys are where your fingers meet the instrument, so their feel matters enormously. Weighted keys feel closer to an acoustic piano, with resistance that builds finger strength and control. Light, unweighted keys feel very different and make moving to an acoustic piano much harder later. Play the keys before you buy if you possibly can, and notice how they respond to a gentle touch and a firm one.
  3. Forgetting the pedals. A sustain pedal matters from the very start, because many pieces need it from the first weeks of learning. Check whether a pedal is included with the piano, and whether it is a proper pedal or a simple footswitch — they feel very different under your foot. If no pedal is included, make sure you know what to buy and that it fits your piano.
  4. Skipping the stand and bench. A piano balanced on a wobbly table makes practice harder and less enjoyable, and a chair at the wrong height makes good hand position difficult. Check whether a proper stand is included or available for your model, and make sure you have a bench or chair at the right height. A steady stand and the right seat turn practice from a chore into something you look forward to.
  5. Judging by the number of sounds. A long list of built-in sounds looks impressive, but most players use a small number of them — usually the main piano sound and perhaps one or two others. Sound quality matters far more than quantity: one beautiful piano sound beats dozens of thin ones. Listen to the main piano sound carefully before you buy, because it is the sound you will live with.
  6. Not checking the headphone socket. Quiet practice is one of the great advantages of a digital piano, but only if the headphone socket works well. Check that the piano has a headphone socket, and if you can, listen through headphones before you buy — some pianos sound very different through headphones than through their speakers. If you share your home, this check matters a great deal.
  7. Forgetting space and power. Measure the spot where the piano will live, including room for the bench and for your knees underneath. Check that the power supply is included in the box, and check the weight of the piano if you will need to move it. A piano that does not fit its space, or arrives without a power supply, is a frustrating start to learning.

Before you buy

Once the checklist is done, take a final listen. Play the main piano sound at a low volume and at a high volume, try the keys gently and firmly, and listen through headphones if you can. Ask the seller about the warranty and the returns policy, because a piano that sounds fine in the shop can feel different in your own room. Then take it home and start with something simple — a few scales and a short piece you like are a much better beginning than a difficult piece that leaves you discouraged.

Questions people ask

How many keys do I need on a digital piano?

A full set of keys is the best choice for most beginners, because fewer keys limit the music you can play as you progress. If space is tight, a smaller keyboard can work for a start, but you will outgrow it sooner.

What are weighted keys?

Weighted keys are built to feel like the keys of an acoustic piano, with more resistance than light plastic keys. They help you build finger strength and control, and they make moving to an acoustic piano much easier later.

Do I need pedals for a digital piano?

A sustain pedal matters from the start, because many pieces need it. Check whether a pedal is included with the piano, and whether it is a proper pedal or a simple footswitch.

Can I learn on a keyboard with fewer keys?

You can learn the basics on a smaller keyboard, but you will run out of keys sooner than you expect. If you are serious about learning, a full set of keys will serve you far longer.

What else do I need to start playing?

You need a sturdy stand, a bench or chair at the right height, a sustain pedal, and a power supply. Headphones are useful for quiet practice in a shared home.
