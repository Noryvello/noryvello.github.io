Eid al-Adha Meat Sharing Guide

By the Noryvello team · Published 19 November 2026 · Updated 19 November 2026

Qurbani meat is traditionally divided into three parts: one for the family, one for relatives and friends, and one for people in need. This guide explains who receives a share, how the three-part division works, and how distribution is organised in practice.

Eid al-Adha is the festival of sacrifice, and the sharing of Qurbani meat is one of its most important traditions. The sacrifice is not only an act of worship: it is also an act of giving. Meat that a family could keep for itself is divided and shared, so that the celebration reaches relatives, neighbours, and people who may rarely eat meat at all.

The sharing follows a simple and generous pattern that has been passed down for generations. Understanding it before Eid helps you plan: how much to set aside, how to pack shares, and how to reach the people you want to give to. This guide explains the division, the people it serves, and the practical side of getting meat from the sacrifice to the table.

Customs and rulings around Qurbani vary between communities and scholars, so check with your local mosque or a trusted scholar if you are unsure about any detail. Handle meat safely: keep it cool, pack it cleanly, and deliver shares promptly.

What Qurbani meat sharing is

Qurbani is the sacrifice of an animal during Eid al-Adha, performed in remembrance of the Prophet Ibrahim and his willingness to sacrifice what he loved most. The meat is not kept entirely by the family that offers the sacrifice. Instead, it is shared, and that sharing is part of the act itself: the celebration is meant to reach beyond one household.

The sharing has a simple purpose. It feeds the family, strengthens ties with relatives and friends, and makes sure that people in need have meat for Eid. In many communities, the days of Eid are the busiest days of the year for giving, and the meat is often delivered personally, with a greeting and a visit, rather than simply handed over.

The three-part division

The well-known division splits the meat into three roughly equal parts. One part is for the family that offered the sacrifice, to eat and enjoy over Eid. One part is for relatives and friends, given as gifts and shared over visits and meals. One part is for people in need, given as charity so that they too can celebrate with meat on the table.

The three parts are a guide rather than a rigid rule, and families adjust them to their circumstances. Some families give more than a third to charity, especially when they know many people in need. Others keep a little more for a large household. The spirit of the division matters more than exact measures: a share for yourself, a share for those close to you, and a share for those who need it most.

  • One part for the family: to eat and enjoy over the days of Eid.
  • One part for relatives and friends: given as gifts and shared over meals.
  • One part for people in need: given as charity so they can celebrate too.

Who receives a share

The first group is your own household: the people who live with you and share your table. The second group is your wider circle: parents, siblings, cousins, friends, and neighbours. These shares are gifts, given with a greeting, and they often travel with a visit: you take meat to an elder relative, or a neighbour drops by with a bag for you.

The third group is people in need: families who struggle to afford meat, elderly people living alone, and anyone in the community who would welcome a share. Mosques and charities often help here, collecting meat and distributing it to families they know. If you are unsure who to give to, your local mosque can usually point you to people who would benefit most.

Giving is also a chance to think about people outside your usual circle. A new family in the neighbourhood, a student far from home, or a neighbour who has had a hard year may all welcome a share and a greeting. The meat opens the door; the visit is what they remember.

How distribution works in practice

On the day of the sacrifice, the meat is usually cut and divided soon after, while it is fresh. Many families work together: one person cuts, another weighs or portions, and another packs. Shares for relatives are often set aside in bags or containers, labelled if needed, and delivered the same day or the next. Shares for charity are often collected by a mosque or a volunteer group.

Timing matters. Meat is best distributed quickly, while it is fresh, and on a warm day it should not sit out for long. If you are delivering shares yourself, plan your route in advance and take a cool bag if you have one. If a mosque or charity is collecting, find out their collection times before Eid so your share arrives when they can handle it.

Preparing and packing shares

A good share is a useful share. Include a mix of cuts where you can, rather than giving one person only bones or only offcuts, and cut the meat into pieces that are easy to cook. Pack shares in clean bags or containers, and tie or seal them well so they travel safely. If you are giving to someone you do not know well, a clean, well-packed share shows care.

Label packs if you are preparing many shares, especially if some are for particular people or families. Keep raw meat away from cooked food, wash your hands and knives between tasks, and wipe down surfaces as you go. A little care in packing makes the giving feel generous rather than hurried.

Sharing when you live far from family

Many people celebrate Eid far from their family, and distance changes how sharing works. If your relatives live in another town or country, you may arrange for Qurbani to be performed where they are, through a trusted mosque or charity, so that the meat reaches them directly. This is a common arrangement, and it means the sharing still happens even when you cannot be there in person.

If you perform Qurbani where you live, your local community becomes your circle of sharing: neighbours, friends, and local families in need. The three parts still apply, even if the people are different. What matters is that the meat is shared, not that it travels a long way to do so.

Storing and using your share

Your own share is for the days of Eid and beyond. Keep what you will cook in the next day or two in the fridge, and freeze the rest in meal-sized portions, labelled with the date. Freezing in portions means you can take out only what you need, and the meat stays in good condition for weeks.

Plan a few meals around your share: a feast dish for Eid day itself, a slow-cooked dish for the following day, and something simple, such as grilled meat with bread and salad, for later in the week. If you received shares from others as well, you may have more meat than you need: pass some on to someone else, and the giving continues.

Label each frozen portion with what it is and the date you froze it, so you can find what you need without opening every bag. Take portions out of the freezer the night before you cook them and let them thaw in the fridge, never on a warm counter. A little organisation now means easy meals for weeks after Eid.

Questions people ask

What is the three-part division of Qurbani meat?

The meat is divided into three roughly equal parts: one for the family, one for relatives and friends, and one for people in need. The division is a guide rather than a rigid rule, and families adjust it to their circumstances.

Who should receive a share of the meat?

Your household, your relatives and friends, and people in need all receive a share. If you are unsure who in your area needs help, your local mosque or a local charity can usually suggest families who would benefit.

Can I give more than one part to charity?

Yes, many families give more than a third to charity, especially when they know many people in need. The three parts are a guide, and giving more to those who need it is in the spirit of the tradition.

What if I perform Qurbani in another country?

Many people arrange Qurbani where their family lives, through a trusted mosque or charity, so the meat reaches relatives and people in need there. Check the arrangement in advance and confirm how the meat will be distributed.

How should I pack shares for delivery?

Pack shares in clean bags or containers, include a mix of cuts, and seal them well. Label packs if you are preparing many, keep raw meat away from cooked food, and deliver shares promptly, using a cool bag on a warm day.

How long can Qurbani meat be kept?

Keep what you will cook soon in the fridge and freeze the rest in meal-sized portions, labelled with the date. Frozen meat keeps for weeks, while fridge meat should be cooked within a few days.
