How to Photograph a Solar Eclipse

Photographing a solar eclipse is safe and rewarding — but only with a certified solar filter covering the front of the lens for every partial phase. This guide covers the safety rules first, then starting settings for cameras and phones, and a pinhole alternative that needs no filter at all.

A solar eclipse is one of the most photographed events in the sky, and the next big one — the total eclipse of 2 August 2027 — will draw cameras across the path of totality and far beyond it. Whether you shoot with a dedicated camera or a phone, the same rule decides everything: protect your eyes and your sensor first, and think about settings second. This guide follows that order.

Never look at the Sun, or through a camera viewfinder, without a certified solar filter. All settings given on this page are starting points, not universal values — the right exposure depends on your filter, your camera, haze, and the Sun's height, so check your shots and adjust.

Safety first: the filter rules

A certified solar filter (ISO 12312-2) must cover the front of the lens for every partial phase and for the whole of an annular eclipse. The filter goes on the front, where it blocks the Sun's light before it enters the lens — that is the only position that protects both your eyes and your camera.

Never use sunglasses, ordinary ND filters, polarisers, smoked glass, or photographic film as a substitute. None of these blocks the Sun's light the way a certified solar filter does, and none of them is safe to look through or to shoot through.

Never look through a camera viewfinder at the Sun without a filter on the lens. A camera lens concentrates sunlight, so looking through it unfiltered is far more dangerous than looking at the Sun with the naked eye. For the same reason, never place eclipse glasses behind binoculars, a telescope, or a camera lens — the concentrated sunlight can burn through them. Eclipse glasses are made for your eyes only, with nothing in front of them.

The filter may be removed only during totality, only from inside the path of totality, and only after the Sun's bright surface is fully covered. The moment totality ends, the filter must go back on at once. If you are outside the path of totality, the filter stays on for the whole event — there is no safe moment to remove it. Note that from the UK the 2 August 2027 eclipse is partial only, so the filter stays on for the whole event there.

Phones need a filter too. Pointing a phone at the Sun can damage its sensor, just as it can damage a camera's sensor, so the same rule applies: cover the rear lens with a certified solar filter for every partial phase.

Camera settings: DSLR and mirrorless

Set the camera to manual mode, so you control the exposure rather than leaving it to the camera. Automatic settings struggle with an eclipse because the frame is mostly dark with one very bright subject, and the camera will keep changing its mind. Shoot RAW, which keeps more detail to work with later than a compressed file does.

Focus manually and lock the focus on the Sun's edge. Autofocus can hunt and miss on a bright disk against a dark sky, so set the focus once, check it, and leave it alone. Use a tripod plus a remote release or the 2-second timer, so pressing the shutter does not shake the camera.

Rehearse before the day. On an ordinary sunny day, fit the filter, set up the tripod, and practise framing and focusing on the Sun. A rehearsal teaches you how your own camera and filter behave, and it means you will not be learning the setup while the eclipse is already under way.

Starting points with a filter on, for the partial phases: ISO 100, f/8–f/11, 1/500–1/4000 second. Bracket several frames — take the same shot at a few different exposures — so that at least one of them is likely to be right. For totality, with the filter off: ISO 100–800, f/5.6–f/11, about 1/1000 second to 1 second or longer. A 200–600 mm lens gives a larger solar disk in the frame, so the eclipsed Sun fills more of the picture.

Remember that these are starting points, not universal values. The right exposure depends on the filter, the camera, haze, and the Sun's height, so check each shot on the camera's screen and adjust as the light changes.

Photographing an eclipse with a phone

Cover the rear lens with a certified solar filter for every partial phase, just as you would with a camera. Hold the filter flat against the lens so no unfiltered light leaks in around the edges.

Lock focus and exposure by tapping and holding on the Sun. Without that lock, the phone will keep refocusing and re-exposing as the light changes, and your shots will come out inconsistent. Use optical zoom, not digital zoom — digital zoom only crops the picture and loses detail, while optical zoom uses the lens itself. Use pro or manual mode if the phone has one, so you can fix the exposure rather than letting the phone decide.

A small tripod mount helps, especially for longer exposures and for keeping the Sun steady in the frame. And remember that phones are best for wide, atmospheric shots: the eclipsed Sun in a landscape, the changing light, the people watching. Those pictures often say more about the day than a small disk of the Sun on its own.

The pinhole alternative

If you have no solar filter, you can still photograph the eclipse safely with a pinhole. Make a small hole in a card and hold the card between the Sun and a flat surface — a wall, a sheet of paper, or the ground. The hole projects an image of the Sun onto the surface, and you can photograph that projection. Photograph the projection, never the Sun through the pinhole: looking at the Sun through the hole is not safe, and the projection is the picture.

Before the day: a quick checklist

  • Certified solar filter (ISO 12312-2) that fits the front of the lens, or over the phone's rear lens.
  • Camera set to manual mode, shooting RAW, with manual focus locked on the Sun's edge.
  • Tripod, plus a remote release or the 2-second timer.
  • A rehearsal done on an ordinary sunny day, with the filter fitted.
  • Starting settings noted down: with the filter, ISO 100, f/8–f/11, 1/500–1/4000 second, bracketing several frames; for totality, ISO 100–800, f/5.6–f/11, about 1/1000 second to 1 second or longer.
  • A 200–600 mm lens if you want a larger solar disk, and a small tripod mount for a phone.
  • A pinhole card ready as a backup, in case you want a second way to capture the eclipse.

Questions people ask

Do I need a filter to photograph an eclipse?

Yes. A certified solar filter (ISO 12312-2) must cover the front of the lens for every partial phase and for the whole of an annular eclipse. Without one, the concentrated sunlight can damage your eyes, your camera's sensor, and a phone's sensor too.

When can I remove the solar filter?

Only during totality, only from inside the path of totality, and only after the Sun's bright surface is fully covered. The filter must go back on the instant totality ends — and during a partial eclipse, it never comes off at all.

Can I photograph an eclipse with a phone?

Yes, with a certified solar filter over the rear lens for every partial phase. Lock focus and exposure by tapping and holding on the Sun, use optical zoom rather than digital zoom, and try wide shots of the landscape and the changing light as well as the Sun itself.

What camera settings should I start with?

With a filter on, for the partial phases, start at ISO 100, f/8 to f/11, and 1/500 to 1/4000 second, and bracket several frames. For totality, with the filter off, start around ISO 100 to 800, f/5.6 to f/11, and about 1/1000 second to 1 second or longer. These are starting points, not fixed values — check your shots and adjust.

Is it safe to photograph a partial eclipse?

Yes, as long as a certified solar filter stays on the front of the lens for the whole event. Never look through the viewfinder without the filter, and never use sunglasses, ordinary ND filters, or smoked glass as a substitute — they do not protect your eyes or your camera.
