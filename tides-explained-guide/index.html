Tides Explained

Tides are the regular rise and fall of sea level, caused mostly by the Moon's gravity pulling on the oceans. The Moon's pull makes a bulge of water on the side of the Earth facing it, and a second bulge on the far side, so most coasts pass through two high tides and two low tides each day.

Stand on a beach for a few hours and the sea will creep up the sand or slide away from it, as regular as breathing. That rise and fall is the tide, and it is driven by something hundreds of thousands of kilometres away: the Moon. The Sun plays a part too, and together they keep the oceans moving in a rhythm that has run for as long as there have been seas.

Tides matter to sailors, fishermen, beach walkers and anyone who lives near the coast. They also follow a pattern simple enough to grasp in a few minutes: two bulges of water, two tides a day, and a monthly cycle of bigger and smaller tides set by the Moon's phases.

How the Moon pulls the sea

Gravity pulls everything towards everything else, and the Moon pulls on the Earth and its oceans. The pull is strongest on the side of the Earth facing the Moon, so the water on that side is drawn towards the Moon and piles up into a bulge. That bulge is a high tide, and it stays roughly under the Moon as the Earth turns.

There is a second bulge on the opposite side of the Earth, which is the part people find surprising. It forms because the Moon pulls the solid Earth towards it more strongly than it pulls the water on the far side, which is further away. The Earth is pulled slightly out from under its far-side ocean, leaving a second bulge of water behind. So the oceans bulge on both sides at once: one bulge pulled towards the Moon, and one left behind on the far side.

As the Earth spins, a coastline passes through both bulges in one day, which gives two high tides, with a low tide in between when the coast is between the bulges. That is why most coasts see two high tides and two low tides each day, roughly twelve hours apart.

Why high tide comes later each day

If the Moon stayed still, high tide would arrive at the same time every day. But the Moon moves along its orbit while the Earth spins, so the Earth has to turn a little further each day to bring the same coast back under the Moon. High tide therefore arrives a little later each day, by about fifty minutes.

That daily shift is why tide times cannot be fixed to the clock. A high tide at noon today will be closer to one in the afternoon tomorrow, and the pattern keeps sliding through the day over the course of a month. It is also why tide tables are published for each coast: the times are always moving.

The Moon's orbit is the same reason the tides follow the Moon's monthly cycle. As the Moon moves from new to full and back again, its position relative to the Sun changes, and that changes how strongly the Sun's pull adds to or cancels the Moon's pull.

The Sun's part: spring and neap tides

The Sun is far bigger than the Moon, but it is also much further away, so its pull on the oceans is weaker than the Moon's. It is still strong enough to matter. When the Sun, Moon and Earth line up, which happens at new Moon and at full Moon, the Sun's pull adds to the Moon's pull and the tides grow bigger. These are called spring tides, and they have nothing to do with the season of spring: the name comes from the old word for rising or swelling.

Spring tides bring the highest high tides and the lowest low tides of the month, which means the biggest difference between high and low water. When the Sun and Moon pull at right angles to each other, which happens at the first and last quarter Moons, the Sun's pull partly cancels the Moon's pull. These are called neap tides, and they bring the smallest difference between high and low water.

Why tides are different from coast to coast

If the Earth were covered in a smooth, even ocean, the tides would be the same everywhere. Real coasts are not smooth. The shape of a bay, the depth of the water, and the way the land funnels the sea all change how high the tide rises and when it arrives. A narrow bay can squeeze the tidal bulge into a much higher tide, while a wide, open coast may see only a small rise.

Most coasts get two high tides a day, but some get only one, and some get a mixed pattern of one big tide and one small tide each day. The local geography decides. That is why two beaches a short distance apart can have noticeably different tide times and heights.

Weather plays a part too. A strong wind blowing towards the shore can push water up and raise the tide, and low air pressure can let the sea level rise slightly. These effects are small beside the Moon's pull, but they are enough to make tide tables a guide rather than a guarantee, which is why sailors always check the latest local figures.

Tides and the shore

Tides shape the shore itself. The twice-daily wash of water sorts sand and pebbles, feeds the creatures that live between the high and low water marks, and carves the rock pools that appear at low tide. The band of shore that the tide covers and uncovers is one of the richest habitats on any coast.

Tides have also shaped human life by the sea. Fishermen time their trips to the tide, harbours are built around it, and some of the earliest clocks and calendars were tied to the Moon that drives it. The same pull that lifts the sea also slows the Earth's spin very slightly and pushes the Moon a little further away each year, a slow exchange of energy that has been running for billions of years.

This guide carries no tide tables or live data, and tide times and heights vary from coast to coast. Before walking on tidal flats, crossing a causeway or going near the water, check a current local tide table, because the tide can come in faster than it looks.

Questions people ask

What causes tides?

Tides are caused mostly by the Moon's gravity pulling on the oceans, which makes a bulge of water on the side facing the Moon and a second bulge on the far side. The Sun's pull adds a smaller effect.

Why are there two high tides a day?

Because the oceans bulge on two sides of the Earth at once, one towards the Moon and one on the far side. As the Earth spins, a coast passes through both bulges each day, giving two high tides.

What are spring tides?

Spring tides are the biggest tides of the month, with the highest high tides and the lowest low tides. They happen around new Moon and full Moon, when the Sun, Moon and Earth line up and their pulls add together.

What are neap tides?

Neap tides are the smallest tides of the month, with the least difference between high and low water. They happen around the first and last quarter Moons, when the Sun and Moon pull at right angles and partly cancel each other.

Why does high tide come later each day?

Because the Moon moves along its orbit while the Earth spins, so the Earth has to turn a little further each day to bring the same coast back under the Moon. High tide arrives about fifty minutes later each day.
