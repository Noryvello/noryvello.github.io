Solar Eclipse 2027 Safety Guide

By the Noryvello team · Published 19 November 2026 · Updated 19 November 2026

Watching the total solar eclipse on 2 August 2027 is safe if you protect your eyes properly, and dangerous if you do not. The rule is simple: use only ISO 12312-2 certified eclipse glasses for looking at the Sun, and never use ordinary sunglasses.

A total solar eclipse is safe to watch, but only if you respect the Sun. Looking directly at the Sun without proper protection can damage the retina, the light-sensitive layer at the back of the eye, and the damage can happen without pain, so you may not notice it at the time. That is why eclipse safety is not a detail to sort out on the day, but the first thing to plan.

The good news is that safe viewing is simple and cheap. Certified eclipse glasses cost little, a pinhole viewer can be made from a piece of card, and the rules are easy to remember. This guide covers what to use, how to use it, and the mistakes to avoid.

This page is general information only, not medical advice. If you are unsure about your eyesight or your eclipse glasses, ask an eye-care professional before the eclipse.

The one rule that matters

Only look at the Sun through ISO 12312-2 certified eclipse glasses. That standard is the international safety mark for direct solar viewing, and glasses that carry it have filters designed to block the harmful light that damages eyes. Genuine eclipse glasses make the Sun look like a small, dim orange disc, and everything else looks dark through them. If you can see ordinary objects clearly through your glasses, they are not eclipse glasses.

Buy your glasses from a reputable seller, and buy them early, because demand rises sharply before a major eclipse and fakes appear. Check that the glasses are marked ISO 12312-2, and check them for damage before use. Scratched, torn or punctured filters should not be used at all. If in doubt, replace them rather than risking your eyesight.

How to use eclipse glasses properly

Using the glasses correctly matters as much as owning them. Put your eclipse glasses on before you look up at the Sun, and look away from the Sun before you take them off. This sounds obvious, but it is the step people get wrong most often, usually because they are excited and forget. Make it a habit before the day: glasses on, then look up; look down, then glasses off.

Check your glasses every time you use them. Hold them up to a bright light and look for holes, scratches or tears in the filter. Do not use glasses that are damaged in any way, and do not try to repair them. Keep them in a case or envelope when you are not using them, so they do not get scratched in a bag or pocket.

Totality: the only safe moment without glasses

There is one exception to the glasses rule, and it is the whole point of the eclipse. During totality, when the Moon covers the Sun's bright disc completely, it is safe to look directly at the eclipse with the naked eye. This is the moment to see the corona, the Sun's outer atmosphere, glowing around the dark disc of the Moon. In Luxor, totality lasts about 6 minutes 20 seconds, from about 1:02 to 1:08 p.m. local time.

The moment totality ends and the Sun begins to reappear, put your glasses back on immediately. The first sliver of returning sunlight is as dangerous as full sunlight. If you are unsure whether totality has ended, keep your glasses on: it is always better to miss a second of the eclipse than to risk your eyes. Set a timer, or have someone in your group call out the time, so nobody is caught looking up when the Sun returns.

The pinhole viewer: a safe way to watch indirectly

A pinhole viewer is a safe, simple way to watch the eclipse without looking at the Sun at all. Make one by punching a small, clean hole in a piece of card, then standing with your back to the Sun and holding the card up so sunlight passes through the hole and projects an image of the eclipsed Sun onto a second piece of paper or the ground. As the eclipse progresses, the projected image changes shape, and you can watch the whole partial phase safely.

The key rule is that you never look through the hole at the Sun. The viewer works by projection, not by looking through it. This makes it ideal for children, for groups, and for anyone who does not have eclipse glasses. You can also use a kitchen colander or a straw hat: any object with small holes will project dozens of little eclipse images at once, which is a lovely thing to watch on the ground.

Cameras, binoculars and telescopes

Never look at the Sun through binoculars, a telescope or a camera viewfinder unless a proper solar filter is fitted to the front of the instrument. These devices gather and concentrate sunlight, which makes them far more dangerous to the eyes than looking with the naked eye. A solar filter must go on the front, where the light enters, not at the eyepiece.

If you want photographs, the safest approach for most people is to use a camera with a solar filter fitted over the lens, and to look at the camera's screen rather than through a viewfinder. Do not point an unfiltered camera at the Sun for long periods, and do not stare at the Sun while aiming. For most people, the best advice is simple: watch the eclipse with your eyes and your glasses, and let the photographs take care of themselves.

Mistakes to avoid

Ordinary sunglasses are never safe for looking at the Sun, no matter how dark they are. The same goes for smoked glass, photographic film, CDs, and any homemade filter that is not certified to ISO 12312-2. These things may make the Sun look dimmer, but they do not block the harmful light that damages the retina, and they create a false sense of safety that is worse than no protection at all.

Other mistakes include using damaged eclipse glasses, letting children watch unsupervised, and forgetting to put glasses back on when totality ends. The pattern behind all of them is the same: people underestimate the Sun because it does not hurt to look at it briefly. Eclipse eye damage is painless at the time and can be permanent, which is why the rules are worth following exactly.

Watching with children

Children can enjoy an eclipse safely, but they need adult supervision throughout. Eclipse glasses made for adults may not fit a child's face properly, so check the fit beforehand and look for glasses sized for children if you can. Practise with the glasses at home before the day, so children know the routine: glasses on before looking up, look away before taking them off.

A pinhole viewer is an excellent option for younger children, because there is no temptation to look at the Sun directly. Give children a job to do, such as drawing the changing shape of the projected Sun, or counting down the minutes to totality. And keep an eye on the clock: children are the most likely to be caught looking up when totality ends, so an adult should be in charge of calling time.

Before the day: a safety checklist

Sort out safety well before 2 August 2027, not on the morning of the eclipse. Buy ISO 12312-2 certified eclipse glasses early from a reputable seller, and buy a spare pair or two, because glasses get lost, sat on and lent to friends. Check every pair for damage when it arrives, and check it again on the day. Make a pinhole viewer in advance and try it out on a sunny day, so you know how it works before you need it.

On the day, know the timing of totality for your location, and set a timer so nobody is caught looking up when the Sun returns. In Luxor, totality runs from about 1:02 to 1:08 p.m. local time. Agree the routine with everyone in your group before the eclipse begins: glasses on before looking up, look away before taking them off, and an adult in charge of calling time. A few minutes of preparation turns a risky day into a safe one.

Questions people ask

Are ordinary sunglasses safe for watching the eclipse?

No, never. Ordinary sunglasses, however dark, do not block the harmful light that damages the retina. Only ISO 12312-2 certified eclipse glasses are safe for looking at the Sun.

Can I look at the Sun during totality?

Yes, only during totality, when the Moon covers the Sun's bright disc completely. Put your glasses back on the moment the Sun begins to reappear. If you are unsure, keep your glasses on.

How do I know my eclipse glasses are genuine?

Genuine glasses are marked ISO 12312-2 and are sold by reputable sellers. Through them, the Sun looks like a small dim disc and everything else looks dark. Check for scratches, tears or holes before use.

Is a pinhole viewer safe?

Yes. A pinhole viewer projects an image of the Sun onto paper or the ground, so you never look at the Sun at all. Never look through the hole at the Sun.

What if my eclipse glasses are scratched?

Do not use them. Scratched, torn or punctured filters should be replaced. If you cannot replace them, use a pinhole viewer instead.

Can children watch the eclipse?

Yes, with adult supervision and properly fitting eclipse glasses, or with a pinhole viewer. Practise the safety routine at home before the day, and have an adult in charge of timing.
