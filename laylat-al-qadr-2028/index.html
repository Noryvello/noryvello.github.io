Laylat al-Qadr 2028

Laylat al-Qadr, often called the Night of Decree or the Night of Power, is the night in Ramadan when the Quran was first revealed. Surah al-Qadr, chapter 97 of the Quran, describes it as better than a thousand months (97:3). For Muslims, it is the most blessed night of the year, and worship offered on it carries a reward greater than worship across a lifetime of ordinary nights.

The exact date of Laylat al-Qadr is not known. The Prophet ﷺ taught Muslims to seek it in the odd nights of the last ten nights of Ramadan, and the most widely observed night is the 27th. This page gives the expected 2028 dates for all five odd nights, explains how the nights are counted, and shares the dua the Prophet ﷺ taught for this night.

All dates on this page are expected and depend on the sighting of the moon. Ramadan 1449 is expected to begin on Friday 28 January 2028, but the start of the month — and every date that follows from it — can shift by a day. Follow your local mosque or authority's announcement for the confirmed dates in your area.

Why the exact night is hidden

The exact date of Laylat al-Qadr was not fixed for the Muslim community. The Prophet ﷺ taught Muslims to seek it in the odd nights of the last ten nights of Ramadan, as narrated in Sahih al-Bukhari. The wisdom often given for this is that the search itself is part of the worship: a person who seeks the night across all five odd nights spends more time in prayer than one who is told a single date.

This is why the page lists all five odd nights rather than one date. Each of the five is a candidate, and each deserves to be spent in worship. A person who prays on all five has, by the teaching, caught the night — whichever of the five it was.

Why the 27th night is the most observed

Of the five odd nights, the 27th night is the most widely observed. Many mosques hold their largest gatherings on that night, and many Muslims who can only take one night off work choose it. But the 27th night is not certain to be Laylat al-Qadr. The exact night is hidden, and the Prophet ﷺ taught Muslims to seek it in the odd nights of the last ten, as narrated in Sahih al-Bukhari. The safest way to catch it is to worship on all five odd nights rather than on one alone.

This is also why the last ten nights as a whole matter so much. A person who prays, reads the Quran, and gives charity across the whole of the last ten nights has covered every odd night, and with it every candidate for Laylat al-Qadr.

What to do on these nights

There is no single fixed programme for Laylat al-Qadr. In general terms, Muslims use these nights for night prayer, reading the Quran, making dua, and giving charity. Those who are able may also observe i'tikaf, staying in the mosque for the last ten days of Ramadan in devotion.

  • Night prayer — praying through part or all of the night, alone or in congregation.
  • Reading the Quran — reciting and reflecting on the Quran, the book first revealed on this night.
  • Dua — asking Allah for forgiveness, guidance, and goodness in this life and the next.
  • Charity — giving to those in need, since charity given on this night carries the reward of the night itself.
  • I'tikaf — for those able, spending the last ten days in the mosque in worship and reflection.

The point is not to do everything, but to do something sincere. A short prayer offered with a present heart is worth more than a long night spent distracted.

The dua for Laylat al-Qadr

The Prophet ﷺ taught Aisha (RA) a short dua for this night. It asks Allah for pardon, and it is the dua most often repeated on Laylat al-Qadr:

اللَّهُمَّ إِنَّكَ عَفُوٌّ تُحِبُّ الْعَفْوَ فَاعْفُ عَنِّي

Transliteration: "Allahumma innaka 'afuwwun tuhibbul-'afwa fa'fu 'anni"

Meaning: "O Allah, You are Pardoning and You love pardon, so pardon me."

Source: Jami' at-Tirmidhi 3513 and Sunan Ibn Majah 3850. Some editions add the word "Karimun" after "'afuwwun" — either form is fine.

The dua is short enough to memorise in a few minutes, and many people repeat it through the night, between prayers and after them.

Preparing for the last ten nights

Because the last ten nights begin at sunset on Wednesday 16 February 2028, the time to prepare is before they arrive. Planning ahead — arranging time off where possible, sorting out family commitments, and deciding which nights to spend in the mosque — makes it far easier to give the nights their due once they begin.

Rest matters too. The nights are long, and the days of fasting continue alongside them. Sleeping during the day where possible, keeping meals light, and pacing yourself across all ten nights helps you stay steady rather than burning out after the first night or two.

It is also worth deciding in advance how you will give charity across the nights. Giving a little on each of the five odd nights means that, whichever night is Laylat al-Qadr, your charity falls on it. The same applies to prayer and reading: a steady plan across all five nights is better than a single heroic night.
