Android App Permissions Guide

Android asks before an app can use your location, camera, microphone or files, but the permission names alone do not say much. This guide explains 12 common permissions in plain words: what each one allows, when it is reasonable to grant, and what to check first.

Permissions are Android's way of asking before an app touches something personal. An app that wants your location, your camera or your contacts must ask, and you can say yes, say no, or say yes only while you are using the app. The system works well — but only if you know what each permission actually allows.

How Android permissions work

When an app first needs something sensitive, Android shows a prompt with the permission name and a choice: allow, deny, or — for some permissions — allow only while using the app. The prompt appears when the app needs the permission, not when you install it, so you can judge the request in context: a maps app asking for location while you search for directions is easy to understand.

Allow only while using the app is the middle choice, and it is the right choice for most permissions. The app gets what it needs while it is on screen, and loses it when you switch away. Allowing all the time is rarely needed — a navigation app guiding you in the background is one of the few cases — and it should make you pause.

Denying a permission does not break the app. The app simply works without that feature, and it may ask again later. If an app refuses to work at all because you denied an unrelated permission, that is a reason to question the app, not your decision.

Permissions can also be granted once. A one-time permission lets the app use something a single time, and Android asks again next time. It is a good choice when you are not sure: you get the feature now, and you decide again later with more information.

Permissions that deserve a second look

Some permissions deserve more thought than others. Location all the time is the first: an app that tracks you in the background builds a record of where you go, every day. Unless the app's whole purpose needs it — navigation while you drive is the usual case — choose while using the app instead.

SMS is the second. Text messages can carry login codes, bank alerts and private conversations, and an app that can read them can see all of that. Only your chosen messaging app and apps that verify you by text code have a real need for SMS access.

Contacts is the third, for a reason people forget: your contacts did not agree to share their details with the app. When you grant contacts access, you share other people's names and numbers. Grant it only when a feature you actually use needs it, such as inviting friends from inside a messaging app.

Body sensors deserve the same care. Heart rate, sleep and step data are among the most personal data a phone holds, and they belong only in health and fitness apps you chose for that purpose. A game or a shopping app has no business reading them.

Reviewing permissions later

Permissions are not a one-time decision. Android lets you review them all in one place: open Settings, then Apps, then Permissions, where you can see every permission and which apps hold it. It is worth a look every few months, because apps you installed long ago may hold permissions you would not grant today.

When you review, start with the permissions that matter most: location, microphone, SMS and contacts. For each app on the list, ask whether it still needs the permission. If you have not used the app in months, consider removing the permission — or removing the app.

Android also helps on its own: on recent versions, permissions are reset automatically for apps you have not used for a long time. That is a safety net, not a replacement for reviewing, because it only covers apps you have forgotten, not apps you use every day with permissions they no longer need.

Finally, remember that permissions are only one part of staying safe. A permission prompt tells you what an app wants; it does not tell you what the app does with it afterwards. Stick to apps from the official store, read the permissions at install time, and trust your judgement when a request feels wrong.

This guide is general safety information, not security advice for a specific device. Permission names and menus vary between Android versions and phone makers, so the wording on your phone may differ slightly from the wording used here.

Questions people ask

What happens if I deny a permission?

The app works without that feature, and it may ask again later. Denying a permission does not break the app, and an app that refuses to work over an unrelated permission deserves a second look.

Can I change a permission after granting it?

Yes. Open Settings, then Apps, then Permissions, where you can see every permission and change any app's access at any time.

Why does an app ask for a permission it does not seem to need?

Sometimes the app has a feature you have not used yet, and sometimes the request is simply unnecessary. Deny it and see whether anything you use stops working.

What does allow only while using the app mean?

The app can use the permission while it is on screen, and loses access when you switch away. It is the right choice for most permissions, including location.

Should I worry about permissions on apps I rarely use?

Yes, a little. Android resets permissions automatically for apps you have not used for a long time, but it is still worth reviewing them yourself every few months.
