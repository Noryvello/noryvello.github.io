Which is cheaper, and when?

Buying wins when you go to many events over several years, because you pay once and wear it every time, while the rental bill grows with every event. Renting wins when you go to only one or two events, because the total rental bill stays well below the purchase price.

With the starting example, buying costs 300.00 and renting costs 360.00, so buying wins by 60.00. The break-even point is 5 events: at a purchase price of 300 and a rental cost of 60 per event, wearing it more than 5 times makes buying cheaper, and wearing it fewer than 5 times makes renting cheaper.

The same logic works the other way round: for a fixed number of events, a higher rental cost per event makes buying more attractive, and a lower rental cost makes renting more attractive. Change the numbers above to find the break-even point for your own plans.

How the maths works

Total events = events per year × years of use.

Buying total = purchase price (paid once).

Renting total = rental cost per event × total events.

The cheaper option is the one with the lower total. The saving is the higher total minus the lower total.

Break-even events = purchase price ÷ rental cost per event. At that number of events both options cost the same.

Worked examples

Each example below uses the same formula as the calculator. Results are rounded to 2 decimal places.

Example 1 — Purchase price 300, renting at 60 per event, 2 events a year for 3 years.
Total events: 2 × 3 = 6.
Buying: 300.00.
Renting: 60 × 6 = 360.00.
Difference: 360.00 − 300.00 = 60.00. Buying is cheaper by 60.00, which is 16.7% less than renting (60 ÷ 360 × 100 = 16.7, rounded to 1 decimal place).

Example 2 — Purchase price 400, renting at 50 per event, 1 event a year for 2 years.
Total events: 1 × 2 = 2.
Buying: 400.00.
Renting: 50 × 2 = 100.00.
Difference: 400.00 − 100.00 = 300.00. Renting is cheaper by 300.00, which is 75% less than buying (300 ÷ 400 = 0.75).

Example 3 — Purchase price 240, renting at 40 per event, 2 events a year for 3 years.
Total events: 2 × 3 = 6.
Buying: 240.00.
Renting: 40 × 6 = 240.00.
Difference: 240.00 − 240.00 = 0. Both options cost exactly the same — this is the break-even point. Check: 240 ÷ 40 = 6 events, which matches the total events.

What to check before you decide

Think about fit and style over the whole period. A bought outfit only saves money if you actually wear it to every event — if your size changes or the style stops suiting you, the cost per wear rises.

Think about variety. Renting lets you wear something different to each event, while buying means wearing the same outfit again. That is a matter of taste, not cost, and this calculator only compares cost.

Check what the rental cost covers. Some rentals include cleaning and minor alterations, and some charge extra for them — ask before you book, and add any extras to the renting side if they apply.

If you might sell the outfit later, remember the calculator does not count that money. Subtract any resale value you expect from the purchase price before you enter it, to see the buying side at its real cost to you.

Questions people ask

When is buying a formal dress or suit cheaper than renting?

Buying is cheaper when the purchase price is lower than the total rental bill — that is, when the purchase price is less than the rental cost per event multiplied by the total number of events. Many events over several years usually favour buying, while one or two events usually favour renting. Enter your own numbers above to see which wins for you.

How many events make buying worth it?

Divide the purchase price by the rental cost per event. With the starting example — a purchase price of 300 and a rental cost of 60 per event — the break-even point is 5 events. Wear it more than 5 times and buying is cheaper; wear it fewer times and renting is cheaper.

Does the calculator include cleaning or alterations?

No. The buying side is the purchase price only, and the renting side is the rental fees only. Cleaning, alterations, storage, deposits and damage charges are not included, so add them to the relevant side yourself if they apply.

What if I sell the outfit later?

Selling it would lower the real cost of buying, but this calculator does not guess a resale value, because it depends on the outfit, its condition and who buys it. If you have a resale value in mind, subtract it from the purchase price before you enter it above.
