Business Insurance Guide

Business insurance protects a small business against the costs of accidents, claims, damage and interruptions that could otherwise close it down. This guide explains the main covers, what each one protects, and how to work out what your business needs. It is general information, not financial advice, and needs differ by business and place — ask a licensed adviser before you buy.

What business insurance is and who needs this guide

Business insurance is a set of policies that protect a business against the costs of things going wrong: a customer injured on your premises, a fire in your workshop, a client who says your advice cost them money, or a week when you cannot trade at all. For a large company, these costs are painful. For a small business, they can be fatal — one serious claim without insurance can end a business that took years to build.

This guide is for the owner of a small business: a shop, a cafe, a workshop, a home business, a freelance practice, or a small team selling services. It explains the main covers in plain words, shows how to work out what your business needs, and sets out the questions to ask an insurer. It quotes no prices and recommends no insurer, because the right cover depends on what your business does, where it works, and what it owns.

Many small business owners put off insurance because it feels complicated or expensive. This guide makes it simpler by breaking the subject into its parts: the covers that protect other people, the covers that protect your property, and the covers that protect your income. Once you understand the parts, choosing becomes a practical exercise, not a mystery.

How business insurance works, step by step

Start by listing what your business could lose. Walk through your business in your mind: the premises, the equipment, the stock, the vehicles, the computers, and the work you do for customers. Then list the ways each of these could be harmed: fire, flood, theft, breakage, and the mistakes that lead to a claim from a customer or a member of the public. This list is the basis of your insurance, because insurance is about protecting what you cannot afford to lose.

Next, match the risks to the covers. Public liability cover pays when a member of the public is injured or their property is damaged because of your business — a customer who slips in your shop, for example. Property cover pays for damage to your premises, equipment and stock. Professional indemnity cover pays when a client says your advice or your service caused them a loss — it matters most for businesses that advise, design or consult. Business interruption cover pays when you cannot trade because of an insured event, such as a fire, and helps with the lost income while you recover.

Then work out how much cover you need for each part. For property, that means the cost of replacing what you own, not what you paid for it years ago. For liability, it means thinking about the worst realistic claim your business could face, not the most likely one. For interruption, it means how long your business could survive without income. These are not easy numbers to work out, and this is where a licensed adviser earns their fee: they have seen what claims actually cost, and they can help you set limits that protect you without paying for cover you do not need.

Finally, compare policies and buy. Get the full wording for two or three policies, compare the cover, the exclusions, the deductible and the limit for each, and read the renewal and cancellation rules. Buy the cover that fits your list of risks, keep the policy wording where you can find it, and note the renewal date. Then review the cover once a year, or whenever your business changes — a new premises, new equipment, new staff, or a new service all change what you need.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

The cost of business insurance varies so much by business type, size, location and cover that no guide can give a useful figure, and this one does not try. What can be said is that the cost of being uninsured is almost always higher than the cost of cover: a single liability claim can cost many times a year's premium. The right question is not whether you can afford insurance, but whether you can afford to be without it.

Working out what you need takes an afternoon. You will need a list of your premises, equipment, stock and vehicles, a note of what your business does day to day, the number of people you employ, and any contracts that require you to hold insurance — many clients and landlords do. With that information in hand, a conversation with a licensed adviser takes an hour or two, and comparing two or three policies takes another hour. It is a small investment of time for protection that lasts a year.

Before you start, check whether any insurance is required by law or by contract in your place and your line of work. Some covers are legally required for certain businesses, and some clients will not work with a business that cannot show proof of cover. Check the local rules and your contracts first, so that the cover you must have is the starting point of your list, not an afterthought.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The first mistake is assuming a home policy covers a home business. It usually does not. A home insurance policy covers your home and your personal belongings, not your business equipment, your business stock, or a client who is injured during a business visit to your home. The fix is to tell your insurer that you run a business from home and to buy the business cover you need — it is often a small addition to the policy, and without it you may have no cover at all for the business.

The second mistake is under-insuring: insuring your property for less than it would cost to replace. If your equipment would cost twice as much to replace as the amount you insured it for, the insurer may only pay part of a claim, even a small one. The fix is to value your property at replacement cost, update the values when you buy new equipment, and review them every year.

The third mistake is forgetting the covers that are not about things. Many small businesses insure their property carefully and forget liability altogether, yet a liability claim is often the costliest event a small business faces. The fix is to think about people as well as property: who could be harmed by your business, and what would that cost? Liability cover is usually the first cover a small business needs, not the last.

The fourth mistake is buying a policy and never reviewing it. A business that has grown — new staff, new premises, new services — but kept the same policy may be under-insured or missing a cover entirely. The fix is a yearly review, and a review whenever the business changes. A ten-minute conversation with your adviser once a year keeps the cover in step with the business.

A practical checklist you can follow

Use this checklist to work out what cover your small business needs, and keep it with your policy.

  • List what your business owns: premises, equipment, stock, vehicles, computers.
  • List the ways each of these could be harmed: fire, flood, theft, breakage, accident.
  • List the people your business could harm: customers, visitors, clients, members of the public.
  • Check whether any cover is required by law or by a client or landlord contract.
  • Match the risks to the covers: public liability, property, professional indemnity, business interruption.
  • Value your property at replacement cost, not at what you paid for it.
  • If you run the business from home, check whether your home policy covers the business — it usually does not.
  • Compare two or three policies on cover, exclusions, deductible and limit, not on price alone.
  • Ask a licensed adviser about anything you do not understand, and keep their answers.
  • Review the cover once a year, and whenever the business changes.

Tips from real practice

Keep an inventory of what you own. A simple list of your equipment and stock, with the date bought and the replacement cost, makes buying cover easier and makes claiming far easier. Photograph the main items and keep the photos with the list. After a fire or a theft, the businesses that recover fastest are the ones that can show the insurer exactly what they lost.

Read the exclusions before you buy, not after a claim. Every policy excludes some things, and the exclusions decide whether a claim is paid. If your business does work that a policy excludes, that policy is the wrong one for you, whatever its price. Ask the adviser to point out the exclusions that matter most for your kind of business.

Bundle covers where it makes sense, but check the bundle. Buying several covers from one insurer in one policy can be simpler and cheaper than buying them separately, but only if the bundle covers everything on your list. A bundle that leaves out one cover you need is not a bargain, whatever it saves.

Finally, think of insurance as part of running the business, not as an extra. The businesses that survive a bad year are the ones that planned for it, and insurance is the simplest form of planning there is. A small business cannot afford to lose its premises, its equipment or its reputation — and insurance is how it makes sure it never has to.

Questions people ask

What insurance does a small business need first?

Most small businesses need public liability cover first, because a claim from an injured customer or member of the public can be the costliest event a small business faces. Property cover for premises, equipment and stock usually comes next. The exact order depends on what your business does, so ask a licensed adviser about your situation.

What is public liability insurance?

Public liability insurance pays when a member of the public is injured or their property is damaged because of your business — for example, a customer who slips in your shop or a visitor whose car is damaged by falling goods. It covers the compensation and the legal costs of the claim, up to the policy limit.

Does a home business need insurance?

Yes. A home insurance policy usually covers your home and personal belongings, not your business equipment, stock, or a client injured during a business visit. Tell your insurer that you run a business from home and buy the business cover you need, or you may find you have no cover at all for the business.

What is professional indemnity cover?

Professional indemnity cover pays when a client says your advice or your service caused them a financial loss — for example, a mistake in a design, an error in a report, or advice that turned out to be wrong. It matters most for businesses that advise, design, consult or provide professional services.

How much cover is enough?

Enough cover means enough to survive the worst realistic loss, not just the most likely one. For property, insure at replacement cost. For liability, think about the largest claim your business could realistically face. A licensed adviser can help you set limits that protect you without paying for cover you do not need.

What should I ask an insurer before buying?

Ask what is covered and what is excluded, what the deductible and the limit are, how to make a claim and how long it takes, whether the policy renews automatically, and how you can cancel it. Tell the insurer exactly what your business does, so the cover matches the real risks.
