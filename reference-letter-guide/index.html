How to Write a Reference Letter

A good reference letter is specific: it says how you know the person, what they are good at, and one real example that shows it. This guide explains the structure, includes a fill-in builder that drafts the letter from your words, and ends with a tone checklist.

Being asked to write a reference letter is a compliment, and it is also a responsibility. The reader — an employer, a school, an admissions tutor — wants to know one thing: what is this person really like to work with? A strong letter answers that question with specifics rather than praise. It says how you know the person, what they are good at, and one real example that proves it. Everything else is decoration.

Only write a reference for someone you know well enough to recommend honestly. Never invent achievements or examples. If you cannot recommend the person in good conscience, it is kinder to decline.

What a reference letter does

A reference letter is a personal recommendation, written by someone who has seen the person at work. It is not a summary of their CV, and it is not a list of adjectives. Its job is to give the reader confidence: this person can do what they claim, and here is the evidence from someone who watched them do it. The best letters are short, specific and honest.

Before you write, ask the person what the letter is for and when it is needed. A letter for a job should stress the qualities that matter in that job. A letter for a course should stress learning, curiosity and reliability. Knowing the purpose lets you choose the right example and the right strengths, instead of writing a letter that could be about anyone.

Structure: opening, body, close

A reference letter follows a simple structure. The opening says who you are, how you know the person, and for how long. One or two sentences is enough. The reader needs to know that you are in a position to judge, and nothing more at this stage.

The body is the heart of the letter. Name two or three strengths, and support them with one real example. The example is what makes the letter believable. Anyone can write that a person is reliable. A letter that describes the time they stayed late to fix a problem before a deadline, and what happened as a result, proves it. One good example is worth more than a paragraph of praise.

The close is a clear recommendation. Say plainly that you recommend the person, and offer to discuss their work further if the reader wishes. Then sign off with your name and role. Keep the whole letter to about a page. A short, specific letter is read carefully; a long, vague one is skimmed.

When the letter is for a specific role

A letter written for a specific job or course is stronger than a general one, because it can match the person's strengths to what the reader is looking for. Ask the person for the job description or the course details, and read them before you write. Then choose the strengths and the example that fit best. If the role needs someone calm under pressure, the example should show them staying calm under pressure. If the course needs independent study, the example should show them working on their own initiative.

This does not mean exaggerating or inventing a match that is not there. It means choosing, from the true things you know about the person, the ones that matter most to this reader. A letter that speaks directly to the role shows the reader that you took the request seriously, and it gives the person the best possible chance.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is writing a letter that could be about anyone. A string of adjectives — hardworking, dedicated, excellent — tells the reader nothing, because every letter says the same things. Specifics are what set a letter apart: a named strength, a real example, a result that can be pictured.

The second mistake is writing too much. A reference letter is not a life story. One page, with a clear opening, a body built around one example, and a plain recommendation, is enough. If the letter runs long, cut the praise and keep the evidence.

The third mistake is agreeing to write a letter for someone you cannot honestly recommend. A lukewarm letter helps nobody, and a dishonest one can cause real harm. If you do not know the person well enough, or you cannot recommend them in good conscience, decline politely and promptly. Suggesting someone else who knows them better is a kind and honest alternative.

Questions people ask

How long should a reference letter be?

About a page is right. A short, specific letter is read carefully, while a long, vague one is skimmed. Keep the opening brief, build the body around one real example, and close with a clear recommendation.

What if I barely know the person?

Decline politely and promptly. A reference from someone who barely knows the person is weak and unhelpful. Suggesting someone else who knows them better is a kind and honest alternative.

Should I mention weaknesses in a reference letter?

A reference letter is a recommendation, so its focus is on strengths. If you have serious doubts, it is better to decline than to write a letter that quietly undermines the person.

Can I use a template for a reference letter?

A template can help with the structure, but the content must be your own. A letter built from someone else's words will read as generic. Use the structure, and fill it with your own observations and examples.

Who should write a reference letter?

Someone who has seen the person at work and can speak honestly about them: a manager, a teacher, a colleague or a supervisor. The best referee is the person who knows the relevant work best, not the most senior person available.
