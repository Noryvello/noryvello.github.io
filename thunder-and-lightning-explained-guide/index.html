Thunder and Lightning Explained

Lightning is a giant spark that jumps when the electrical charge inside a storm cloud grows too large, and thunder is the sound of the air around the lightning heating and expanding faster than sound. We see the flash first because light travels almost instantly, while sound takes about three seconds to cover a kilometre.

Staying safe in a thunderstorm

Before the science, the safety rules, because they matter more than anything else in this guide. When thunder roars, go indoors. A solid building is the safest place in a thunderstorm. Stay inside until at least thirty minutes after the last thunder you hear, because lightning can strike after the rain has passed.

Outdoors, avoid water, open fields, hilltops, isolated tall trees and metal fences. If you are caught in the open with no shelter, crouch low with your feet together, head tucked, and as little of your body touching the ground as you can manage. A car with a hard metal roof is safer than open ground: stay inside it and avoid touching metal parts. Rubber tyres do not protect you; the metal shell does.

Indoors, stay away from windows, and avoid plumbing and wired electrical equipment during the storm, because lightning can travel through pipes and wires. Unplug sensitive electronics if you can do so safely before the storm arrives. If someone is struck by lightning, they do not carry a charge and are safe to touch: call for emergency help at once.

How lightning forms

Lightning begins inside a tall storm cloud, where strong updrafts and downdrafts throw water droplets and ice crystals around. As the ice crystals collide, they pick up electric charge: the smaller crystals tend to become positively charged and rise to the top of the cloud, while the larger, heavier particles become negatively charged and sink to the bottom.

The base of the cloud becomes strongly negative, and the ground below it becomes positively charged in response, as if the cloud were pulling the opposite charge up out of the Earth. The difference in charge keeps growing until the air between the cloud and the ground can no longer hold it back. Air is normally a poor conductor of electricity, but under enough electrical pressure it breaks down and lets a channel of current through.

That channel is lightning. A jagged path of ionised air reaches down from the cloud, a matching path reaches up from the ground, and when they meet, a huge current flows along the joined channel. The whole flash takes a fraction of a second, though it often flickers as several strokes follow the same channel one after another.

Most lightning never reaches the ground at all. The majority of flashes happen inside a single cloud, or between two clouds, and we see them as a broad flicker that lights up the whole sky. That is sheet lightning, and it is the most common kind. The forked bolt that reaches the ground is the dramatic one, but it is not the usual one.

Why thunder follows the flash

Lightning heats the air around its channel to an extreme temperature, far hotter than the surface of the Sun, in a fraction of a second. The air expands violently, faster than the speed of sound, and that sudden expansion makes a shock wave. The shock wave is what we hear as thunder.

We see the flash first because light travels almost instantly, while sound is comparatively slow. Sound covers about one kilometre in three seconds, so the gap between the flash and the thunder tells you how far away the lightning was. Count the seconds, divide by three, and you have the distance in kilometres. The estimator above does the arithmetic for you.

Thunder sounds different depending on the distance. A nearby strike makes a sharp crack, because the shock wave arrives all at once. A distant storm makes a low rumble, because the sound is stretched out as it travels, and because the lightning channel is long and different parts of it are at different distances from you. Thunder that seems to roll on and on is the sound of a long channel arriving piece by piece.

Lightning can also be seen without any thunder at all. Distant storms on the horizon can light up the sky while their thunder is too far away to hear, which is sometimes called heat lightning. It is not a different kind of lightning: it is ordinary lightning too far away for its sound to reach you.

Why lightning picks where it strikes

Lightning does not strike at random. It follows the path of least resistance, which usually means the tallest object nearby, because a tall object shortens the gap the lightning has to cross. That is why lone trees, hilltops, masts and towers are struck more often than the flat ground around them, and why standing under a lone tree in a storm is dangerous.

Water is also a risk, because it conducts electricity well and because a person in water is the tallest thing on a flat surface. Golf courses, open fields and beaches are dangerous for the same reason: a person standing in the open is the highest point around. Inside a building, lightning usually finds a path through wiring or plumbing, which is why you should stay away from both during a storm.

Lightning can strike more than once in the same place. The old saying that it never strikes twice is not true: tall buildings and towers are struck many times, and some are fitted with lightning rods precisely because strikes are expected. A lightning rod gives the strike a safe path to the ground, away from the building itself.

These safety rules are general guidance. In a severe storm, follow the advice of your local weather service or emergency authority, and never shelter under a lone tree, near water, or in an open field.

Questions people ask

What causes lightning?

Lightning is a giant spark. Inside a storm cloud, colliding ice crystals separate electric charge until the difference grows so large that the air breaks down and a channel of current jumps, either within the cloud or down to the ground.

Why do we hear thunder after the lightning?

Lightning heats the air around it extremely fast, and the air expands faster than sound, making a shock wave we hear as thunder. Light reaches us almost instantly, but sound takes about three seconds per kilometre, so the thunder arrives later.

How far away is lightning if thunder comes 9 seconds after the flash?

About 3 kilometres away. Sound travels about 1 kilometre in 3 seconds, so divide the seconds by 3: 9 divided by 3 is 3.

Is it safe to shelter under a tree in a thunderstorm?

No. A lone tree is the tallest thing around and is more likely to be struck, and the current can travel through the tree and the ground. Go indoors, or crouch low in the open if there is no shelter.

Does lightning ever strike the same place twice?

Yes. Tall buildings and towers are struck many times, which is why some are fitted with lightning rods. The saying that lightning never strikes twice is not true.
