Satellite Tracking Information Portal

Satellite tracking is the art of knowing where a satellite is and when it will pass over your head. Satellites follow predictable paths called orbits, which is why their passes can be predicted days ahead. This guide explains how tracking works, how to see a satellite with your own eyes, and where to check positions through the official trackers.

How to use this guide

This guide explains satellite tracking in plain words: how we know where thousands of satellites are, how to predict when one will pass over your home, and how to spot one in the night sky. You do not need a telescope, and you do not need any background in physics. Most of what you need is a clear sky and the right time.

How satellite tracking works

Satellites are tracked by watching them, listening to them and doing the mathematics of their orbits. Ground stations with radar and telescopes measure where a satellite is, and the satellite's own radio signals tell the ground how healthy it is. Those measurements are combined into a set of numbers that describe the orbit, and the numbers are updated regularly as the orbit slowly changes.

The most widely used description of an orbit is a pair of lines of numbers called a two-line element set, known as a TLE. A TLE describes the shape of the orbit, how high it is, how tilted it is and where the satellite is along it. With a current TLE and a little mathematics, anyone can work out where a satellite will be at any moment in the next few days.

The tracking itself is done by space agencies and by the organisations that operate the satellites. The United States Space Force tracks tens of thousands of objects in orbit, including working satellites, dead satellites and pieces of debris, and publishes orbit data for most of them. Space agencies track their own spacecraft with their own ground stations, spread around the world so that a satellite is almost always in touch with one of them.

Orbits: the paths satellites follow

An orbit is the path a satellite takes as it falls around the Earth, held by gravity but moving too fast to fall down. Low Earth orbit, up to about 2,000 kilometres high, is where the International Space Station flies, about 400 kilometres up, circling the Earth roughly every ninety minutes. Most Earth observation satellites fly here too, because being close gives them a clear view.

Medium Earth orbit sits between about 2,000 and 36,000 kilometres up, and it is home to the navigation satellites that run GPS and similar systems, at about 20,000 kilometres. From that height, a small group of satellites can cover the whole Earth, which is why navigation works anywhere on the planet with a clear view of the sky.

Geostationary orbit is a special ring about 36,000 kilometres above the equator, where a satellite orbits at exactly the same speed as the Earth spins, so it appears to hang still in the sky. Television and weather satellites use this orbit, because a dish on the ground can point at them once and never move. Other satellites use polar orbits, passing over the north and south poles, which lets them see the whole Earth as the planet turns beneath them.

Seeing a satellite with your own eyes

You can see many satellites with no equipment at all, and the best time is just after sunset or just before sunrise. At those times the ground where you stand is dark, but a satellite hundreds of kilometres up is still catching sunlight, so it shines against the dark sky. A satellite looks like a steady, bright star moving in a straight line across the sky, taking a few minutes to cross from one horizon to the other.

The International Space Station is the brightest and easiest satellite to see, and on a good pass it outshines every star in the sky. To see it, you need to know when it will pass over your home, which you can find from an official tracker by entering your location. Then go outside a few minutes early, find a spot with a clear view of the horizon, and look in the direction the tracker tells you.

A few tips make the difference between seeing a satellite and missing it. Let your eyes adjust to the dark for a few minutes, and do not look at a bright phone screen while you wait. Watch for a light that moves steadily and does not blink: planes blink and change direction, while satellites glide in a straight line at a steady speed. If the light suddenly fades and vanishes, the satellite has entered the Earth's shadow, which is normal and expected.

The official trackers to use

Official trackers are the only places that show real satellite positions. Space agencies publish tracking pages for their own spacecraft, and the organisations that watch the sky publish predictions for thousands of satellites. A good tracker lets you enter your town or your coordinates and shows you a list of passes for the next few nights, with the time, the direction and how bright the satellite will be.

When you use a tracker, check two things: that it says where its orbit data comes from, and that the data is fresh. Orbit predictions go stale within days, because the thin air at low altitudes slowly drags satellites down, so a tracker using week-old data will show passes at the wrong times. A tracker that updates its data daily is the one to trust.

Be careful with tracker apps and websites that do not say where their data comes from. Some show a pretty map with a satellite icon moving across it, but the icon is drawn from old data, or from no data at all. If a tracker cannot tell you the source of its orbit data, treat its predictions as entertainment, not information, and check them against an official tracker before you go outside.

The International Space Station

The International Space Station, known as the ISS, is the largest object humans have ever put in orbit, about the size of a football field. It has been continuously inhabited since November 2000, which means there have been people living in space without a break for over twenty-five years. Crews of typically six or seven astronauts and cosmonauts live and work aboard it, coming and going in rotation.

The station orbits about 400 kilometres above the Earth, completing one circuit roughly every ninety minutes, which means its crew sees sixteen sunrises and sixteen sunsets every day. It is a laboratory, and the experiments aboard it study how the human body changes in weightlessness, how materials behave without gravity, and how plants grow in space. The results feed into medicine, materials science and the planning of future missions.

The ISS is also the most visible satellite in the sky, and the best first satellite to spot. Because it is so large and so low, it reflects a great deal of sunlight, and on a good pass it is unmistakable: a bright, steady light crossing the sky in about five minutes. Many people who have never looked for a satellite before find the station on their first try, using nothing more than a prediction from an official tracker.

Why satellites look like moving stars

Satellites shine by reflected sunlight, not by their own light. A satellite has no lamp; what you see is sunlight bouncing off its body and its solar panels, like moonlight, which is also reflected sunlight. That is why satellites are visible just after sunset and just before sunrise, when the Sun is below your horizon but still shining on the satellite high above you.

They look like stars because they are small and far away, so your eye sees them as points of light rather than shapes. The difference is the movement: stars stay fixed in their patterns all night, while a satellite crosses the sky in minutes. That steady glide, with no blinking and no change of direction, is the giveaway that the moving light is a satellite and not a plane.

A satellite can also flare, suddenly becoming much brighter for a few seconds, when its flat panels catch the Sun at just the right angle and throw a beam of reflected light towards you. The old Iridium satellites were famous for these flares, which could be brighter than any star. Modern satellites flare less often, but a sudden brightening is still one of the most striking things you can see in the night sky.

Common mistakes when tracking satellites

The most common mistake is thinking that a bright light in the sky must be a satellite. Planes are the usual confusion: they are lower, slower-looking and they blink, while satellites glide steadily. Bright planets are another confusion: Venus and Jupiter can be very bright, but they do not move across the sky in minutes. If the light blinks or stays in one place, it is not a satellite.

The second mistake is trusting a random tracker app. A tracker that does not say where its orbit data comes from, or that uses data a week old, will send you outside at the wrong time to look in the wrong direction. Always check a prediction against an official tracker, especially if you are planning to show a satellite to someone else and do not want to keep them waiting for nothing.

The third mistake is expecting a satellite to look like a picture. In photographs, satellites are detailed machines with panels and antennas, but to your eye they are points of light, and that is all they will ever be without a telescope. The wonder of seeing one is not in the detail, but in the thought: that small moving light is a machine built by people, flying hundreds of kilometres above your head at thousands of kilometres per hour.

Questions people ask

How do I track a satellite?

Use an official tracker and enter your location, and it will show you when satellites will pass over your home. Trackers work from orbit data that describes each satellite's path, and they predict passes days ahead. Check that the tracker says where its data comes from and that the data is fresh, because orbit predictions go stale within days.

Can I see a satellite with my eyes?

Yes, many satellites are visible to the naked eye, and the International Space Station is the brightest and easiest to spot. The best time is just after sunset or just before sunrise, when the ground is dark but the satellite is still in sunlight. It looks like a steady, bright star moving in a straight line across the sky, taking a few minutes to cross from one horizon to the other.

What is a satellite pass?

A pass is the period when a satellite is visible above your horizon, usually lasting a few minutes. The tracker tells you when the pass starts, how high the satellite will climb in the sky, and which direction it will travel. A pass that climbs high overhead is the easiest to see, while a low pass near the horizon can be hidden by trees and buildings.

Where do I check satellite positions?

Check an official tracker run by a space agency or by an organisation that watches the sky. A good tracker lets you enter your location and shows passes with times, directions and brightness. Avoid trackers that do not say where their orbit data comes from, because their predictions may be drawn from old data or from nothing at all.

What is the ISS?

The ISS is the International Space Station, the largest object humans have ever put in orbit, about the size of a football field. It has been continuously inhabited since November 2000, and crews of typically six or seven people live and work aboard it. It orbits about 400 kilometres above the Earth, circling the planet roughly every ninety minutes, and it is the brightest satellite in the night sky.

Why do satellites look like moving stars?

Satellites shine by reflected sunlight, and they are small and far away, so your eye sees them as points of light. They move because they are travelling fast around the Earth, crossing the sky in a few minutes. Unlike planes, they do not blink, and unlike stars, they do not stay still. A satellite that suddenly fades has entered the Earth's shadow, which is normal.
