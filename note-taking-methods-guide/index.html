Note-Taking Methods Guide

This guide compares four note-taking methods — Cornell, outline, mind-map and charting — and shows you the structure, steps and best uses of each. Pick a method to see its printable structure, then print it out and try it on your next set of notes.

No method is best for everyone. The method that works is the one you will keep using, so try each one for a week before you settle, and adapt the structure to fit your subject.

The four methods at a glance

Each method organises the same information differently, and the difference matters. Cornell notes split the page into notes, cues and a summary, which makes them easy to review. Outline notes use numbered points and indents, which suit material that already has a clear order. Mind-map notes spread out from a central topic, which suits brainstorming and revision overviews. Charting notes use a table, which suits comparing things side by side.

If you are unsure where to start, try Cornell notes first. The cue column and summary strip give you a built-in review step, and the structure works for most subjects without much adjustment.

Cornell notes

Cornell notes were designed for lectures, and their strength is what happens after the lesson. During the lesson you write notes in the wide right column, in short phrases rather than full sentences. After the lesson you fill in the narrow left column with questions or key words that cue each part, and you write a short summary at the bottom of the page.

The review is what makes the method work. Cover the right column, read a cue, and try to recall the notes beside it before you peek. That turns every page of notes into a self-test, and it takes only a few minutes. The summary strip forces you to decide what the lesson was really about, which is a useful check on whether you understood it.

Outline notes

Outline notes are the simplest method to learn, and the most natural when the material already has a clear structure. Each main idea becomes a numbered point, and supporting details, examples and definitions go underneath it, indented. The shape of the page shows the shape of the topic at a glance, which makes outlines easy to review quickly.

The risk with outlines is writing too much. Keep each line short, and use your own words rather than copying sentences word for word. If a point needs more than two or three indented lines, it is probably two points, and splitting it will make the notes clearer.

Mind-map notes

Mind-map notes start with the main topic in the centre of a blank page, with branches reaching out to sub-topics and smaller branches for details. They suit subjects where ideas connect in many directions rather than in a straight line, and they are especially useful for revision overviews, when you want to see a whole topic on one page.

Keep each branch to one or two words, and use colour or small symbols to mark the branches that matter most. Mind-maps are harder to use during a fast lecture, because you cannot predict the branches in advance, so they work best as a second pass: take quick notes first, then turn them into a mind-map when you review.

Charting notes

Charting notes use a table, with one column for each category you want to compare and one row for each item. They are the best method when the point of the material is comparison: dates against events, people against their ideas, or types of anything against their features. Reading down a column compares items; reading across a row shows one item in full.

The trick is choosing the categories before you start. If you do not know the categories yet, leave the first column blank and add them as the lesson goes on. Charting works badly for material with no natural categories, so if you find yourself forcing the table, switch to outlines instead.

Making notes you will actually use

The best notes are the ones you go back to, and that depends less on the method than on two habits. The first is writing in your own words: copying sentences feels like work, but it lets your brain switch off, while putting an idea in your own words forces you to understand it. The second is reviewing within a day, while the lesson is still fresh enough to fill in the gaps.

Leave white space on every page. Notes packed edge to edge are hard to read and leave nowhere to add a thought later. A blank line between ideas, a margin for questions, and a short summary at the end of each set of notes will do more for your revision than any fancy system.

Questions people ask

Should I take notes by hand or on a screen?

Both can work, and the best choice is the one you will keep using. Handwriting slows you down, which often means you write in your own words rather than copying. Typing is faster and easier to search. If you type, resist the urge to copy everything word for word.

How do I keep up with a fast speaker?

Do not try to write everything down. Listen for main ideas, and use short phrases and abbreviations for the rest. Leave a gap where you missed something, and fill it in from the textbook or a classmate after the lesson.

What is the Cornell method?

Cornell notes split the page into three parts: a wide right column for notes, a narrow left column for cues and questions, and a summary strip at the bottom. After the lesson you test yourself by covering the notes and using the cues to recall them.

Can I mix methods?

Yes. Many people take quick outline notes during a lesson and then turn them into a mind-map or a Cornell page when they review. Mixing methods is fine as long as the result is a set of notes you can read and use later.

How should I review my notes?

Review within a day of taking them, while you still remember the lesson well enough to fill gaps. Then review again a few days later, using the cues or headings to test yourself instead of just rereading.
