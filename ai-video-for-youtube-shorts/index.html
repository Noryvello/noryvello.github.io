How to Make YouTube Shorts with AI Video Tools

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

YouTube Shorts are vertical videos of up to 60 seconds, and AI video tools are a good way to make the clips for them. This guide covers the vertical format, hook-first structure, clip planning for 10 to 60 seconds, captions, and music, with honest notes on what AI video can and cannot do for a Short.

Shorts are a natural fit for AI video. The format is short, the clips are short, and a single idea can carry the whole video. A ten-second clip of a storm rolling over a mountain, a thirty-second tour of a fantasy castle, or a forty-five-second explanation with visuals: all of these are within reach of a beginner with an AI video tool and a free editing app.

But the format is also unforgiving. Viewers decide in the first second or two whether to keep watching, and they can swipe away just as fast. A Short made from pretty AI clips with no hook, no pacing, and no point will not hold anyone. The tools make the footage easy; the craft is in the structure, the editing, and the idea.

Honest limit: AI video needs human editing to make good Shorts. Generated clips are raw material, not finished videos. Faces and hands distort, text on screen scrambles, and clips vary in quality, so review every clip, cut the weak parts, and add your hook, captions, and music in editing.

The vertical format

Shorts are vertical videos, taller than they are wide, designed to fill a phone screen held upright. That shape changes how you plan every shot. A subject that looks good in a wide landscape frame can look lost in a vertical one, and a scene with important detail on the left and right edges will lose that detail when cropped.

Generate in a vertical aspect ratio if your tool offers one. Most video generators let you choose the shape of the clip, and picking vertical from the start means the model composes for the tall frame: subjects centred, important detail in the middle, and empty space used well. A clip generated vertical looks deliberate; a wide clip cropped to vertical often looks like an accident.

If your tool only generates wide clips, crop in editing. Keep the subject centred, and check the crop on a phone screen before publishing, because a crop that looks fine on a computer can cut off a face or a hand on a phone. Some editing tools can track a subject and keep it centred automatically, which helps with moving shots.

Think about the safe areas too. The platform places buttons, titles, and captions over parts of the screen, usually at the bottom and the right edge. Keep important visual detail away from those areas, and place your own captions where they will not clash with the platform's interface. A quick test on your own phone before publishing catches most of these problems.

Hook-first structure

The first two seconds of a Short decide whether anyone watches the rest. Viewers scroll quickly, and a slow opening, a logo, a long introduction, or a quiet establishing shot, loses them before the video starts. The hook must come first.

A hook is a promise: this video will show you something worth your time. It can be a striking image, a surprising fact, a question, or a bold claim. "This castle does not exist" over a shot of a floating fortress is a hook. "Watch what happens when the tide turns" over a shot of a beach is a hook. The hook does not need to be loud; it needs to be interesting.

After the hook, deliver on it quickly. If the hook promises a reveal, show the reveal within a few seconds. If it asks a question, answer it before the viewer loses patience. Shorts reward videos that respect the viewer's time: every second should either show something new or move the idea forward.

End with a clear finish, not a fade into nothing. A Short can end with a final image, a short line of text, or a question that invites a comment. An ending that feels deliberate makes the video feel complete, and it makes the viewer more likely to watch your next one.

Clip planning for 10 to 60 seconds

Planning a Short means planning in shots. A 10-second Short might be a single striking clip with a caption. A 30-second Short might be four or five shots. A 60-second Short might be eight to ten shots. The exact count depends on the idea, but the principle is the same: plan the shots before you generate them.

Start by writing the idea in one sentence. "A tour of a fantasy castle at sunset" is a plan. "A castle" is not. From the sentence, list the shots you need: an establishing wide shot, a closer shot of the gate, a shot of the towers, a shot of the courtyard, and a final wide shot. Each shot is one generation, and each one should show something the others do not.

Match the shot length to the pace. Fast, energetic Shorts use short shots of two to three seconds each. Calm, beautiful Shorts can hold a shot for four or five seconds. A shot that stays on screen too long feels slow; a shot that flashes past too fast feels confusing. Watch your draft on a phone and notice where your attention wanders: that is where a shot is too long.

Plan for more clips than you need. If your Short needs six shots, generate eight or ten, and pick the best. Having spares means you can replace a clip that has an artefact or does not match the others, instead of publishing a weak shot because you have no alternative.

Captions that work on a phone

Many people watch Shorts without sound, so captions are not optional. They carry the words for viewers who cannot hear the audio, and they reinforce the words for viewers who can. Good captions are short, large, and well timed.

Keep each caption to a few words at a time, and time them to the voiceover or the beat of the music. A caption that shows a whole sentence at once is hard to read on a phone. Breaking the same sentence into short phrases, each shown for a second or two, is much easier to follow.

Use a large, bold font with a clear contrast against the video. White text with a dark outline works on most backgrounds. Place captions in the middle of the screen, away from the platform's buttons and titles. Test the captions on a phone, not just on a computer, because text that looks fine on a large screen can be unreadable on a small one.

Add captions in editing, not in the video generator. Text in generated video usually comes out garbled, and even when it looks right, you cannot control the wording, the size, or the timing. An editing tool gives you full control, and many can generate captions automatically from a voiceover, though you should always check the wording before publishing.

Music and sound

Music sets the mood of a Short faster than any visual. A calm piano track makes a landscape feel peaceful. An upbeat track makes a product showcase feel energetic. The right music can carry a Short; the wrong music can fight it.

Choose music that matches the pace of your editing. Fast cuts suit upbeat tracks. Slow, lingering shots suit calm ones. If the music and the visuals disagree, the viewer feels it even if they cannot name it. A simple test is to watch the Short with the sound off, then with the sound on: the music should make the video better, not just louder.

Use music you have the right to use. Royalty-free libraries and the audio library provided by the platform are safe choices. Commercial songs without permission can get your video muted, removed, or claimed, and they can cause problems for your channel. If you are unsure about a track, do not use it.

Sound effects can help too. A soft whoosh on a transition, the sound of waves under a beach scene, or a gentle chime on a reveal can add polish. Use them sparingly: one or two well-placed effects beat a constant layer of noise. Keep the overall volume balanced so that music, effects, and voiceover do not compete.

Realistic expectations

AI video tools are powerful, but they are not a shortcut to a good Short. The generator produces raw clips, and the quality varies from one generation to the next. Some clips will be beautiful. Some will have warped faces, morphing objects, or flickering textures. Expecting every generation to be usable is the fastest route to disappointment.

The realistic workflow is to generate generously, review honestly, and edit ruthlessly. Generate more clips than you need, discard the ones with artefacts, and keep only the shots that look good and match each other. A Short built from five strong clips looks better than a Short built from ten clips where half are flawed.

It is also realistic to expect your first few Shorts to be rough. Prompting, pacing, and editing are skills that improve with practice. Your tenth Short will be noticeably better than your first, because you will know which prompts work, which shots to plan, and how to cut a video that holds attention.

Finally, be honest about what AI video is. It is a tool for making visuals, not a replacement for an idea. The Shorts that perform well have a clear idea, a strong hook, and tight editing. AI video can supply the footage for those Shorts, but it cannot supply the idea. That part is yours.

Editing is where Shorts are made

If there is one lesson to take from this guide, it is that editing matters more than generation. The generator makes clips. The editor makes a video. The difference between a forgettable Short and a good one is almost always in the editing: the hook, the pacing, the captions, the music, and the cuts.

Learn the basics of a free editing tool: trimming, ordering, adding text, and adding music. These four skills cover most of what a Short needs. Practice by re-editing the same set of clips in different ways: a fast version and a slow version, a version with music and a version with a voiceover. Comparing the versions teaches you what editing does.

Watch your draft on a phone, in the same way your audience will watch it. Notice where you want to swipe away, where you lose interest, and where you feel confused. Those moments are editing problems, and fixing them, by cutting a shot, moving the hook earlier, or adding a caption, is the work that turns raw clips into a Short worth watching.

Questions people ask

What shape should an AI clip be for YouTube Shorts?

Vertical, taller than it is wide, so it fills a phone screen. Generate in a vertical aspect ratio if your tool offers one, or generate wide and crop in editing, keeping the subject centred so nothing important is cut off.

How long should a YouTube Short be?

Shorts can run up to 60 seconds, and many successful ones are between 15 and 45 seconds. The right length depends on the idea: a single visual moment may need only 10 seconds, while a short explanation may need the full minute. Keep every second earning its place.

Can AI video alone make a good Short?

Rarely. AI video produces good raw clips, but a good Short needs human editing: a strong hook, tight pacing, captions, music, and a clear ending. The generator supplies the footage; the editing supplies the video.

How do I add captions to a Short?

Add captions in an editing tool after you assemble the clips, not in the video generator. Keep captions short, large enough to read on a phone, and timed to the voiceover or the beat of the music. Always check the wording before publishing.

Should I use music in my Shorts?

Music helps most Shorts, because it sets the mood and keeps the pacing tight. Use music you have the right to use, such as tracks from a royalty-free library or the audio library provided by the platform. Avoid commercial songs without permission.
