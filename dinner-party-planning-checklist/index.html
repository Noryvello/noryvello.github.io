This page is general planning guidance only. Always ask guests about allergies and dietary needs before you cook, and check food labels if you are unsure whether a dish suits a guest.

Choose a menu you can actually cook

The best dinner party menu is one you can cook calmly. Pick dishes you have made before, or at least one dish you know well, and build the rest of the menu around it. A simple menu cooked well always beats an ambitious menu that keeps you in the kitchen all evening. If a dish needs last-minute attention, make it the starter or the dessert, not the main course.

Prepare as much as you can in advance. Sauces, dressings, salads, and most desserts can be made the day before. On the evening itself, you want to be setting the table and welcoming guests, not chopping vegetables. Write out a simple timeline the day before, working backwards from the time guests arrive.

Ask about dietary needs early

Ask every guest about allergies and dietary needs when you invite them, not on the night. It is far easier to plan one vegetarian or vegan main from the start than to improvise one at the last minute. If you are unsure what a guest can eat, ask them directly — people are usually happy to explain, and they would much rather be asked than served something they cannot eat.

When you serve, label dishes or simply tell guests what is in each one. Keep serving spoons separate so a dish stays suitable for the guests it was made for. A little care here makes every guest feel welcome at the table.

Set the table before you start cooking

Set the table first, then cook. It sounds backwards, but it works: once the table is set, the room looks ready, and you can cook without worrying about the room. Count plates, glasses, and cutlery for every guest and every course before you start, and put out water glasses and a jug of water so guests can help themselves.

Keep the centrepiece low and simple so guests can see each other across the table. Candles, a small vase of flowers, or a bowl of fruit all work. The table does not need to be perfect — it needs to be welcoming, with enough room for plates, glasses, and elbows.

Questions people ask

How many courses should I serve at a dinner party?

Three courses — starter, main, and dessert — is a good standard for a dinner party. Two courses work well for a relaxed evening, and four courses suit a longer, more formal night. Choose a number you can cook calmly rather than one that keeps you in the kitchen.

How far ahead should I invite guests to a dinner party?

A week or two ahead is usually enough for a small dinner party. It gives guests time to reply and gives you time to plan the menu around dietary needs. For a larger party or a special occasion, three weeks is safer.

What should I do if a guest has an allergy?

Ask them exactly what they cannot eat, and plan a dish they can eat from the start rather than adapting one at the last minute. Check labels on sauces, stocks, and dressings, and keep serving spoons separate so dishes stay safe.

How do I keep the conversation going at a dinner party?

Seat guests next to people they will enjoy talking to, and introduce guests who have not met. A simple question at the table — a recent trip, a favourite meal, a book worth reading — is usually enough to get people talking.

What if a dish goes wrong on the night?

Do not panic and do not apologise for it at length. Serve what you have, move on to the next course, and keep the evening moving. Guests remember the welcome and the company far more than any single dish.
