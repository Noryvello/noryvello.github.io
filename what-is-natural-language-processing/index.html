What Is Natural Language Processing (NLP)?

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

Natural language processing, or NLP, is the branch of AI that lets computers work with human language: reading it, understanding it, and writing it. It powers translation tools, spam filters, voice assistants, and chatbots. This guide explains what NLP does, where you already use it, and why language is so hard for computers.

Every day, you type, speak, and read words, and software quietly works with those words on your behalf. Your phone turns your speech into text. Your email sorts spam from real messages. A translation tool turns a foreign sentence into your own language. All of these are natural language processing at work, and most people use it dozens of times a day without ever hearing the name.

NLP is one of the oldest and most practical branches of AI. It is also the branch behind the most famous AI tools of today, because chatbots are, at heart, NLP systems. This guide explains what NLP does, how it works in plain terms, and where its limits lie.

NLP systems work with patterns in words, not with meaning. They can misunderstand sarcasm, miss context, and produce confident answers that are wrong. Treat their output as a draft to check, and keep sensitive personal details out of online language tools.

What NLP does, in plain words

Natural language processing is the technology that lets a computer handle human language. "Natural language" means the language people actually speak and write, as opposed to computer code. "Processing" means doing useful work with it: reading it, making sense of it, translating it, summarising it, or writing new text in reply. If a program takes words in and does something sensible with them, NLP is probably involved.

The field has two sides. One side is understanding: turning human words into something the computer can work with. When you ask a voice assistant to set an alarm, NLP must work out that you want an alarm, and at what time. The other side is generation: turning the computer's output back into words a person can read. When a chatbot answers your question, NLP is doing the generation. Most real tools use both sides together.

It helps to think of NLP as a bridge between two very different worlds. People speak in messy, ambiguous, context-dependent language, full of slang, typos, and half-finished thoughts. Computers need precise, structured input. NLP is the set of techniques that lets the two meet in the middle, so you can talk to a machine in ordinary words and get an ordinary answer back.

Understanding language: the hard part

Understanding is the harder half of NLP, because human language is full of ambiguity. The word "bank" can mean a river's edge or a place that keeps money. The sentence "I saw her duck" could mean you watched her pet bird, or you watched her bend down quickly. People resolve these puzzles instantly, using context, common sense, and knowledge of the world. Computers have none of those, so they must work out the meaning from patterns in the words themselves.

Modern NLP systems handle ambiguity by looking at the whole sentence, and often the whole conversation, rather than one word at a time. They learn from training data which meanings are likely in which contexts. If the sentence mentions money, "bank" probably means the financial kind. If it mentions a river, it probably means the edge. The system is not truly understanding; it is making a very well-informed guess based on patterns.

This guessing works remarkably well for ordinary language, which is why voice assistants and chatbots feel so capable. But it breaks down at the edges: sarcasm, jokes, implied meanings, and cultural references. When someone says "great, another meeting" in a flat tone, a person hears the sarcasm immediately. An NLP system, reading only the words, may take it at face value. The gap between pattern and meaning is where NLP still struggles.

Generating language: writing back

The other half of NLP is generation: producing words. Early language software generated text from templates, filling in blanks like a form letter. Modern systems generate text word by word, predicting the most likely next word based on patterns learned from training data. That is how chatbots write fluent paragraphs, how translation tools produce natural-sounding sentences, and how your phone's keyboard suggests the next word as you type.

Generation has improved enormously, and that is why AI writing can feel so human. A modern system can match a tone, follow a format, and keep a consistent style over a long passage. It can write a formal email, a casual message, or a poem, adjusting its words to fit. The fluency is real, and it is genuinely useful for drafting, summarising, and explaining.

But generation has the same weakness as understanding: it is pattern-matching, not knowledge. A generated answer can be beautifully written and completely wrong. It can invent a quote, misstate a date, or confidently describe something that does not exist. The writing gives no hint of the error, because the system writes wrong answers in the same smooth style as right ones. Fluency is not evidence of truth, and that is the most important thing to remember about generated text.

Everyday uses you already know

Translation is one of the most visible uses of NLP. When you paste a sentence into a translation tool, NLP analyses the grammar and meaning of the source language and produces a sentence in the target language. Modern translation is far better than the word-for-word tools of the past, because it considers whole sentences and their context, but it still struggles with idioms, humour, and cultural references.

Spam filters are a quieter example. Your email service uses NLP to read incoming messages and decide which are genuine and which are junk. It learned the patterns of spam from millions of examples: the phrasing, the structure, the tell-tale signs. It is not perfect, which is why a real message occasionally lands in the spam folder, but it saves most people from a flood of unwanted mail every day.

Voice assistants and chatbots are the uses people notice most. A voice assistant must turn speech into text, work out what you want, and reply in words. A chatbot must read your message, understand the request, and write a helpful answer. Both rely on NLP at every step, from understanding your words to generating a reply. They are the most direct experience most people have of NLP, and they show both its power and its limits.

Key tasks, explained simply

NLP is made of smaller tasks, each with its own name. Translation turns text from one language into another. Sentiment analysis works out whether a piece of text is positive, negative, or neutral, which businesses use to read customer feedback. Summarisation shortens a long document into its main points. Question answering finds or writes an answer to a question, which is what chatbots and search assistants do.

Named-entity recognition is another key task: spotting names of people, places, organisations, and dates in a text. When a system highlights a person's name in a document, or pulls a date out of an email to suggest a calendar entry, it is using named-entity recognition. It is a small task, but it powers many features people take for granted.

These tasks combine into the tools you use. A voice assistant uses speech recognition, question answering, and generation. A translation tool uses understanding and generation in two languages. A chatbot uses almost all of them at once. Knowing the task names is not essential, but it helps you understand what a tool is actually doing when it reads and writes for you.

Why language is so hard for computers

Language is hard for computers because it is not a code with fixed rules. It is a living, messy human invention, full of exceptions, shortcuts, and shared assumptions. Grammar has rules, but people break them constantly. Words have meanings, but the meaning changes with context. A sentence can mean the opposite of what it says, as in sarcasm, or mean more than what it says, as in a hint.

Computers, by contrast, like precision. They work best with clear rules and unambiguous inputs. Language gives them neither. Every sentence is a puzzle with missing pieces, and the missing pieces are supplied by shared human experience: what the world is like, what people usually want, what is polite, what is funny. A computer has none of that experience, so it must guess from patterns alone.

That is why NLP is an ongoing field of research, not a solved problem. Systems have become dramatically better, and for everyday tasks they are genuinely useful. But the deep problem remains: a system that works with patterns in words can go very far, and yet it still does not understand what the words mean. The gap between processing language and understanding it is the central challenge of NLP.

NLP and large language models: how they fit together

Large language models are the most powerful NLP systems ever built, and they have changed the field. Before them, NLP tools were usually built for one task: one system for translation, another for spam filtering, another for answering questions. A large language model can do many of these tasks with a single system, because it learned the general patterns of language from a huge amount of text.

This does not mean older NLP has disappeared. Small, specialised systems are still used where they are faster, cheaper, or more reliable than a large model. A spam filter does not need a chatbot to do its job. A spell-checker does not need to understand a whole conversation. The right tool depends on the task, and the best systems often combine a large language model with smaller, specialised NLP tools.

For a beginner, the practical point is this: when you use a chatbot, you are using NLP at its most advanced. When you use a translation tool or a spam filter, you are using NLP in its everyday form. Both are the same field, applied at different scales, and both are worth understanding if you want to use AI tools well.

Questions people ask

Is NLP the same as a chatbot?

No. NLP is the field of technology that lets computers work with human language. A chatbot is one product built with NLP. Translation tools, spam filters, and voice assistants are also built with NLP, and none of them is a chatbot.

What is the difference between NLP and an LLM?

NLP is the broad field of language technology, covering everything from spell-checking to translation. An LLM, a large language model, is one kind of NLP system, trained on huge amounts of text to read and write language. All LLMs use NLP, but not all NLP uses LLMs.

Can NLP understand sarcasm?

Not reliably. Sarcasm depends on tone, context, and knowing what the speaker really means, which is hard for software. NLP systems can sometimes spot sarcasm from patterns, but they often get it wrong, especially in short messages without context.

Does NLP work in every language?

No. NLP works best in languages with large amounts of written text available for training, such as English. Languages with less training data get weaker results, and some languages have very little NLP support at all. Quality varies a great deal by language.

Is my text private when I use NLP tools?

It depends on the tool. Many online tools send your text to a server to process it, and their privacy policies say what happens to it. Avoid typing passwords, financial details, or other sensitive information into any online language tool.
