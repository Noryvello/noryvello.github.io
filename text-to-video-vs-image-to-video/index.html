Text-to-Video vs Image-to-Video: Which to Use?

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

Text-to-video invents a scene from your words alone, while image-to-video animates a picture you provide. Text-first wins when you want the model to imagine freely; image-first wins when you need a specific look to stay consistent. Most good AI videos use both, and this guide explains when to reach for each.

Every AI video generator offers one or both of these two modes, and choosing between them is one of the first decisions you make on any project. The choice matters more than it seems, because the two modes give you different kinds of control. Text-to-video gives you control through language: you describe what you want, and the model invents it. Image-to-video gives you control through a picture: you show the model what you want, and it animates it.

Neither mode is better in general. They are good at different things, and the best creators switch between them from shot to shot. This guide explains how each mode works, when each one wins, how to combine them, and the honest limits of both.

Honest limit: both modes have limits. Text-to-video can invent the wrong scene, and image-to-video can drift away from your starting picture as a clip plays. Faces, hands, and on-screen text are unreliable in both modes. Check every clip before using it, and add text in editing rather than asking the generator to draw it.

How text-to-video works

Text-to-video starts with nothing but a written prompt. You describe a scene, the model reads the description, and it generates a short clip that matches. The model has never seen your exact scene before; it builds it from patterns learned from training videos, combining what it knows about the subject, the setting, the lighting, and the movement you described.

The strength of text-to-video is imagination. You can ask for scenes that would be impossible or expensive to film: a castle floating above clouds, a city street in the rain at night, a desert at dawn with a lone traveller. The model invents the whole scene, including details you did not mention, which can be delightful when the invention is good.

The weakness is precision. A text prompt is a loose description, and the model fills in the gaps with its own guesses. If you need a specific product, a specific person, or a specific place, text alone is often not enough. The model may generate something close to your description but wrong in the details that matter, such as the shape of a product or the colour of a logo.

Text-to-video also starts fresh with every generation. Two clips generated from the same prompt will look similar but not identical, and a character or setting can change noticeably between clips. That makes text-to-video a poor choice for scenes where consistency matters, and a good choice for standalone shots where each clip stands on its own.

How image-to-video works

Image-to-video starts from a picture you provide. You upload a photo or an AI-generated image, add a short description of the movement you want, and the model generates a clip that begins with your image and then moves. The picture sets the scene, and the prompt sets the motion.

The strength of image-to-video is control. The characters, the objects, the colours, and the composition all come from your starting image, so they stay close to what you intended. If you want a specific product in your video, start from a photo of that product. If you want a specific character, start from an image of that character. The model animates what you give it rather than inventing something new.

The weakness is that the model can drift. The opening frame usually matches your image closely, but as the clip plays, details can change: a face may shift, an object may morph, or the background may rearrange itself. The longer the clip, the more room there is for drift. Checking the end of the clip, not just the beginning, is essential.

Image-to-video is also the standard way to extend a video. You can take the last frame of one clip, use it as the starting image for the next, and build a longer sequence from short pieces. Each join needs checking, because the new clip may not match the old one perfectly, but this is how most creators make AI videos longer than a single clip allows.

When text-first wins

Text-to-video is the better choice when you can describe the scene clearly in words and do not need a specific look. Establishing shots are the classic example: a wide view of a city, a landscape, or a sky. These shots set the scene, and the model has plenty of freedom to invent something beautiful.

Text-first also wins when you are exploring ideas. If you are not sure what a scene should look like, generating a few text-to-video clips is a fast way to see possibilities. You can try different moods, different settings, and different camera moves, and pick the direction that feels right before committing to a starting image.

Abstract and atmospheric scenes suit text-first too. A dream sequence, a swirling sky, or a fantasy landscape does not need a specific starting image, and the model's invention is part of the appeal. When the goal is mood rather than accuracy, text-to-video is often the faster and more creative choice.

Finally, text-first wins when you have no image to start from. If the scene exists only in your head, text is the only way to begin. You can always generate a still image first, refine it until you like it, and then animate it with image-to-video, but for a quick first look, text-to-video gets you there in one step.

When image-first wins

Image-to-video is the better choice whenever the look matters more than the invention. Product videos are the clearest example: the product must look like the real product, with the right shape, colour, and label. Starting from a photo of the product is the only reliable way to get that.

Character consistency is the second big case. If the same person appears in several clips, image-to-video from a single starting image keeps them recognizable. Text-to-video will generate a slightly different person each time, which breaks the feeling that the video follows one character through a story.

Image-first also wins for specific places. If your video is set in a real location, a photo of that location as a starting image keeps the buildings, the layout, and the atmosphere true. A text description of the same place would produce a generic version that looks like everywhere and nowhere.

Branded content is a fourth case. If the video needs a logo, a colour scheme, or a visual style that matches a brand, starting from an image that already has those elements is far more reliable than describing them in words. The model keeps what the image shows; it often fumbles what a prompt describes.

The table is a guide, not a rule. Real projects mix the modes freely, and the right choice for a shot depends on what that shot needs. A video that uses text-to-video for its wide shots and image-to-video for its close-ups is using each mode where it is strongest.

Combining both modes

The most effective workflow uses both modes together. A common pattern is to generate a still image first, refine it until you like it, and then animate it with image-to-video for the shots that need consistency. For the shots where invention is fine, such as wide establishing shots, use text-to-video directly.

Another useful combination is to use text-to-video to explore, then image-to-video to produce. Generate a few text clips to find a look you like, take the best frame from the best clip, and use that frame as the starting image for the final clips. This gives you the creativity of text-to-video and the control of image-to-video in one workflow.

You can also chain the modes: generate a clip with text-to-video, take its last frame, and use it as the starting image for an image-to-video clip that continues the scene. This is how creators build longer sequences from short generations, and it works because each new clip starts from a real frame rather than from a fresh guess.

When combining modes, keep the style words consistent across all prompts. If the text-to-video shots are described as cinematic with warm light, use the same words for the image-to-video shots. Consistent style words are the simplest way to make clips from different modes feel like parts of the same video.

Consistency tips for multi-clip videos

Consistency is the hardest part of making a multi-clip AI video, and it is where most projects fall apart. A character who looks different in every shot, or a setting that changes colour between clips, breaks the viewer's trust. A few habits help.

  • Use one starting image per scene. Generate all clips in a scene from the same image, so the characters and setting share a common source.
  • Reuse the same style words. Keep the lighting, colour, and mood words identical across every prompt in a video.
  • Generate extra takes. For each shot, generate several clips and pick the ones that match each other best. Discarding mismatched clips is faster than trying to fix them.
  • Keep shots short. Short clips drift less than long ones, so a video built from short shots stays more consistent.
  • Cut on movement. When joining clips, cut at a moment of motion, such as a turn or a step, rather than at a still moment. The viewer's eye follows the movement and notices the join less.
  • Check the joins. Watch each transition between clips and check that the lighting, the colours, and the characters match. If a join looks wrong, regenerate one of the clips.

Honest limits of each mode

Both modes share some limits, and each has its own. Text-to-video can invent the wrong scene entirely, and it can be hard to steer: changing one word in a prompt can change the whole clip in unexpected ways. It is also the worse mode for anything that needs to be exact, such as a product, a logo, or a specific person.

Image-to-video can drift, and it can also be too faithful: if the starting image has a flaw, the clip will carry that flaw forward. It also struggles with large movements. A starting image of a person standing still does not easily become a clip of that person running, because the model has to invent too much. Small, gentle movements work best; big actions often distort.

Both modes struggle with faces, hands, and text. Both produce short clips. Both vary from one generation to the next, so no result is guaranteed. The creators who get the best results are the ones who plan for these limits: short shots, simple actions, consistent starting images, and careful review of every clip.

Questions people ask

Which is better, text-to-video or image-to-video?

Neither is better in general; it depends on the shot. Text-to-video is better when you can describe the scene in words and want the model to invent it. Image-to-video is better when you already have the look you want and need the model to keep it consistent.

Can I use a photo I took as the starting image?

Yes, as long as it is your own photo or one you have permission to use. Real photos work well as starting images, especially for landscapes, products, and places. Avoid uploading photos of other people without their consent.

Does image-to-video keep my image exactly?

The opening frame usually matches your image closely, but the model may change details as the clip plays, especially in longer clips. Faces, small objects, and fine patterns are the most likely to drift. Check the end of the clip, not just the beginning.

Can I combine text-to-video and image-to-video in one video?

Yes, and most creators do. Generate a starting image with an image tool, animate it with image-to-video for the shots that need consistency, and use text-to-video for establishing shots and scenes where invention is fine. Join the clips in an editing tool.

Which mode is better for keeping a character the same across clips?

Image-to-video is better, because every clip starts from the same picture of the character. Text-to-video starts fresh each time, so the character can change between clips. For the best consistency, use one starting image for all clips in a scene.
