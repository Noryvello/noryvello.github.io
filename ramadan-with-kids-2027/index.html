Ramadan with Kids 2027

By the Noryvello team · Published 19 November 2026 · Updated 19 November 2026

Ramadan is a wonderful month for children when they are included gently, with activities, stories and small fasts of their own. This guide has practical ideas for families in Ramadan 2027, from first fasts to crafts, routines and giving. Everything here is flexible, so you can shape it to your children's ages and energy.

Children love Ramadan when they are part of it. The late nights, the shared meals, the decorations, the sense that the whole family is doing something special together — these are the memories that stay with them. Ramadan 2027 is expected to begin on Monday 8 February, and a little planning now can make the month joyful for the youngest members of the family.

The golden rule is gentleness. Children are not required to fast, and the month should feel like an invitation, not a test. The ideas on this page are about belonging: small fasts, simple activities, stories at bedtime, and the habit of giving. Take what suits your family and leave the rest.

Why children love Ramadan

Ask adults about their childhood Ramadans and they will tell you about the same things: staying up late, eating together, the excitement of the moon, and the feeling that the house itself was different. Children love Ramadan because it is out of the ordinary. The routine changes, the family gathers, and the month has its own sights, sounds and smells.

You can build on that natural excitement with small touches. Decorate a corner of the house together. Let children help set the iftar table. Count down the days on a simple chart. Give the month a shape they can see and follow, and they will look forward to it each year.

Above all, let them see that Ramadan makes the adults happy. Children take their feelings about the month from the people around them. If the grown-ups are calm, generous and glad to be fasting, children will want to be part of it too.

First fasts: the gentle way in

Children are not required to fast until puberty, but many want to try. The gentle way in is a short fast: from breakfast until lunchtime, or from lunchtime until iftar. A half-day fast lets a child feel part of the month without the strain of a full day, and it is a real achievement worth celebrating.

Let the child choose. Ask whether they would like to try, and for how long, and make it clear that they can stop whenever they want. A child who breaks a first fast at noon and feels proud has had a good Ramadan experience; a child pushed to continue while miserable has had the opposite. Praise the effort, not the length.

As children grow older, they will naturally want to fast for longer, and many teenagers fast the whole month. When a child does complete a full day for the first time, mark it: a special iftar, a small gift, or simply telling the family. These milestones become part of their Ramadan story.

A Ramadan routine children can follow

Children thrive on routine, and Ramadan works best for them when the days have a clear shape. A simple routine might be: school as usual, a rest or quiet activity in the afternoon, helping with iftar, the evening meal together, a story, and bed. Suhoor is optional for young children — most will sleep through, and that is fine.

Sleep matters. Late nights are part of Ramadan's fun, but children still need their rest, especially on school days. A slightly later bedtime than usual, with a lie-in where possible, is a good balance. If the weekend allows a later night and a visit to the mosque, so much the better.

Keep one anchor in the day that belongs to the children: a craft, a story, a good deed, or a few minutes of reading together. A small daily ritual gives the month a rhythm they can follow and remember.

Activities and crafts for the month

A few simple activities will fill the month happily. Make paper lanterns and crescent moons to hang in the window. Build a Ramadan countdown chart, with a sticker or a star for each day. Create a good-deeds jar, where each family member drops in a note about something kind they did that day.

Cooking together is another favourite. Children can wash salad, set the table, shape dough, or decorate a simple dessert. Iftar tastes better when you helped make it, and the kitchen is a natural place for conversation about the month.

Outdoor time still matters. A walk before iftar, a visit to the park in the morning, or a family trip to the mosque all break up the day. Ramadan is not a month to stay indoors: fresh air and gentle activity keep children happy and make the evening meal taste better.

Stories and lessons for young hearts

Ramadan is a storytelling month. The story of the first revelation, the kindness of the Prophet, the generosity of the early Muslims — these are stories children remember for life. Tell one short story each evening, or read from a children's book about Ramadan, and let the children ask questions.

Keep the lessons simple and kind. Ramadan teaches patience, gratitude and care for others, and children learn these best through examples rather than lectures. A story about sharing food, a visit to a neighbour, or a small act of charity will teach more than any lesson about the rules.

Many families read a little of the Quran together each day, even just a few verses. Children who are learning to read Arabic can practise a short passage, and younger children can listen. The sound of the Quran in the house is itself a lesson, and a peaceful way to end the day.

Iftar and suhoor with children

Iftar is the children's favourite part of the day, and it deserves a little ceremony. Let them help set the table, pour the water, and arrange the dates. Count down the last minutes together, and break the fast as a family. The shared moment matters more than the food.

Keep children's portions sensible and their choices real. A child who is fasting a half-day will be genuinely hungry by iftar, so have their favourite food ready. A child who is not fasting can still enjoy the meal and the gathering — nobody should feel left out of the table.

Suhoor is usually for the grown-ups, but older children who are trying a fast may want to join. If they do, keep it short and warm: a simple meal, a quick prayer, and back to bed. There is no need for a child to lose sleep over a meal they can eat at breakfast.

Helping children understand giving

Ramadan is the month of giving, and children can take part in ways that suit them. A charity jar on the kitchen table, into which children drop coins through the month, is a simple start. At the end of Ramadan, the family gives the jar's contents together, and the children see where it goes.

Giving is not only about money. Children can give food to a neighbour, help pack a parcel for a food bank, or make a card for someone who is lonely. These acts teach the heart of Ramadan — that the month is about others as much as ourselves — and they give children a sense of purpose.

Talk about giving simply and honestly. Explain that some families have less, that fasting helps us understand that, and that sharing what we have is one of the best parts of the month. Children understand fairness instinctively, and Ramadan gives them a chance to act on it.

Questions people ask

At what age do children start fasting?

Children are not required to fast until puberty. Families usually introduce fasting gradually, with short fasts first, and children take it up fully when they are ready.

How long should a first fast be?

A few hours, or until lunchtime, is a common gentle start. Let the child choose the length, and make it clear they can stop whenever they want. Praise the effort, not the length.

What if my child wants to fast the whole day?

Praise the intention and allow it if they are well and happy. Keep an eye on them, make sure they eat a good suhoor, and let them break the fast at any point if they struggle.

How do I keep children busy during Ramadan?

Crafts, stories, helping with iftar, and small charity projects all fill the days happily. A short daily activity, such as a countdown chart or a good-deeds jar, gives the month a rhythm.

Can children go to Taraweeh?

Yes, many mosques welcome children at Taraweeh. Keep visits short for young children, and let them pray a few rakats before heading home.

What about school during Ramadan?

School continues as usual. A good night's sleep and a sensible routine matter more than anything. If your child is fasting, let the teacher know so they can be understanding.
