How to Choose a University Course

Choosing a course is easier when you compare like with like. This guide gives you a worksheet for scoring up to three courses on interest, strengths and course features, plus a checklist of questions to ask on an open day.

This worksheet ranks courses by your own scores, not by any league table. It cannot tell you which course is best: it only makes your own judgement clearer. Check the facts on each course with the institution itself before you apply.

Start with what you enjoy

A university course lasts years, and you will spend most of that time reading, writing and thinking about one subject. Enjoyment is not a luxury here; it is what carries you through the hard weeks. Before you look at any course list, write down the subjects you already enjoy and the ones you would happily spend more time on. The best course for you is almost always built around something on that list.

Enjoyment is not the only thing, though. A subject you love with a teaching style you hate will wear you down, and a course that leads nowhere you want to go will feel like a dead end. That is why the worksheet scores four things, not one: interest, fit with your strengths, what comes after, and the course itself.

The four things to score

Interest in the subject is the first score, and the most important: how much do you want to spend years on this? Fit with your strengths is second: does the course play to what you are good at, whether that is writing, numbers, practical work or discussion? A course that fights your strengths will feel harder than it needs to be.

Career and study options after the course is third: what doors does it open, and do you want to walk through them? Course features is fourth: the teaching style, the balance of lectures and small groups, placements, and where the course is taught. Score each one honestly from 1 to 5, and let the totals show you what your judgement already knows.

How to use the worksheet

Name up to three courses you are considering, then score each one on the four things. Be honest rather than hopeful: a 5 for interest should mean you genuinely love the subject, not that you wish you did. When you have all twelve scores, the worksheet ranks the courses by total, and shows you the working so you can see where each course won and lost.

Pay attention to the pattern, not just the total. A course that scores 5 on interest and 2 on everything else is a different choice from a course that scores 4 across the board. The totals rank your courses; the pattern tells you why.

Open-day questions that actually help

Open days are your best chance to check the facts behind your scores, but only if you ask the right questions. Ask what the course actually covers and how much choice you get later on. Ask how teaching is split between lectures, small groups and practical work, and how you will be assessed. Ask current students what a typical week looks like: their answer will tell you more than any brochure.

Take the checklist at the top of the page with you, and tick each question off when you have an answer. If a question gets a vague answer, ask it again in a different way. The institutions expect these questions, and a straight answer is a good sign in itself.

When two courses tie

Ties are common, and they usually mean both courses are good choices. Break the tie with the things the worksheet does not measure: which campus felt right when you visited, which course content excited you more when you read the detail, and which one you would pick if you had to decide today with no more information. The answer to that last question is often the honest one.

If you still cannot decide, talk to someone who knows the subject — a teacher, a tutor, or a graduate of one of the courses. They can tell you things no worksheet can, such as how the teaching really feels and what the workload is like in the second year.

After you choose

Once you have chosen, commit to the choice and stop comparing. Every course has dull parts, and comparing your course with the one you did not pick will only make them look worse. Read the course description properly, look at the reading list, and start thinking about your personal statement, which is where you explain why this course and why you.

Remember that choosing a course is not choosing a life. People change direction after university all the time, and the skills a course teaches — writing, thinking, working with others — travel further than the subject itself. Pick the course that fits you best now, and trust yourself to adapt later.

Questions people ask

Should I choose by subject or by career?

Start with the subject, because you will spend years studying it, then check what it leads to. A course you enjoy that opens doors you want is the best combination. If a career needs a particular course, that matters too, so score both on the worksheet.

What if I do not know what I want to study?

Start with what you enjoy and what you are good at, and look for courses that combine the two. Talk to teachers and graduates, and visit open days for two or three very different courses. Comparing real courses often makes the choice clearer than thinking about it alone.

How much does location matter?

More than most people expect. You will live there for years, so think about the cost of living, how far it is from home, and whether the place itself suits you. Score it under course features on the worksheet.

Should I listen to league tables?

Treat them as one piece of information, not the answer. League tables measure things that may not matter to you, and they say nothing about whether you will enjoy the course. Your own scores on the worksheet are a better guide to fit.

Can I change course later?

Sometimes, but it depends on the institution and the courses involved, and it is not something to count on. Choose as if you will stay, and ask at an open day what the rules are for changing course, just so you know.
