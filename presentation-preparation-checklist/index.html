Presentation Preparation Checklist

This checklist covers everything worth doing before you present, from knowing your audience and shaping your structure to rehearsing and checking the room on the day. Tick each item off as you go, and use the timing estimator to check your slide count fits the time you have.

This checklist covers preparation, not the talk itself. No checklist can guarantee a good presentation, and the rehearsal steps matter most, so leave time for at least one full run-through out loud.

Start with the audience

Every good presentation starts with the people listening, not with the slides. Before you write a single word, ask yourself who is in the room, what they already know, and what they care about. A talk for beginners needs more background and fewer technical terms; a talk for experts can skip the basics and get straight to the interesting part. If you guess wrong about the audience, even a well-made talk will feel too long or too shallow.

Write the audience down in one sentence: who they are and what they want from your talk. Then write the single message you want them to take away. If you cannot state that message in one sentence, the talk is not ready to be built yet. Everything else — the structure, the slides, the examples — exists to support that one message.

Shape the structure before the slides

It is tempting to open your slide software first, but structure comes before slides. A simple shape works for most talks: an opening that says why the topic matters, up to three main points, and a closing that returns to your message. Three points are easier to follow than five, and easier for you to remember when you are nervous.

Give each section a rough length before you start writing. If you have thirty minutes and three points, each point gets roughly eight minutes, with time left for the opening and the close. This plan is also what makes the timing estimator useful: when you know how long each part should take, you can spot the part that is running long before the day itself.

Slides that support, not replace, you

Slides are there to help the audience follow you, not to carry the talk on their own. One idea per slide is the rule that matters most: if a slide has two ideas, split it. Short lines beat full sentences, because the audience cannot read a paragraph and listen to you at the same time. If your slides make sense without you, they are probably doing too much.

Check readability from a distance. A slide that looks fine on your laptop can be unreadable from the back of a room, so stand back from your screen or test it on the projector if you can. Charts need labels big enough to read, and images need to be sharp, not stretched or blurry. When in doubt, make the text bigger and cut a line.

Rehearse out loud, on your feet

Reading your notes silently is not rehearsal. A talk only becomes real when you say it out loud, standing up, at the pace you will use on the day. The first run-through will almost always be longer than you expect, and it will show you the sentences that sound fine on paper but trip you up when spoken. Time it, and write down where the time goes.

Rehearse a second time after fixing the slow parts. If you can, rehearse in front of one honest person and ask them two questions: what was the main message, and where did they stop following. Their answers will tell you more than another hour alone with your slides. And prepare for questions: write down the three hardest ones you might be asked, and have a short answer ready for each.

On the day

Arrive early enough to set up without rushing. Test the projector, the sound and the clicker, and stand where you will speak so you know how the room feels. Put water and your notes within reach, and keep your phone silent and out of sight. A calm setup makes a calm start, and the first minute sets the tone for the rest of the talk.

Keep a backup copy of your slides somewhere you can reach without your laptop, such as a USB stick or a second device. Technology fails at the worst moments, and a backup turns a disaster into a short pause. If something does go wrong, keep going: the audience remembers how you handled it far longer than they remember the glitch itself.

Questions people ask

How many slides do I need for my talk?

It depends on your pace and your time. As a rough guide, a steady pace is about one slide every 90 seconds, so a 30 minute talk fits about 20 slides. Use the timing estimator on this page to check your own slide count against the time you have.

How many times should I rehearse?

At least once out loud, standing up, with a timer. A second run-through after fixing the slow parts makes a real difference, and a practice run in front of one honest person is worth more than several runs alone.

What should I do if I run out of time during the talk?

Know in advance which slides you can skip without losing your message, and mark them in your notes. If the clock is against you, jump to your closing rather than rushing through everything that is left.

Should I memorise my talk word for word?

No. Memorising word for word makes you sound stiff, and one forgotten line can throw you off completely. Know your structure and your key phrases, and use short notes for anything that must be said exactly.

What if the projector or laptop fails?

That is what the backup copy is for. Keep your slides on a second device or a USB stick, and know your opening and your main points well enough to carry on without slides if you have to.
