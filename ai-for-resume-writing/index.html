How to Use AI for Resume Writing

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

AI can help you write a stronger resume by turning your real experience into clear achievement bullets, tailoring your summary to each job post, and matching the language employers use. This guide shows how — and where the hard line sits: nothing on your resume may be invented.

A resume is a short document with a hard job: in a minute or two of reading, it must convince a stranger that you can do a job you have not yet done. Most resumes fail not because the person is unqualified, but because the writing hides the evidence — vague duties instead of real results, and a generic summary that could belong to anyone.

AI is good at fixing exactly that. It can turn a list of duties into achievement bullets, reshape a summary for a specific role, and help you use the words employers actually search for. Used well, it makes your real record easier to see. Used badly, it invents one — and that is the line this guide never crosses.

Never invent jobs, degrees, or skills on your resume. AI can suggest impressive-sounding experience you do not have, and adding it is dishonest — it can cost you the job when it is discovered, and it can follow you afterwards. Every line on your resume must be true, and you must be ready to talk about it in an interview.

Start with your real record

Before AI touches your resume, gather the raw material yourself: every job, project, and responsibility you want to claim, with dates, details, and anything you achieved. Write it roughly — bullet points are fine. The goal is a complete, honest inventory of what you have actually done.

Be specific about results while you still remember them. "Helped customers" is vague; "answered customer queries by phone and message, handling around forty a day" is a real record. AI can sharpen the wording later, but it cannot supply the facts — those come from you, and they must be right.

If a detail is uncertain, leave it out or check it. Guessing at a job title, a date, or a result is how small errors creep into a resume. A shorter resume that is entirely true beats a longer one with a line you cannot defend.

Achievement bullets that show results

The strongest resume bullets describe achievements, not duties. "Responsible for the team rota" says what you were supposed to do; "rebuilt the team rota to cover weekend gaps, cutting last-minute call-outs" says what you actually achieved. Employers read for evidence, and achievements are evidence.

AI can help you turn duties into achievements. Give it a duty and ask it to suggest achievement-style rewrites — then pick the version that matches what really happened, and adjust the wording until it is exactly true. If none of the versions is true, the duty stays as a duty; a plain true line is better than an impressive false one.

Use numbers only where they are real and you can explain them. A genuine figure — the size of a team, the number of customers served, the time saved — makes a bullet concrete. An invented figure is a lie that an interviewer can uncover with one question.

Tailoring to each job post

A resume written for every job impresses no one. Tailoring means reshaping your resume for each role: leading with the experience that matters most for that job, using the employer's own language for the role, and cutting what is irrelevant. AI makes this fast — paste the job post and your resume, and ask which of your experiences best match the requirements.

Use the job post as a checklist. For each requirement, ask yourself: do I have real evidence for this? If yes, make sure it appears prominently. If no, leave it out — claiming a requirement you do not meet wastes everyone's time, including yours, and it will surface in the interview.

Tailor the order as well as the words. The first third of your resume is what gets read first and remembered best. Put the experience most relevant to this job there, even if it is not your most recent role.

Writing a summary that sounds like you

The summary at the top of your resume — two or three sentences — is the hardest part to write and the easiest to get wrong. Generic summaries ("a motivated professional seeking new challenges") say nothing. A good summary names what you do, what you have done, and what you are aiming for, in plain language.

Ask AI to draft several summary options from your real record, in different tones, then choose the one that sounds most like you and edit it until it does. Read it aloud: if it sounds like a stranger, it needs your words in it. The summary should make a reader want to read the rest — not make them wade through buzzwords.

Keep it short and specific. Your years of experience, your main strength, and the kind of role you want are usually enough. Anything longer belongs in the body of the resume, where the evidence lives.

Keyword matching without stuffing

Many employers use software to screen resumes before a human reads them, and the software looks for the words in the job post. Matching those words matters — but only where they are true. If a post asks for a skill you genuinely have, use the employer's exact term for it somewhere in your resume.

AI can compare your resume with a job post and point out the terms you are missing. Use that as a checklist, and add the terms that honestly apply to you. The goal is to make your real experience visible to the screening software, not to pretend to experience you do not have.

Never stuff keywords into a hidden block or a list of skills you cannot demonstrate. Screening software may be fooled, but the human who reads next will not be — and a resume full of claimed skills you cannot discuss is worse than a short, honest one.

Formatting for humans and screening software

A resume must be readable by two audiences: the screening software and the person who reads it next. Simple formatting serves both. Use a clear structure — contact details, summary, experience, education, skills — with plain headings and a consistent layout. Fancy designs, tables, and graphics often confuse screening software and can hide your content.

Keep it to one or two pages. One page is right for most people early in their career; two pages suit longer careers with substantial experience. If your resume runs longer, the problem is usually that it lists everything rather than selecting what matters for this job.

Save and send it in the format the employer asks for, and check how it looks after saving. A resume that looks tidy on your screen but breaks in another format makes a poor first impression. Print it or preview it as the employer will see it before you send it.

Questions people ask

Can AI write my whole resume?

AI can draft every section of a resume, but it should never write it unsupervised. It can invent jobs, degrees, and skills that sound plausible, and it cannot know what you actually achieved. Use it to sharpen wording and structure; supply every fact yourself and check every line.

Will employers know I used AI?

A resume that reads like a template — vague, buzzword-heavy, and impersonal — raises suspicion whatever wrote it. A resume with specific, true achievements in your own voice does not. The question is not whether AI helped, but whether the resume is honest and specific.

What is an achievement bullet?

An achievement bullet describes something you actually accomplished, with evidence: what you did, how you did it, and what changed. "Rebuilt the rota to cover weekend gaps" is an achievement; "responsible for the rota" is a duty. Employers read achievements as proof you can do the job.

How do I tailor a resume to a job post?

Read the post carefully, list its requirements, and match each one to real evidence in your experience. Lead with the most relevant experience, use the employer's language for skills you genuinely have, and cut what does not matter for this role. One tailored resume beats ten generic ones.

Should I include keywords from the job post?

Yes, where they are true. Screening software looks for the words in the job post, so using the employer's terms for skills you genuinely have helps your resume get seen. Never add keywords for skills you cannot demonstrate — the human reader will check.
