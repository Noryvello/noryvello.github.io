Eid al-Fitr 2027 Celebrations Around the World

By the Noryvello team · Published 19 November 2026 · Updated 19 November 2026

Eid al-Fitr is celebrated by Muslims in every corner of the world, and while the prayer and the joy are the same everywhere, the customs around them are wonderfully varied. This guide tours the celebrations country by country, from family feasts to street festivals. Eid al-Fitr in 2027 is expected on Tuesday 9 March, depending on the sighting of the moon.

One festival, a thousand customs. Eid al-Fitr is the same celebration everywhere — the end of Ramadan, the morning prayer, the joy of breaking the fast for the last time — but every country, and often every family, marks it in its own way. The foods differ, the greetings differ, the clothes differ, and the visiting has its own local shape.

This guide is a tour of those customs, from the Middle East to Southeast Asia, from Africa to the West. It describes common traditions rather than rules: customs vary from family to family as well as from country to country, and that variety is part of the festival's beauty.

What Eid al-Fitr celebrates

Eid al-Fitr, the Festival of Breaking the Fast, marks the end of Ramadan, the month of fasting. It begins on the first day of Shawwal, the month that follows Ramadan in the Islamic calendar, and it is a day of gratitude: for the strength to complete the fast, for the blessings of the month, and for the community that shared it.

The day follows a pattern recognised everywhere. The morning begins with the Eid prayer, prayed in congregation. Then come the visits: family, friends and neighbours, with food and sweets at every stop. Children receive Eidi, money or small gifts, and the day is filled with greetings — "Eid Mubarak", meaning "Blessed Eid", is understood almost everywhere.

Charity is part of the festival too. Before the prayer, Muslims give Zakat al-Fitr, so that everyone can share in the celebration. Eid is a festival that begins with giving, and its joy is meant to be shared with the whole community, including those who have least.

The Middle East: family feasts and open houses

In much of the Middle East, Eid begins with the morning prayer in large congregations, often in mosques and open grounds filled with families. After the prayer, the visiting begins, and homes keep open doors: relatives, friends and neighbours drop in through the day, and every guest is offered coffee, dates and sweets.

Food is at the heart of the celebration. Each country has its festive dishes, and families take pride in their Eid table. Sweet dishes are especially important — the festival is sometimes called the Sweet Eid — and trays of pastries, filled with nuts or dates, appear in every home. Coffee, served in small cups with cardamom, accompanies every visit.

Children are the centre of attention, dressed in new clothes and collecting Eidi from every relative they visit. In many places the celebrations continue for several days, with families taking turns to host, and the evenings filled with visiting and conversation long after the first day.

South Asia: chaand raat, Eidi and sweet dishes

In South Asia, the celebration begins the night before Eid, with chaand raat — the night of the moon — when the new moon is sighted and the festival is confirmed. Markets and streets fill with last-minute shoppers, women decorate their hands with henna, and the excitement builds through the night.

Eid morning brings the prayer, and then the feasting. Sweet vermicelli, known as sheer khurma, is the signature dish of the day in many homes, served to every guest. Biryani, kebabs and rich curries follow, and the table stays laden all day as relatives arrive. Eidi is given generously to children, often in crisp new notes tucked into envelopes.

Visiting follows a warm pattern: elders first, then relatives and friends, with each visit bringing more food and more greetings. The phrase "Eid Mubarak" is exchanged a hundred times, and the day often ends with the whole extended family gathered for a final meal together.

Southeast Asia: homecoming and open houses

In Southeast Asia, Eid — known locally as Hari Raya in Malaysia and Singapore, and Lebaran in Indonesia — is the great homecoming festival of the year. In the days before Eid, millions of people travel back to their home towns and villages to celebrate with family, in a journey known in Indonesia as mudik. Roads and stations fill, and the homecoming is itself part of the celebration.

The open house is the signature custom of the region. Families open their homes to all comers — relatives, friends, neighbours, colleagues of every background — and every guest is fed. Long tables of festive food, from rice dishes to sweets, are laid out, and the visiting continues for days.

Forgiveness is a special theme of Eid in Southeast Asia. It is customary to ask forgiveness of elders and of anyone you may have wronged, often with a formal greeting and a handshake or an embrace. Children receive money in small green packets, and the festival's warmth extends to the whole community.

Turkey and Central Asia: bayram visits and sweets

In Turkey, Eid al-Fitr is known as Ramazan Bayramı, and it is a holiday of visiting and sweets — so much so that it is also affectionately called Şeker Bayramı, the Sugar Festival. The days are filled with visits to elders, who are greeted with a kiss on the hand, and children collect sweets and money at every stop.

Turkish delight, baklava and other sweets are the currency of the festival, offered to every guest with coffee or tea. Families prepare trays of sweets in advance, and the visiting follows a pattern of respect: the young visit the old, and the old bless the young. Public transport and museums are often busy, as families make the most of the holiday days.

In Central Asia, similar customs hold, with festive meals, visiting, and special dishes prepared for the days of Eid. The celebration blends local tradition with the shared festival, and the emphasis, as everywhere, is on family, food and generosity.

Africa: community prayers and shared meals

Across Africa, Eid al-Fitr is a community celebration, with large outdoor prayers bringing whole towns together. In many places the prayer is held in a central open space, and the congregation — in colourful dress, with children everywhere — is a sight to remember. The prayer is followed by visits, feasting and music.

Food is shared generously, and in many communities the festival meal is a collective affair, with neighbours contributing dishes and eating together. Special dishes vary by region, but the pattern is the same: the best food the household can offer, shared with whoever comes to the door.

New clothes are a much-loved part of Eid in Africa, and markets in the days before the festival are busy with families buying fabric and having outfits made. The colours are bright, the tailoring is a point of pride, and Eid morning is a parade of the community at its finest.

Eid in the West: community halls and public celebrations

In Europe, North America and Australia, Eid al-Fitr is celebrated with a particular warmth, because the festival is a chance for Muslim communities to gather and to share their celebration with neighbours. Mosques and community centres organise large prayers, often in hired halls to fit the crowds, followed by fairs, food stalls and family activities.

Many communities hold public Eid festivals in parks and town centres, with food, crafts, music and activities for children. These festivals are open to everyone, and they have become a joyful part of the local calendar in many cities. Neighbours and colleagues are often invited to share the day.

For families far from their countries of origin, Eid in the West is also about keeping traditions alive: the foods of home, the greetings of childhood, and the stories told to a new generation. The festival adapts to its setting while keeping its heart — prayer, family, generosity and joy — wherever it is celebrated.

Questions people ask

When is Eid al-Fitr 2027?

Eid al-Fitr 2027 is expected on Tuesday 9 March 2027. Islamic dates depend on the sighting of the moon, so the exact date is confirmed closer to the time.

Why do Eid dates differ between countries?

Because the moon is sighted on different days in different places, and because some countries follow local sighting while others follow announcements from elsewhere. The date can differ by a day between countries.

What does Eid Mubarak mean?

Eid Mubarak means Blessed Eid. It is the most common Eid greeting, understood by Muslims almost everywhere in the world.

What is Eidi?

Eidi is money or small gifts given at Eid, most often to children, by parents, relatives and elders. It is one of the most loved traditions of the festival.

What foods are shared at Eid?

Sweet dishes are common almost everywhere, and each region has its own festive foods. Family feasts, open houses and shared meals are part of the celebration in every country.

How do people greet each other at Eid?

Eid Mubarak is understood almost everywhere. Local greetings vary: in Turkey people say Bayramınız mübarek olsun, and in Southeast Asia the greeting often includes asking forgiveness.
