Project Management Learning Hub

Project management is the skill of getting a group of people to deliver something together, on time, without chaos. This hub teaches the three methods you will meet most often — Waterfall, Agile and Kanban — with plain explanations, the key terms, a worked example and questions to check yourself.

Methods are tools, not magic — the best method is the one your team will actually follow. This hub teaches the ideas; your judgement picks how to use them, and no method can rescue a project with an unclear goal.

What project management is (and is not)

A project is a piece of work with a goal, a start and an end: building a website, launching a product, moving an office. Project management is the work of planning that piece of work, coordinating the people doing it, and steering it to the finish. It is mostly communication — making sure everyone knows what is being built, who is doing what, and what happens next.

What it is not is paperwork for its own sake. Charts and reports are useful only when they help people decide and act. A project manager who spends all day updating a plan that nobody reads is not managing a project; they are decorating one. The methods in this hub all exist to serve the same purpose: help a team agree on the goal, see the work, and finish it together.

Waterfall: plan first, then build

Waterfall is the oldest method and the simplest to picture: the work flows down through stages, one after another. First you gather requirements, then you design, then you build, then you test, then you deliver. Each stage finishes before the next begins, and the plan is agreed up front. It works best when the goal is clear and unlikely to change — building a bridge, for example, or a product with fixed regulations.

Its strength is clarity: everyone knows the plan, the order and the deadline. Its weakness is rigidity: if the goal changes halfway through, going back is expensive, because earlier stages were built on assumptions that no longer hold. Waterfall suits projects where change is costly and the requirements are genuinely stable — and it struggles where the customer is still discovering what they want.

Agile: work in small steps

Agile turns Waterfall around. Instead of planning everything up front, the team works in short cycles called sprints — usually one to four weeks — and delivers a small working piece at the end of each one. After each sprint, the team shows the work, gathers feedback, and plans the next small step. The goal stays in view, but the route to it can change as the team learns.

Agile suits work where the goal is clear but the details are uncertain — most software, most creative work, most new products. Its strength is feedback: problems surface early, when they are cheap to fix. Its weakness is that without discipline it can drift — sprints without a clear goal become busywork. Good Agile teams protect two habits above all: a short planning meeting at the start of each sprint, and an honest review at the end.

Kanban: see the work, limit the load

Kanban is the simplest method of all: a board with columns for the stages of work — to do, doing, done — and a card for each task. The team moves cards across the board as work progresses, and everyone can see the state of the project at a glance. The one rule that makes Kanban work is the limit on work in progress: each column has a maximum number of cards, so the team finishes things before starting new ones.

Kanban suits ongoing work with a steady flow of tasks — support teams, content teams, maintenance work — where sprints would feel forced. Its strength is visibility and flow: bottlenecks show up immediately as a column filling up. Its weakness is that it has no built-in rhythm for planning or review, so teams must add those habits themselves or the board slowly fills with stale cards.

Key terms, plainly explained

Project management has its own vocabulary, and most of it is simpler than it sounds. Learning the terms matters because teams use them as shorthand — if you do not know what a dependency is, you will miss the warning when someone says one is blocked.

  • Scope: what the project will and will not deliver — the boundary of the work.
  • Milestone: a significant point in the schedule, such as a finished design or a launch date.
  • Dependency: a task that cannot start until another task finishes.
  • Stakeholder: anyone affected by the project or able to affect it — customers, managers, users.
  • Backlog: the ordered list of work waiting to be done, most important first.
  • Sprint: a short fixed period in Agile during which the team completes a set of work.
  • Risk: something that might go wrong, with a likelihood and an impact if it does.
  • Retrospective: a meeting at the end of a sprint or project to discuss what went well and what to change.

A worked example: planning a small website project

Imagine a small bakery wants a website with a menu, an order form and a story page, delivered in six weeks. With Waterfall, you would plan everything first: agree the pages, the design and the content in week one, build in weeks two to five, test and launch in week six. The bakery sees the finished site only near the end, so any change of mind late on is expensive.

With Agile, you would split the work into three two-week sprints. Sprint one delivers a simple site with the menu. Sprint two adds the order form. Sprint three adds the story page and polish. The bakery sees real progress every two weeks and can change its mind cheaply. With Kanban, you would put all the tasks on a board and work through them in priority order, limiting the doing column to three cards so nothing is half-finished. All three methods can deliver the same site — the choice is about how much change you expect and how the team likes to work.

Self-check: test what you learned

Answer each question in your head, then read the answer. If you get one wrong, read the lesson again — the answer is always in the lesson above.

  • Question: When does Waterfall work best? Answer: when the goal is clear and unlikely to change, and the cost of going back is high — for example, a building project or a product with fixed regulations.
  • Question: What happens at the end of an Agile sprint? Answer: the team delivers a small working piece, shows it, gathers feedback, and plans the next sprint based on what they learned.
  • Question: What is the one rule that makes Kanban work? Answer: the limit on work in progress. Each column has a maximum number of cards, so the team finishes things before starting new ones.
  • Question: What is a dependency? Answer: a task that cannot start until another task finishes. If a dependency is blocked, everything waiting on it is blocked too.
  • Question: What is a backlog? Answer: the ordered list of work waiting to be done, most important first. The team takes work from the top of the backlog.
  • Question: What is a retrospective for? Answer: it is a meeting to discuss what went well and what to change, so the team improves how it works instead of repeating the same mistakes.

Choosing a method for your project

Choose by asking three questions. First, how clear is the goal? If it is fixed and detailed, Waterfall fits. If it will evolve as you learn, Agile fits. Second, is the work a project with an end, or ongoing work with no finish line? Ongoing work suits Kanban. Third, how does your team like to work? A method the team resists will fail no matter how good it looks on paper.

You can also mix methods honestly: many teams plan in Waterfall style for the big picture and work in Agile sprints for the details, or run Kanban boards inside an Agile project. The method serves the team, not the other way round — borrow what helps, drop what does not, and be honest with the team about what you are doing and why.

Common mistakes beginners make

The most common beginner mistake is planning in too much detail too early. A plan that tries to predict every day of a six-month project will be wrong by week two, and the team will stop trusting it. Plan the near future in detail and the far future in outline, and revisit the plan regularly. A plan is a living guide, not a promise carved in stone.

Other mistakes are just as common: starting work before the goal is agreed, letting the scope grow without saying no, and holding meetings that produce no decisions. Guard against all three with simple habits — write the goal down and get agreement, keep a visible list of what is out of scope, and end every meeting by naming who does what by when.

  • Planning every detail up front instead of planning near work in detail and far work in outline
  • Starting before the goal is agreed and written down
  • Letting scope grow silently instead of deciding what is out of scope
  • Holding meetings that end with no named owner and no date

Questions people ask

What is the difference between Agile and Waterfall?

Waterfall plans everything up front and builds in stages, one after another. Agile works in short cycles, delivering a small working piece each time and adapting the plan as the team learns. Waterfall suits stable goals; Agile suits goals that evolve.

What is Kanban best for?

Kanban suits ongoing work with a steady flow of tasks, such as support or content work. A board shows every task's stage at a glance, and a limit on work in progress stops the team starting more than it can finish.

Do I need a certificate to manage projects?

No. Certificates can help a career, but the skill itself comes from practice: planning real work, coordinating real people and learning from real mistakes. Start by managing a small project well, and let the experience build from there.

What is a sprint in Agile?

A sprint is a short fixed period — usually one to four weeks — during which a team completes a set of work and delivers a small working piece. Each sprint ends with a review of the work and a retrospective on how the team worked.

How do I stop a project from growing out of control?

Write down the scope — what the project will and will not deliver — and agree it with everyone involved. When new requests arrive, judge them against the scope and decide openly whether to add them, swap them for something else, or say no.

What is the most important habit for a new project manager?

Ending every meeting with named owners and dates. A decision without an owner is a wish, and a task without a date is a hope. Write down who does what by when, and follow up on it.
