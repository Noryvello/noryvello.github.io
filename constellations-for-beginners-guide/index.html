Constellations for Beginners Guide

A constellation is a pattern of stars that people have named and used to find their way around the night sky for thousands of years. This guide covers eight of the easiest constellations to find, including the Plough and Orion, and shows how the Plough's pointer stars lead to the North Star.

On a clear dark night, the sky is full of stars, and at first they all look alike. Constellations are the way in. They are patterns of bright stars that are easy to recognise once someone points them out, and they work like landmarks: learn a few, and the whole sky starts to make sense.

This guide covers eight constellations that are easy to find from northern mid-latitudes, with tips for spotting each one, the seasons when they are easiest to see, and a dark-sky checklist to help you get started. No telescope is needed: every constellation here is visible with the naked eye.

What a constellation is

A constellation is a pattern of stars that people have grouped together and named. The stars in a constellation are not necessarily near each other in space: they only look close together because we see them from Earth, lined up in the same direction. Some stars in a pattern may be far closer to us than others.

Astronomers recognise 88 official constellations, which divide the whole sky between them, like countries on a map. Every star in the sky belongs to one of them. The eight in this guide are the easiest to start with, because they are bright, distinctive, and visible for much of the year from northern mid-latitudes.

Constellations are for finding your way around the sky. They are not horoscopes, and they say nothing about personality or the future. This guide treats them as star patterns and signposts, which is what they have been for sailors, farmers and travellers for thousands of years.

Start with the Plough and the North Star

The best place to begin is the Plough, also called the Big Dipper, which is part of the constellation Ursa Major, the Great Bear. Seven bright stars form the shape of a saucepan, and it is one of the most recognisable patterns in the northern sky. Once you have found it, you have a starting point for the rest of the sky.

The two stars at the front of the pan, Dubhe and Merak, are called the pointer stars. Draw a line through them, going away from the pan, and follow it for about five times the distance between the two stars. You arrive at Polaris, the North Star, which sits at the end of the handle of the Little Dipper, the constellation Ursa Minor.

Polaris is special because it sits almost directly above the North Pole. As the Earth spins, the other stars appear to circle around it, but Polaris itself barely moves. It always marks north, which is why sailors and travellers have used it to navigate for centuries. Finding Polaris from the Plough is the single most useful trick in beginner stargazing.

On the opposite side of Polaris from the Plough sits Cassiopeia, five bright stars in the shape of a wide W. If the Plough is low in the sky, Cassiopeia is usually high, and the two constellations take turns around the pole through the night and through the year. Between the Plough, Polaris and Cassiopeia, you can always find your bearings.

The seasons of the sky

The constellations you can see change through the year, because the Earth orbits the Sun and the night side of the Earth faces different parts of the sky in different seasons. A constellation that is high in the evening sky in winter will be up in the daytime in summer, and invisible.

Orion is the great winter constellation. Its three-star belt is unmistakable, and it dominates the evening sky in the winter months. Follow the belt downwards and you reach Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, in the constellation Canis Major, the Great Dog, which follows Orion across the sky like a hunting dog following its master.

Leo is the spring constellation, with a backwards question mark of stars called the Sickle forming the Lion's head and chest. Lyra and Cygnus are summer constellations: Lyra holds the bright star Vega, and Cygnus is a long cross flying along the Milky Way, with the bright star Deneb at its tail. Vega, Deneb and Altair, in the constellation Aquila, form the Summer Triangle, a huge triangle that is easy to spot on a summer night.

Some constellations never set. The Plough, the Little Dipper and Cassiopeia circle the pole without ever dropping below the horizon from northern mid-latitudes, so they are visible all year round. Astronomers call them circumpolar constellations, and they are the reliable landmarks of the northern sky.

Your first night out

You do not need any equipment to start. The naked eye is enough for every constellation in this guide, and a pair of ordinary binoculars will show you more stars inside the patterns once you know where to look. A telescope comes later, if you want one: learn the sky first, and the telescope will be far more rewarding.

Darkness matters more than equipment. Town lights wash out all but the brightest stars, so the further you get from street lights, the more you will see. Give your eyes time to adjust to the dark: it takes around twenty minutes for your night vision to reach its best, and a glance at a bright phone screen will undo it. A dim red light, or a phone screen turned to its lowest brightness, is the kindest way to check a star chart.

Start with the Plough and Orion, which are the easiest patterns to find, and build out from there. Learn one new constellation each time you go out, and within a few months you will know your way around the sky in every season. Questions people ask

What is a constellation?

A constellation is a pattern of stars that people have grouped together and named. Astronomers recognise 88 official constellations, which divide the whole sky between them like countries on a map.

How do I find the North Star?

Find the Plough, then follow the line through its two pointer stars, Dubhe and Merak, for about five times the distance between them. That line leads to Polaris, the North Star, at the end of the Little Dipper's handle.

Why do different constellations appear in different seasons?

Because the Earth orbits the Sun, the night side of the Earth faces different parts of the sky through the year. Orion is a winter constellation, Leo is spring, and Lyra and Cygnus are summer.

Do I need a telescope to see constellations?

No. Every constellation in this guide is visible with the naked eye on a clear dark night. Binoculars will show more stars inside the patterns once you know where to look.

What is the Summer Triangle?

The Summer Triangle is a large triangle formed by three bright stars: Vega in Lyra, Deneb in Cygnus, and Altair in Aquila. It is easy to spot on a summer evening and is a good starting point for summer stargazing.
