House Floor Plan Directory

This directory describes the most common house floor plan types, organised by number of bedrooms and by style. For each type you will find the usual room count, the typical layout, and the honest pros and cons.

This directory describes common floor plan types in general terms. Room sizes, room counts and layouts vary by country, builder and era, so always check the actual plans before making a decision.

How to choose a floor plan

A floor plan is the map of a home: it shows where the rooms are, how they connect, and how the space is used. Two homes with the same number of bedrooms can feel very different, because the plan decides how light moves through the rooms, where the noise goes, and how easy it is to live in the space day to day.

Start with the way you live, not with the number on the listing. Who sleeps at home, who works at home, who visits, and what do you do in the evenings? A family that cooks together needs a kitchen that fits more than one person. A person who works from home needs a quiet corner with a door. A household with older relatives needs a bedroom and a bathroom on the ground floor. The best plan is the one that fits your days, not the one with the longest list of rooms.

This directory covers the common plan types by bedroom count and by style. For each type, read the typical layout, the pros and the cons, and think about how the plan would feel on an ordinary weekday. Remember that plans vary by country and by builder: the names in this directory are the common ones, but the details always depend on the actual home.

One-bedroom and two-bedroom plans

One-bedroom. A one-bedroom plan usually has one bedroom, one bathroom, a kitchen and a living area, and sometimes a small dining space. The layout is usually simple: the bedroom sits at one end of the home for privacy, and the living area and kitchen share the other end. One-bedroom homes suit single people and couples who want low running costs and little upkeep. The trade-off is space: there is no spare room for guests, no home office, and little room for storage, so good built-in cupboards matter more than in a larger home.

Two-bedroom. A two-bedroom plan adds a second bedroom and often a second bathroom or a shared family bathroom. The second bedroom is usually smaller than the main bedroom and works well as a guest room, a child's room, or a home office. Many two-bedroom plans put the bedrooms on opposite sides of the living area, which gives each bedroom privacy, or group them together on one side with the living space on the other. Two-bedroom homes are a common choice for small families and for couples who want a spare room without the cost of a large house.

  • Pros: lower cost to buy and to heat; less cleaning and upkeep; easier to furnish.
  • Cons: limited storage; no room to grow; guests share the living space.

Three-bedroom plans

Three-bedroom. The three-bedroom plan is the most common family layout in many countries. It usually has a main bedroom with its own bathroom, two further bedrooms, a family bathroom, a kitchen, and a living room, and often a separate dining room or a combined kitchen and dining space. Some three-bedroom plans add a small study, a utility room, or a garage with storage.

Layouts vary. In one common arrangement, all three bedrooms sit on one floor or one side of the house, with the living rooms on the other side. In another, the main bedroom is separated from the other two, which suits families with older children or with a guest who stays often. When you look at a three-bedroom plan, check the size of the second and third bedrooms: in some plans they are generous, and in others they fit little more than a single bed and a wardrobe.

  • Pros: room for a family, guests, or a home office; easy to sell later, because demand is strong.
  • Cons: higher running costs than a smaller home; the smallest bedroom can be very small.

Four-bedroom and larger plans

Four-bedroom. A four-bedroom plan usually has a main bedroom with its own bathroom, three further bedrooms, at least two bathrooms, a kitchen, a living room, and a dining room, with room for a study, a utility room, or a playroom. Five-bedroom plans add another bedroom and often another bathroom, and sometimes a second living room or a separate wing for guests or older relatives.

Large plans need careful thought about how the space is used. A home with many rooms can still feel cramped if the rooms are small, and a home with few rooms can feel generous if the rooms are large and well lit. Check where the bedrooms sit: in a good large plan, the main bedroom is quiet and private, the children's bedrooms are near a bathroom, and there is a living area where the whole household can gather.

  • Pros: space for a large family, guests, and work; flexible use of rooms.
  • Cons: higher purchase price and running costs; more cleaning and maintenance; rooms can be underused.

Open-plan living

Open-plan. Open-plan living removes most of the walls between the kitchen, the dining area and the living room, creating one large shared space. The idea is that the household can cook, eat, talk and relax in the same room, and that light can travel across the whole space instead of being trapped in small rooms.

Open plans suit households that spend time together and entertain often. They make a small home feel larger, and they let a parent in the kitchen see children playing in the living area. The trade-offs are real: cooking smells and noise travel everywhere, there is nowhere to hide a messy kitchen, and the heating bill can be higher because one large space is harder to warm than separate rooms. Some homes solve this with a sliding door or a partial wall, so the space can be opened or closed as needed.

  • Pros: bright, social, flexible space; a small home feels larger.
  • Cons: noise and cooking smells travel; no hiding place for mess; harder to heat.

Single-storey plans: ranch and bungalow

Ranch. A ranch plan, also called a rambler in some places, is a long, low, single-storey home with all the rooms on one level. The living rooms usually sit in the middle, with bedrooms at one end and the garage at the other. Ranch homes often have a wide frontage and a simple roofline, and they suit flat plots of land.

Bungalow. A bungalow is also single-storey, but it is usually smaller and more compact than a ranch, with a cosy, cottage-like feel. Some bungalows have a loft or a half-storey under the roof for extra space. Single-storey plans suit anyone who wants to avoid stairs: older people, people with mobility problems, and families with small children. The trade-off is land: a single-storey home needs a larger plot for the same floor area, and the rooms can feel spread out.

  • Pros: no stairs; easy to move around; safe for children and older people.
  • Cons: needs a larger plot; rooms can feel spread out; less privacy between rooms.

Two-storey and split-level plans

Two-storey. A two-storey plan puts the living rooms on the ground floor and the bedrooms upstairs. This is the classic family layout: the day happens downstairs and the night happens upstairs, and the separation gives both privacy and quiet. Two-storey homes use land efficiently, because the same footprint holds twice the floor area.

Split-level. A split-level plan divides the home into two or three levels, each half a flight of stairs apart: often the living room on one level, the kitchen and dining room half a level up, and the bedrooms half a level above that. Split-level homes suit sloping plots, because the plan follows the slope of the land. The trade-off is stairs everywhere: every move through the house means a short flight, which suits active households but not everyone.

  • Pros: efficient use of land; clear separation of living and sleeping areas.
  • Cons: stairs on every trip; split-level plans have stairs between every area.

Small and compact plans: cottage, townhouse and duplex

Cottage. A cottage plan is a small, simple home, usually one or two bedrooms, with a compact kitchen and a living room built around a fireplace or a stove. Cottages are cosy and cheap to run, and they suit single people, couples, and holiday homes. The trade-off is space: storage is limited and there is rarely room for guests.

Townhouse. A townhouse, also called a terraced house, shares walls with the homes on either side and is usually two or three storeys tall. The living rooms sit on the ground floor, the bedrooms upstairs, and a small garden or yard sits at the back. Townhouses suit towns and cities, where land is expensive: they use a small plot well and cost less than a detached home of the same size.

Duplex. A duplex is a building divided into two homes, side by side or one above the other, each with its own entrance. A duplex suits two households that want to live close together — two generations of one family, for example — or an owner who wants to live in one half and rent the other. The trade-off is shared walls and shared outdoor space.

  • Pros: affordable; efficient use of land; low upkeep.
  • Cons: shared walls mean shared noise; limited outdoor space; little room to extend.

What to check before you choose

Before you commit to a plan, walk through it in your mind on an ordinary day. Where do you put the shopping when you come in? Where do the children do their homework? Where does the washing dry? Is there a bathroom near the bedrooms, and a toilet near the living room? Is there storage near the front door, in the bedrooms, and in the kitchen? Small answers to small questions decide whether a plan works.

Check the light. Rooms that face the morning sun are pleasant for breakfast; rooms that face the afternoon sun can be hot in summer. Check the noise: bedrooms next to a busy road or above a living room will hear more than bedrooms at the back of the house. Check the storage: a home without enough cupboards will always feel untidy, however beautiful the rooms are.

Finally, remember that a plan on paper is not a home. Visit the home if you can, at different times of day, and stand in each room. A plan that looks generous on paper can feel tight in person, and a plan that looks plain can feel calm and easy to live in. The right plan is the one that fits your life, not the one that impresses on first sight.

Questions people ask

How many bedrooms do I need?

It depends on who lives in the home and how the rooms are used. A spare bedroom can be a guest room, a child's room, or a home office, so think about the rooms you need, not just the number on the listing.

What is an open-plan layout?

Open-plan living removes most of the walls between the kitchen, the dining area and the living room, creating one large shared space. It makes a home feel larger and brighter, but cooking smells and noise travel everywhere.

What is the difference between a ranch and a bungalow?

Both are single-storey homes with all rooms on one level. A ranch is usually long and wide with the living rooms in the middle, while a bungalow is usually smaller and more compact, with a cosy, cottage-like feel.

What is a split-level home?

A split-level home divides the rooms into two or three levels, each half a flight of stairs apart. The layout suits sloping plots, but every move through the house means a short flight of stairs.

Should I choose a one-storey or two-storey home?

A one-storey home avoids stairs and suits anyone with mobility needs or small children, but it needs a larger plot. A two-storey home uses land efficiently and separates sleeping from living, but every trip upstairs means climbing.

What should I check in a floor plan before buying?

Walk through an ordinary day in your mind: storage near the door, a bathroom near the bedrooms, light in the rooms you use most, and noise from the road or the living room. Visit the home at different times of day if you can.
