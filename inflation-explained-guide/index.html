Inflation Explained

Inflation is a general rise in prices over time, which means the same amount of money buys less than it used to. It is measured by tracking the price of a typical basket of goods and services, and it touches everyone who spends or saves.

Most people meet inflation at the shops: the same basket of groceries costs a little more than it did last year. That slow creep is inflation — not one item going up in price, but the general level of prices rising over time.

What inflation means

Inflation is a general rise in prices over time. When inflation is happening, the same amount of money buys less than it used to — a loaf, a bus ticket and a haircut all cost a little more than they did a year ago. The money itself has not changed; what it can buy has shrunk.

The key word is general. One item going up in price is not inflation — that can happen for all sorts of reasons, such as a bad harvest or a new model. Inflation is when prices across the economy are rising together, so the buying power of money falls overall.

The opposite is also possible. When the general level of prices falls, that is deflation, and money buys more over time. Deflation is rarer than inflation, and it brings its own problems, because people may put off spending in the hope that prices will fall further.

How inflation is measured

Inflation is measured by tracking the price of a typical basket of goods and services over time. The basket holds the things an average household buys: food, housing, transport, clothing, healthcare and more. Statisticians check the price of each item regularly and compare it with the price before.

The result is usually expressed as a rate — a number that says how much the general level of prices has risen over a year. If the rate is low, prices are rising slowly; if it is high, they are rising fast. The rate is an average, so it will not match any one household's experience exactly.

Different countries measure inflation in slightly different ways, with different baskets and different methods. The idea is the same everywhere: follow a basket of typical spending and watch how its price changes. The rate that comes out is a guide to the trend, not a precise measure of anyone's own costs.

Why prices rise

Prices rise for several reasons, often at once. One is rising costs: when wages, materials or energy get more expensive, businesses pass some of that cost on in higher prices. If the cost of making and moving things goes up, the price on the shelf usually follows.

Another reason is strong demand. When many people want the same things at the same time — a popular product, a busy holiday season, a growing economy — sellers can raise prices and buyers will still pay. When demand is weak, the opposite happens, and sellers may cut prices to attract buyers.

Expectations matter too. If people expect prices to rise, workers ask for higher pay and businesses raise prices in advance, which can turn the expectation into reality. That is one reason central banks and governments watch inflation closely: once it becomes expected, it is harder to bring back down.

What inflation means for savings

Inflation quietly shrinks what savings can buy. Money kept in a drawer buys less each year, because prices rise while the money stays the same. Money in a savings account grows with interest, but if prices rise faster than the interest, the savings still lose buying power.

The effect is small in any one year but large over many years, because it compounds: each year's rise builds on the last. Questions people ask

What is the difference between inflation and a price rise?

A price rise is one item costing more. Inflation is the general level of prices rising together, so the same money buys less overall. One item going up is not inflation on its own.

Why does inflation happen?

Prices rise when costs rise, when demand is strong, and when people expect prices to rise. Often several of these reasons act together, which is why inflation is hard to pin to a single cause.

Is inflation always bad?

A low, steady rate of inflation is a normal part of most economies. The problems come when inflation is high or unpredictable, because then it is hard for people and businesses to plan.

What does inflation do to savings?

It shrinks what savings can buy. Money that earns less than the rate of price rises loses buying power over time, even though the amount itself does not fall.

Can inflation go down?

Yes. The rate of inflation can fall when cost pressures ease or demand weakens. Prices themselves usually keep rising, just more slowly — a falling rate means slower rises, not falling prices.
