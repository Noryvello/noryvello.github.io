Why Is the Sky Blue, Explained

The sky looks blue because air scatters the blue part of sunlight far more than the red part. Sunlight is a mix of colours, and as it passes through the air, tiny air molecules bounce the short blue light waves in all directions, so blue light reaches our eyes from every part of the sky.

Sunlight looks white, and air looks clear, so a blue sky is a small puzzle. The answer is that sunlight is not really white: it is a mix of all the colours of the rainbow, and air treats those colours differently. The blue part gets scattered in every direction, and that scattered blue light is what we see as the sky.

The effect is called Rayleigh scattering, after the physicist Lord Rayleigh, who described it in the 19th century. It explains not only the blue sky but also red sunsets, the pale sky near the horizon, and why the sky looks darker from a mountain top. One simple idea covers all of it.

Sunlight is a mix of colours

White light is not a single colour. It is all the colours of the rainbow mixed together, and a prism or a rainbow shows them separated: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo and violet. Each colour is light with a different wavelength. Red light has long waves, and blue and violet light have short waves.

When sunlight enters the atmosphere, it meets air molecules that are far smaller than the waves of light. Those tiny molecules scatter the light, bouncing it in all directions, but they do not scatter every colour equally. Short waves are scattered far more strongly than long waves, so blue light is scattered much more than red light.

The scattered blue light goes in every direction, including down towards us. Wherever you look in a clear daytime sky, some of that scattered blue light is coming towards your eyes, so the whole sky looks blue. The red and orange light is scattered much less, so most of it travels straight on, and the Sun itself still looks white or yellowish.

Why blue and not violet

Violet light has even shorter waves than blue light, so it is scattered even more strongly. That raises an obvious question: why is the sky not violet? There are two reasons. First, much of the violet light is absorbed high in the atmosphere before it can be scattered towards us. Second, our eyes are less sensitive to violet than to blue, so even the violet light that does reach us makes less of an impression.

Blue is the colour that survives both filters. It is scattered strongly, it is not absorbed as much as violet, and our eyes see it well. The result is the blue we know. If our eyes worked differently, the sky might look a different colour entirely, which is a good reminder that colour is partly about the light and partly about the eye that sees it.

Why sunsets are red

At noon, sunlight takes the shortest path through the atmosphere to reach us, straight down through the air overhead. At sunset, the Sun is low, and its light has to travel through a much longer stretch of air to reach our eyes. Along that long path, almost all the blue light is scattered away in other directions, and by the time the light reaches us, mostly reds and oranges are left.

That is why the sky around a setting Sun glows red, orange and pink. The colours are deepest when the air holds dust, smoke or other small particles, which scatter the light even more. That is why sunsets after a volcanic eruption or a dusty day can be especially vivid, with the whole western sky turning red.

The same effect colours the Moon when it is low in the sky. Moonlight passing through a long stretch of air loses its blue part, leaving the warm orange tones of a rising Moon. It is the same scattering at work, seen on a different light.

Why the sky changes with altitude

The blueness of the sky depends on how much air is doing the scattering. At sea level, there is a deep layer of air above us, and the scattered blue light is bright. Climb a high mountain and there is less air above you, so less blue light is scattered towards your eyes, and the sky looks a deeper, darker blue.

Keep going up and the effect continues. From a high-flying aeroplane, the sky overhead is a noticeably darker blue than it is from the ground. From space, there is no air at all, so there is no scattering, and the sky is black even in full daylight. Astronauts see the Sun shining in a black sky, with stars visible beside it.

The sky near the horizon looks paler than the sky overhead for a similar reason. Light from near the horizon has passed through more air, and more of its blue has been scattered away, so the horizon sky looks washed out and pale compared with the deep blue overhead.

Why clouds are white

Clouds are made of water droplets and ice crystals that are much bigger than air molecules. Because the droplets are large compared with the waves of light, they scatter all colours of light roughly equally, instead of favouring blue. All the colours mixed together make white, so clouds look white.

Thick clouds look grey for a different reason. They are so dense that little light gets through to the bottom of the cloud, so the underside is in shadow and looks grey. The top of the same cloud, lit by direct sunlight, is still bright white. A cloud's colour is about how much light reaches it and how much gets through, not about the cloud itself changing colour.

Smoke and haze sit between the two cases. Their particles are bigger than air molecules but smaller than cloud droplets, so they scatter light in a way that washes out the blue and gives the sky a pale, milky look. That is why the sky looks hazy and less blue on a hot, dusty day.

This guide gives the standard explanation in simplified form. Real atmospheric optics includes more detail, such as the way larger particles scatter light differently, but the scattering of blue light by air molecules is the main reason the daytime sky looks blue.

Questions people ask

Why is the sky blue instead of another colour?

Air molecules scatter short blue light waves far more strongly than long red waves, so blue light reaches our eyes from every part of the sky. Violet is scattered even more but is absorbed high in the atmosphere and our eyes are less sensitive to it.

Why are sunsets red?

At sunset, sunlight travels through much more air, and almost all the blue light is scattered away along the way. Only the reds and oranges are left, so the sky around the setting Sun glows red and orange.

Why is the sky darker blue on a mountain?

There is less air above you at altitude, so less blue light is scattered towards your eyes. The sky looks a deeper, darker blue, and from space, with no air at all, the sky is black.

Why are clouds white if the sky is blue?

Cloud droplets are much bigger than air molecules, so they scatter all colours of light roughly equally. All the colours mixed together make white. Thick clouds look grey because little light reaches their undersides.

What is Rayleigh scattering?

Rayleigh scattering is the scattering of light by particles much smaller than the wavelength of the light, such as air molecules. It scatters short blue waves far more than long red waves, and it is named after the physicist Lord Rayleigh, who described it in the 19th century.
