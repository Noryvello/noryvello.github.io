How Rainbows Form, Explained

A rainbow forms when sunlight enters raindrops, bends and splits into colours, reflects off the back of each drop, and bends again as it leaves. Each colour leaves the drop at a slightly different angle, and the drops that send coloured light to your eyes sit in an arc across the sky.

A rainbow looks like a painted arch in the sky, but it is really an optical trick played by sunlight and rain. There is nothing solid out there: a rainbow is light, bent and reflected inside millions of raindrops, and it exists only for the person looking at it. Move, and the rainbow moves with you.

The whole effect comes from three simple steps that happen inside each raindrop: sunlight bends as it enters, reflects off the back of the drop, and bends again as it leaves. Each step is simple, and together they make one of the most beautiful sights in nature.

What happens inside a raindrop

Sunlight looks white, but it is a mix of colours, and a raindrop works like a tiny prism. When sunlight enters a raindrop, it slows down and bends, a process called refraction. The bending separates the colours, because each colour bends by a slightly different amount: red bends least, violet bends most.

Once inside the drop, the light hits the back of the drop and reflects, bouncing back like light off a mirror. Then it reaches the front of the drop and bends again as it leaves, separating the colours a little more. By the time the light reaches your eye, the colours have spread out into a band.

Each raindrop sends you only one colour, depending on its position in the sky relative to you and the Sun. Drops higher in the sky send you red light, and drops lower down send you violet. Millions of drops together build the full band of colour, with red on the outside of the arc and violet on the inside.

Why a rainbow is an arc

The colours reach your eyes at fixed angles from the point directly opposite the Sun, which is called the antisolar point. Red light comes to you at an angle of about 42 degrees from that point, and violet at a slightly smaller angle. Every drop that sits at the right angle sends you its colour, and the drops at a fixed angle around a point form a circle.

A rainbow is really a full circle of light, but we almost always see only the top half of it. The ground cuts off the bottom, because the drops that would form the lower half of the circle are below the horizon. From a high place, such as a mountain top or an aeroplane, where the ground no longer blocks the view, you can sometimes see the full circle.

The arc is always opposite the Sun, which is why you can never see a rainbow at midday when the Sun is high. The higher the Sun, the lower the arc, and once the Sun climbs above about 42 degrees, the whole rainbow sinks below the horizon. That is why rainbows are morning and late-afternoon sights.

The order of the colours

A rainbow's colours run from red on the outside of the arc to violet on the inside: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo and violet. That list of seven colours is the traditional one, but the rainbow itself is a continuous band, and the colours blend into each other without sharp edges. The number seven is a convention, not a count of separate bands.

Red sits on the outside because red light bends least inside the drop, so it leaves the drop at the widest angle. Violet bends most, so it leaves at the narrowest angle and sits on the inside of the arc. The other colours fall in between, in order of how much they bend.

The brightness of the colours depends on the size of the raindrops. Large drops make bright, sharply defined rainbows. Very small drops, such as fine drizzle, make pale, washed-out rainbows, because the colours overlap and blur together.

Double rainbows

Sometimes a second, fainter rainbow appears outside the first one. That is a double rainbow, and it happens when sunlight reflects twice inside the raindrops instead of once. The second reflection costs light, so the outer bow is always fainter than the main one.

The double rainbow also has its colours in reverse order, with red on the inside and violet on the outside. The second reflection flips the order, which is a neat way to tell the two bows apart. The space between the two bows often looks darker than the rest of the sky, because the drops there send no rainbow light to your eyes at all.

Rainbows can also form in moonlight, though they are faint and usually look white to the eye, because our eyes do not see colour well in dim light. A camera with a long exposure can reveal the colours of a moonlit rainbow, which is sometimes called a moonbow.

The best conditions for a rainbow

A rainbow needs three things at once: the Sun behind you and low in the sky, rain falling in front of you, and a clear view between you and the rain. That combination is most common just after a shower passes, when the Sun comes out while rain is still falling in the distance.

A dark sky behind the rain makes the colours stand out, which is why the brightest rainbows appear against a dark storm cloud. Morning rainbows appear in the west, because the Sun is in the east, and evening rainbows appear in the east, because the Sun is setting in the west.

There is no pot of gold and no end to reach. A rainbow depends on your position relative to the Sun and the rain, so if you walk towards it, it moves with you, always keeping the same angle. Two people standing apart see slightly different rainbows, made by different raindrops. Each rainbow is personal.

A rainbow is light, not an object, and it has no end you can reach. Never look directly at the Sun while searching for one, and if the storm that made the rain is still close, stay sheltered until the thunder has passed.

Questions people ask

How does a rainbow form?

Sunlight enters a raindrop and bends, splitting into colours, then reflects off the back of the drop and bends again as it leaves. Millions of drops together build the band of colour we see as a rainbow.

Why is a rainbow shaped like an arc?

The colours reach your eyes at a fixed angle of about 42 degrees from the point opposite the Sun, and drops at a fixed angle form a circle. The ground cuts off the bottom half, so we see an arc.

Why do double rainbows have reversed colours?

A double rainbow forms when sunlight reflects twice inside the raindrops. The second reflection flips the colour order, so red is on the inside and violet on the outside, and the outer bow is fainter.

When is the best time to see a rainbow?

When the Sun is low behind you and rain is falling in front of you, usually just after a shower in the morning or late afternoon. A dark sky behind the rain makes the colours stand out.

Can you reach the end of a rainbow?

No. A rainbow depends on your position relative to the Sun and the rain, so it moves as you move. Two people standing apart see slightly different rainbows.
