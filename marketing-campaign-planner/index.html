Marketing Campaign Planner

This planner turns one goal, one audience and one budget into a dated week-by-week campaign plan with real tasks. You pick up to three channels, and it splits your budget between them and tells you what to do each week, from the first post to the final review.

This planner gives you a plan, not guaranteed sales. It cannot promise how many people will buy, click or sign up — those results depend on your offer, your price and your audience. Use the plan as a starting point, then measure what actually happens and change the plan as you learn.

How to use this planner

Start by choosing one goal. The planner asks for a single goal on purpose, because a campaign that tries to do everything at once usually does nothing well. Then describe your audience in one line — who they are and what they care about — and enter your total budget as a plain number. The budget can be any amount, including zero: a campaign with no money still needs a plan, and the planner works just as well for it.

Next, pick up to three channels. The planner lets you choose three at most, and the guide below explains why. Then choose how many weeks the campaign will run and a start date. Tap Build my campaign plan and you will get a dated plan: every week has a focus, a start date, and a short list of tasks. The budget is split evenly across your channels, and the last week is always a review week, because a campaign you never measure teaches you nothing.

When the plan looks right, tap Copy my plan to keep it as plain text you can paste into your notes or send to a teammate. Your inputs are saved on this device only, so if you come back tomorrow the planner will remember what you entered. Nothing you type is sent anywhere — the whole plan is built in your browser.

How to set one goal

A campaign goal is a single sentence that says what success looks like. Good goals name one result and one number: sell 50 cakes in four weeks, get 100 people to sign up for a class, bring 200 new visitors to the shop. Bad goals name everything at once: more sales and more followers and more awareness and a better reputation. A goal that tries to cover everything cannot be measured, and a goal that cannot be measured cannot be improved.

The number in the goal does not need to be perfect. If you have never run a campaign before, pick a number that feels slightly ambitious but possible, and treat it as a target to learn from rather than a promise to yourself. After the first campaign you will have real numbers, and the second campaign's goal will be much easier to set. The point of the number is to give you something to compare against at the end.

Write the goal down before you pick any channel or spend anything. Every later decision — which channel, what to say, how long to run — is easier when you can ask one question: does this help the goal? If it does not, leave it out. A short campaign with one clear goal beats a long campaign with five vague ones, every time.

How to pick 2 or 3 channels

A channel is a place where your audience already spends time: search results, a social app, email, the street outside your shop, a video site. The temptation is to be everywhere at once, but every channel needs its own message, its own timing and its own attention. Three channels done well reach far more people than ten channels done badly, because each one gets enough of your time to actually work.

Pick channels by asking where your audience already is, not where you wish they were. If your buyers are local, the street and the local groups matter more than a video site. If your buyers compare prices online first, search matters more than posters. If you already have a list of past buyers, email is often the cheapest way to reach them again. And if you are not sure, pick the channel you can keep up with: a channel you post on twice a week beats a channel you forget about after three days.

Two channels is a fine number for a first campaign. Three is the most you should take on unless you have help. The planner enforces this limit on purpose — it will not let you pick a fourth channel, because the plan would only get thinner. If you finish the campaign and want more, add a channel to the next one, with real results to guide you.

How to plan a month of content

Content is what you actually say and show: posts, photos, short videos, messages, flyers. Planning a month of it sounds like a lot, but it becomes simple when you work in themes instead of single posts. Give each week one theme — week one introduces the product, week two shows how it works, week three shares what buyers say, week four reminds people before the offer ends. Then each post is just one small piece of that week's theme.

Write the content in batches. Sit down once a week, write the week's posts in one go, and take the photos you need at the same time. Writing one post at a time, every day, is the fastest way to run out of ideas and give up. A batch of five posts takes less time than five separate posts written on five separate days, because you are already thinking about the theme.

Keep every post short and useful. One post should make one point: one benefit, one photo, one clear next step. If a post tries to say everything about your product, it says nothing clearly. And repeat your best message in different words — most people will see only one or two of your posts, so the people who see the third one are often seeing your message for the first time.

How to measure success

Measuring means comparing what happened with the goal you set at the start. If the goal was 50 sales, count the sales. If the goal was 100 sign-ups, count the sign-ups. Write the number down next to the goal, and then ask the more useful question: where did the result come from? Which channel brought the most buyers? Which post got the most replies? The answer tells you what to do more of next time.

Count only what you can actually see. Sales you can count from your till or your order list. Sign-ups you can count from your form. Messages you can count from your inbox. Do not guess at numbers you cannot see, and do not count followers as success unless followers were the goal — a thousand new followers and no sales is not a successful sales campaign, however good it looks.

Measure once a week during the campaign, not only at the end. A weekly check takes ten minutes and lets you fix problems early: if one channel is bringing nothing after two weeks, move its remaining budget to the channel that is working. A campaign you adjust halfway is worth far more than a perfect plan you never look at again.

Worked example: a small shop launches a new product

Maria runs a small bakery and wants to sell a new cake she created. Her goal: sell 60 of the new cakes in four weeks. Her audience: people in her neighbourhood who buy cakes for birthdays and weekends. Her budget: 300 in total, and she picks three channels — social media, email to past buyers, and a poster in the shop window. She starts on a Monday.

Week one is preparation. She takes photos of the cake, writes three short posts, and sends an email to past buyers saying the new cake is coming. She puts the poster up. No selling yet — just the announcement. Week two is the launch: the cake goes on sale, she posts a photo of the first one leaving the shop, and she asks the first buyers to tell her what they think.

Week three she shares what buyers said — two short quotes, with their permission — and posts a photo of the cake being made. Week four she reminds people that the launch price ends on Sunday, and on Monday she counts: 54 cakes sold. Slightly under the goal, but she can see that email brought the most buyers and the poster brought almost none. Her next campaign will spend more on email and less on posters — and that is exactly what measuring is for.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is having no goal. A campaign without a goal has no way to decide what to do next, and no way to know when it is done. It drifts until the budget runs out, and then nobody can say whether it worked. The second mistake is every channel at once: ten channels, each getting a few minutes of attention, each too thin to work. Pick two or three and give them real time.

The third mistake is never measuring. If you do not count the result, you cannot learn anything, and the next campaign will repeat the same guesses. The fourth mistake is talking about yourself instead of the buyer: our shop, our history, our passion. Buyers care about what the product does for them, not about your story. The fifth mistake is stopping too early — most campaigns need at least two weeks before the numbers mean anything, because the first week is mostly setup and noise.

The sixth mistake is changing everything at once. If you change the message, the channel and the offer in the same week, you will never know which change made the difference. Change one thing, watch the result, then change the next. Patience is not the exciting part of marketing, but it is the part that makes the next campaign better than the last one.

What this planner cannot do

This planner cannot write your posts for you, take your photos, or talk to your buyers. It gives you the structure — the weeks, the tasks, the budget split — and the words are still yours to write. It cannot choose your audience for you either: if you describe the wrong audience, the plan will be built for the wrong people, and no amount of planning fixes that. Knowing your buyers is work only you can do.

It also cannot predict results. The budget split is even and simple on purpose, because a fair starting split beats a clever guess you cannot explain. As you measure real results, you should move money toward the channels that work — the planner will not do that for you, because only your own numbers can say which channel deserves more. And it cannot run the campaign while you are away: the plan only works if someone does the tasks, week by week.

Questions people ask

What is a marketing campaign?

A marketing campaign is a planned set of messages with one goal, one audience and a fixed time. It is different from everyday posting because it has a clear purpose — sell a certain number, get a certain number of sign-ups, or reach a certain group — and a clear end date, so you can measure whether it worked.

How long should a campaign run?

Two to four weeks is a good length for a first campaign. Shorter than two weeks, and most people will not see your message more than once. Longer than eight weeks, and the message goes stale unless you have fresh content every week. The planner supports two to twelve weeks, and the last week is always a review week.

How much budget do I need to start?

You can start with no money at all — a campaign with no budget still needs a goal, an audience and a plan, and the planner works the same way. If you do have money to spend, start small: a modest budget spread over two or three channels teaches you more than a large budget spent in one place before you know what works.

Which channels should a beginner pick?

Pick the channels where your audience already spends time, and pick at most three. For a local shop, that often means a social app plus a poster or a local group. For an online seller, it often means search plus one social app. If you already have past buyers you can contact, email is usually the cheapest channel of all.

How do I know if the campaign worked?

Compare the result with the goal you set at the start. If the goal was a number of sales or sign-ups, count them. Then look at which channel brought the most, and which message got the most replies. A campaign worked if it hit the goal — and even one that missed the goal worked if it taught you what to change next time.

Can I run a campaign with no money?

Yes. A no-money campaign uses the channels that cost nothing: your own social pages, messages to past buyers, a poster in your own window, and asking happy buyers to tell their friends. The plan matters just as much, because your time is the budget — and the planner helps you spend that time on the tasks that matter most.
