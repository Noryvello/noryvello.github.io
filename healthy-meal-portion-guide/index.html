Healthy Meal Portion Guide

This guide explains healthy meal portions in plain language, using the plate method and simple hand measures you can use anywhere. It gives general nutrition education only, with portion sizes by food group and practical tips for everyday meals.

This guide gives general nutrition education only — not medical advice. It prescribes no diet and makes no personal recommendations. For advice about your own diet, speak to a qualified professional.

Why portions matter

A portion is the amount of a food that goes on your plate at one meal. Portions have grown over the years: plates are bigger, servings in restaurants are bigger, and packets are bigger. When portions grow, it is easy to eat more than the body needs without noticing, because the plate still looks normal — it is the plate that has changed, not your appetite.

Portion sizes are not about strict rules or weighing every meal. They are about having a simple guide for how much of each kind of food makes a balanced plate. When the balance is right — plenty of vegetables, a moderate amount of protein, and a moderate amount of starchy food — most people find their meals satisfying without needing to count anything.

This guide gives general information only. It does not prescribe a diet, and it does not tell any individual what to eat. People's needs differ by age, size, activity, health and many other things, and this page cannot know yours. Use it to understand portions in general, and speak to a qualified professional for advice about your own diet.

The plate method

The plate method is a simple way to build a balanced meal, and it needs no scales and no counting. Start with a plate — a normal dinner plate, not an oversized one. Fill half of it with vegetables or salad. Fill one quarter with protein foods, such as beans, lentils, eggs, fish, chicken or meat. Fill the remaining quarter with starchy foods, such as rice, bread, pasta or potatoes.

The plate method works because it sets the proportions, not the amounts. A larger person uses a larger plate and a smaller person uses a smaller one, and the balance stays the same. It also works for any cuisine: a plate of rice, vegetables and fish follows the same pattern as a plate of bread, salad and beans. The foods change; the proportions stay.

A drink goes beside the plate, not on it. Water is the simplest choice with a meal. If you have a sauce or a dressing, keep it on the side, so you can see how much you are using. A small amount of fat, such as oil or butter, is part of many meals — the hand measures in the next section show how much that means in practice.

Hand-measure tricks

Your hand is a portion guide you always carry with you, and it has a useful property: it scales with your body. A larger person usually has larger hands and needs larger portions, and a smaller person usually has smaller hands and needs smaller ones. Hand measures are approximate, but they are close enough for everyday meals, and they work anywhere — at home, at work, or in a restaurant.

The four hand measures are simple. Your palm — the flat of your hand without the fingers — is a guide for a portion of protein foods: a piece of fish, chicken or meat about the size and thickness of your palm. Your fist is a guide for a portion of vegetables, or for a serving of fruit. Your cupped hand is a guide for a portion of starchy foods: cooked rice, pasta or potatoes that fit in one cupped hand. Your thumb, from the tip to the first joint, is a guide for a portion of fats: butter, oil, or a spread.

Use the measures as a starting point, not as a law. Some days you will be hungrier than others, and some meals will be built differently — a soup, a stew, or a salad does not sit neatly on a plate. The hand measures are there for the days when you want a quick check, not for every bite of every meal.

Portions by food group

The list below is a general guide to one portion of each food group, using everyday measures. A portion is not a rule — it is a way of picturing how much of each food makes a balanced day.

  • Vegetables: one portion is about a fistful of raw vegetables or salad, or about three heaped tablespoons of cooked vegetables. Most guides suggest several portions a day, of different colours where possible.
  • Fruit: one portion is about a fistful — one medium apple, banana or pear, or a small handful of berries or grapes.
  • Grains and starchy foods: one portion is about a cupped handful of cooked rice or pasta, one medium potato, or one slice of bread.
  • Protein foods: one portion is about a palm-sized piece of fish, chicken or meat, one egg, or about three tablespoons of cooked beans or lentils.
  • Dairy and alternatives: one portion is about a small glass of milk, a small pot of yoghurt, or a piece of cheese about the size of your thumb.
  • Fats and oils: one portion is about a thumb-tip of butter or oil — a small amount, because fats are dense in energy.
  • Drinks: water is the simplest drink with a meal. Sweet drinks add energy without adding fullness, so many guides suggest keeping them occasional.
  • Snacks: a snack is a small amount between meals — a piece of fruit, a small handful of nuts, or a small pot of yoghurt — not a second meal.

These are general guides, not personal targets. The right amount for any person depends on their age, size, activity and health, and this page cannot know those things. If you want advice about your own portions, speak to a qualified professional.

Portions when eating out

Restaurant portions are often larger than home portions, and the plate is often larger too. A simple approach is to decide before you order: choose the smaller size when one is offered, share a starter, or ask for a box and take half the main course home. None of these needs willpower in the moment, because the decision is made before the food arrives.

Buffets are a special case, because the food is always there and the plate is always empty. A useful approach is to start with the salad and vegetables, then add protein and starch in plate-method proportions, and then stop and wait before deciding whether to go back. A short pause is often enough to tell whether you are still hungry or simply still surrounded by food.

Drinks matter too. A large sweet drink can add as much energy as part of the meal, without making you feel full. Water with a meal is the simplest choice, and it leaves the flavour of the food to speak for itself.

Portions for packed lunches and snacks

A packed lunch follows the same plate method, in a box instead of on a plate: half vegetables or salad, a quarter protein, and a quarter starchy food. A sandwich can follow the same pattern if it is filled generously with salad and a moderate amount of filling, with fruit on the side.

Snacks are best kept small and planned. A piece of fruit, a small handful of nuts, or a small pot of yoghurt is a snack; a bag of crisps eaten without thinking is a meal in disguise. If you find yourself snacking often, it may be that your meals are too small — or that you are thirsty, tired, or bored rather than hungry.

Children follow the same general pattern, with smaller hands and smaller plates. The plate method and hand measures work for family meals because they scale with the person. Children's needs vary widely by age and activity, so this page gives no specific amounts for children: for individual advice, speak to a qualified professional.

Common portion mistakes

The most common mistake is the plate itself: an oversized plate makes a normal portion look small, and a small plate makes the same portion look generous. If your plates are very large, a normal dinner plate — or even a side plate for some meals — can change how a portion feels without changing the food.

A second mistake is serving from the pot at the table. When the food sits in front of you, a second helping is easy and thoughtless. Serving in the kitchen, and putting the pots away, adds a small pause that is often enough. A third mistake is eating from the packet: without a plate, there is no natural stopping point, and the packet decides the portion instead of you.

A fourth mistake is treating all foods as if they were the same. A fistful of salad and a fistful of rice are very different in energy, even though the volume is the same. The plate method handles this by giving each food group its own share of the plate, so the balance is set by the pattern rather than by guesswork.

Building the habit

Portion habits change slowly, and that is fine. Pick one change at a time: use a smaller plate for a week, or add an extra serving of vegetables to dinner, or put snacks in a small bowl instead of eating from the packet. One change that sticks is worth more than five changes that last a day.

Notice what works. If the plate method makes meals feel easier, keep it. If hand measures help when you eat out, use them. If a change makes meals stressful, drop it: food is meant to be enjoyed, and a guide that makes eating miserable is not doing its job.

This guide is general education, not personal advice. Your body, your health, your culture and your life are your own, and no page can tell you what to eat. Use the plate method and the hand measures as tools, enjoy your food, and speak to a qualified professional if you want advice that fits you.

Questions people ask

What is the plate method?

The plate method is a simple way to build a balanced meal: fill half your plate with vegetables or salad, one quarter with protein foods, and one quarter with starchy foods such as rice, bread or potatoes.

How much protein should be on my plate?

As a general guide, a portion of protein is about the size and thickness of your palm: a piece of fish, chicken or meat, or the equivalent in beans, lentils or eggs. Your own needs vary, so this is a general guide only.

Do I need to weigh my food?

No. The plate method and hand measures are designed to work without scales: your palm, fist, cupped hand and thumb give a quick guide to portions anywhere, at home or when eating out.

What if I am still hungry after a portion?

Hunger varies from day to day, and a single portion is a general guide, not a limit. Many guides suggest waiting a short while after a meal before deciding whether to eat more, since fullness takes time to register. For advice about your own appetite and diet, speak to a qualified professional.

Are hand measures accurate?

Hand measures are approximate, but they are close enough for everyday meals, and they have a useful property: they scale with your body. A larger person usually has larger hands and needs larger portions.

Can children use the plate method?

The plate method works as a general guide for family meals, because hands and plates scale with the person. Children's needs vary widely by age and activity, so for individual advice about a child, speak to a qualified professional.
