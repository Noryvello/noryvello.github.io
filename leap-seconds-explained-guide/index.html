Leap Seconds Explained

A leap second is a single extra second added to the world's clocks to keep them in step with the Earth's rotation. This guide explains why the extra second is needed, how it differs from a leap day, and what the clock shows when one is added.

What a leap second is

A leap second is one extra second, added to the world's clocks at the end of a day. It is the smallest correction timekeeping ever makes: a single second, slipped in so quietly that most people never notice it. Leap seconds are added at the end of June or the end of December, and they are always announced in advance.

When a leap second is added, the last minute of the day has sixty-one seconds instead of sixty. Clocks that show the leap second display 23:59:60 before rolling over to 00:00:00. Most clocks and devices handle this automatically, so the extra second passes without anyone having to do anything.

Why the Earth's rotation needs a nudge

The problem a leap second solves is that there are two different ways of measuring time, and they do not quite agree. Atomic clocks, which keep time by counting the vibrations of atoms, tick with perfect regularity. The Earth, by contrast, does not spin with perfect regularity. Its rotation speeds up and slows down slightly, affected by the Moon, the oceans, the atmosphere and the movement of rock deep inside the planet.

Over months and years, the tiny differences add up. Atomic time and Earth time drift apart, and without a correction the two would slowly separate. A leap second closes the gap. It is added when the drift reaches about nine tenths of a second, keeping the two kinds of time within a second of each other.

A short timeline of the idea

The idea of the leap second grew out of the arrival of atomic clocks in the middle of the twentieth century. Before atomic clocks, time was defined by the Earth's rotation itself, so there was nothing to drift away from. Once clocks could keep time more steadily than the Earth spins, a way was needed to keep the two in step.

The leap-second system began in the early 1970s. In the early decades, leap seconds were added fairly often, sometimes more than once a year. In recent years they have become rarer, because the Earth's rotation has been running slightly faster for a stretch. The pattern is not regular, which is why leap seconds are announced a few months in advance rather than fixed on a schedule.

Leap second vs leap day

A leap second and a leap day both fix a mismatch, but they fix different mismatches. A leap day fixes the calendar: the Earth takes about 365 and a quarter days to orbit the Sun, so an extra day is added to February every four years or so to keep the calendar year in step with the seasons. A leap second fixes the clock: the Earth's spin does not match atomic time exactly, so an extra second is added when the drift needs correcting.

The two are easy to tell apart. A leap day is about the Earth's journey around the Sun, and it follows a fixed rule you can work out in advance. A leap second is about the Earth's spin on its own axis, and it is added only when measurements show it is needed. One adds a day, the other adds a second.

What happens when one is added

For most people, nothing visible happens at all. The extra second passes at midnight, and devices that keep time automatically simply count it. People watching a clock that shows seconds might see 23:59:60 for one second, and that is the whole event. The main work happens behind the scenes, where the organisations that run time signals and computer systems prepare for the extra second in advance.

Leap seconds matter most to systems that depend on precise timing, such as navigation, communications and scientific measurement. For those systems, a second of drift is a real error. For everyday life, a leap second is a quiet piece of maintenance, keeping the clocks we live by honest about the planet we live on.

Why most people never notice

A single second is a tiny thing. A day holds eighty-six thousand four hundred seconds, so one extra second is a change of about one part in eighty-six thousand. Nobody's daily routine is timed that closely, and the leap second is added at midnight, when most of the world is asleep. The result is a correction that matters enormously to precise systems and not at all to everyday life.

The quietness of the leap second is part of its design. It was chosen as the smallest possible correction, added at the quietest possible moment, so that the world's clocks could stay honest without disrupting anyone's day. When you hear that a leap second is coming, you can safely ignore it — your devices will count it for you.

This page explains the idea in general terms and does not list leap-second dates. Leap seconds are announced in advance by the international timekeeping service, and the system itself is under review, so the rules may change in future.

What does the clock show during a leap second?
