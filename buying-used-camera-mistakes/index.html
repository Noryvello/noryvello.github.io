7 Mistakes to Avoid When Buying a Used Camera

A used camera can be a great buy, but only if you check it properly before paying. This guide covers the 7 mistakes buyers most often make, from skipping the sensor check to forgetting the battery, and ends with a tick-off inspection checklist you can use on the day.

A used camera can be the smartest way to buy serious photographic gear. Cameras lose value quickly once they leave the shop, so a year or two of careful use by someone else can save you a large part of the price. But a used camera is also a machine with moving parts, glass and electronics, and its faults are not always visible in a listing photo. The mistakes in this guide are the ones buyers make most often, and most of them come from the same place: trusting the sale instead of testing the camera. Work through the seven mistakes before you meet the seller, then use the inspection checklist at the end while the camera is in your hands.

This guide is general buying advice. It cannot test a camera for you, and it makes no claims about any particular model or seller. If anything feels wrong during an inspection, walk away — another camera will come along.

Mistake 1: Not checking the sensor

The sensor is the most expensive part of a camera to repair or replace, and it is also the part a seller is least likely to mention. Dust and scratches on the sensor show up as spots on every photo, and you will only see them when you look at a test photo at full size. Before you pay, take a photo of a plain bright wall or sky and look at it closely on a screen, not just on the camera's small display. Marks that stay in the same place in every photo are on the sensor. A dusty sensor can be cleaned, but a scratched one is a repair job, and that should change what the camera is worth to you.

Mistake 2: Ignoring the shutter

Every camera shutter has a working life, and a tired shutter can fail without warning in the middle of a shoot. Listen to it fire several times: it should sound even and crisp, with no dragging or double-clicking. If the camera shows a shutter count, ask the seller for it and compare it with what you would expect for the camera's age. A high count is not a reason to walk away on its own, but it is a reason to pay less and to test the camera thoroughly. If the seller cannot or will not tell you the count, treat that as information too.

Mistake 3: Skipping a full lens inspection

Hold each lens up to a bright light and look through it from both ends. Fungus looks like fine threads or spots, haze looks like a soft fog, and scratches catch the light as bright lines. Any of these will soften your photos, and fungus can spread to other lenses stored nearby. Turn the focus ring and the zoom ring through their full range: they should move smoothly, with no grinding or sticking. Check that the aperture blades open and close cleanly, and test autofocus on something near and something far. A lens fault is easy to miss in a quick look and expensive to fix later.

Mistake 4: Forgetting the battery and charger

A camera without a working battery is a paperweight, and old batteries lose their ability to hold a charge. Ask the seller to charge the battery fully before you meet, and check how the camera reports its health if it shows that information. Make sure the charger is included and that it actually charges: a missing charger is an extra cost, and some chargers are hard to find for older models. If the camera takes a battery grip or a second battery, check those too. Factor the cost of a replacement battery into the price if the one included is clearly tired.

Mistake 5: Not testing every button, dial and port

In a quick demo you will use the shutter button and maybe one dial. The faults hide in the controls you never touch. Press every button, turn every dial, and flip every switch, including the ones you do not plan to use. Open the memory card door and check the slot reads and writes a card. Check the hot shoe, the tripod socket and any ports for damage or bent pins. Look at the screen and the viewfinder for dead spots or scratches. Ten minutes of careful testing now is worth far more than a repair bill later.

Mistake 6: Judging condition from photos alone

A listing photo can hide a great deal: wear on the grip, a sticky shutter button, a faint smell of damp, or a screen with a hairline crack that only shows at an angle. If you can, meet the seller and handle the camera yourself before paying. If the camera must come by post, ask for close-up photos of every side, the sensor, the lens glass and the battery compartment, and keep every message until you have tested the camera. A seller who refuses detailed photos is telling you something about the camera.

Mistake 7: Buying with no return option or proof of purchase

A used camera is a used machine, and even a careful inspection can miss a fault that appears a week later. Before you pay, agree in writing what happens if the camera fails soon after the sale, and keep a receipt or a record of the payment. Check whether any remaining maker's warranty transfers to a new owner. A deal with no way back is only worth taking at a price you can afford to lose entirely. If the seller rushes you or refuses any record of the sale, walk away: another camera will come along.

Before you pay

Take your time at the inspection, and do not let a seller hurry you through it. A genuine seller expects questions and testing, and will not mind you working through a checklist. Compare the camera with other listings before you decide, and remember that the price should reflect everything you found: a missing charger, a tired battery or a dusty sensor all reduce what the camera is worth. If anything feels wrong, even if you cannot name it, walk away. Cameras come up for sale every day, and the next one may be the right one.

Questions people ask

What is the first thing to check on a used camera?

Take a test photo and look at it closely. A plain bright photo shows sensor dust and spots, and it also proves the camera actually takes pictures.

How do I check the sensor without special tools?

Take a photo of a plain bright wall or sky with a small aperture, then view it at full size on a screen. Dust shows as dark spots, and damage shows as marks that do not move.

Should I buy a used camera by post?

Only if you cannot meet the seller and the listing has a clear return option. Ask for close-up photos of every side, the sensor and the lens, and keep all messages until you have tested the camera.

What should be included in the box?

At least the camera, a battery, a charger and a body cap. A strap, lens caps and a memory card are common extras. Missing parts cost money to replace, so factor them in.

Is a high shutter count always a problem?

Not on its own. Shutters are built to last a long time, and a well-kept camera with a high count can still be a good buy. What matters more is how the camera behaves when you test it.
