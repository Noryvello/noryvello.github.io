How to Ask for a Raise

Asking for a raise goes better with preparation: a clear record of what you have achieved, a sense of the going rate for your role, and a calm script for the conversation. This guide gives you a preparation checklist and a script builder to help you ask with confidence.

Asking for a raise is one of the most uncomfortable conversations at work, and most of the discomfort comes from going in unprepared. The conversation itself is simple: you describe what you contribute, you show that you have thought about what the role is worth, and you ask to discuss it. What makes it hard is doing that without a plan. Preparation turns the conversation from a nerve-wracking guess into a calm discussion about facts you already know.

Outcomes vary and nothing here guarantees a raise. Pay decisions depend on your employer's circumstances, and this guide is general information only, not professional advice.

Before you ask

Start with your achievements. Write down what you have done since your last pay review: projects you finished, problems you solved, responsibilities you took on, and anything that made a difference to your team or your organisation. Be specific. A list of concrete achievements is the foundation of the whole conversation, because it gives your manager something real to respond to. Vague claims about working hard are easy to agree with and easy to ignore.

Next, look into the going rate for your role in general terms. Published salary guides, job listings for similar roles, and conversations with people in your field all help you understand the range. You are not looking for a single figure to demand. You are building a sense of what is reasonable, so that you can talk about pay with confidence rather than guessing.

Think about timing. A good time to ask is when your contribution is fresh in your manager's mind: after a successful project, at a review meeting, or when you have taken on new responsibilities. A difficult time is in the middle of a crisis, or when the organisation is clearly struggling. Timing does not decide the outcome, but it shapes the conversation.

Practising the conversation

Once you have a draft, practise saying it out loud at least once. Reading words on a screen is not the same as speaking them, and the first time you say a sentence aloud is often when you discover that it sounds stiff or too long. Practise in private, at your normal pace, and adjust any sentence that feels unnatural until the draft sounds like you. If you can, practise with a friend or a family member and ask them to play the manager: their questions will show you which parts of your case need strengthening.

Practising also calms the nerves. The conversation feels less frightening when you have already said the opening lines once, even to an empty room. You do not need to memorise the draft word for word. You need to know the order of your points — what you achieved, what the role is worth, what you would like to discuss — so that you can find your way back to them if the conversation wanders.

The conversation itself

Keep the conversation calm and factual. Start by saying what you would like to discuss and why you asked for the meeting. Then describe your achievements in plain terms, and explain that you have looked into the typical range for the role. Then ask to discuss how your pay reflects your contribution. That is the whole structure. You do not need a long speech, and you do not need to apologise for asking.

Listen to the answer. Your manager may need time to check with others, and that is normal. If they ask what figure you have in mind, talk in terms of a range rather than a single number, and explain how you reached it. If they raise concerns about your work, listen carefully rather than arguing on the spot. A calm response earns more respect than a defensive one, and it keeps the door open for the next conversation.

If the answer is no

A no is not always final. Ask what would need to change for the answer to be different, and when you could discuss it again. Sometimes the honest answer is that the budget is fixed for now, and the useful outcome is a clear plan: what to achieve, and when to revisit the conversation. If the answer is no with no path forward, that is useful information too. It tells you something honest about the role, and you can decide what to do with that knowledge in your own time.

Whatever the outcome, thank your manager for the conversation. Asking for a raise takes courage, and handling the answer with grace leaves a good impression either way. Keep a note of what was agreed, and follow up on it at the time you discussed.

Questions people ask

When is the right time to ask for a raise?

A good time is when your contribution is fresh in your manager's mind: after a successful project, at a review meeting, or when you have taken on new responsibilities. Avoid asking in the middle of a crisis.

How much should I ask for?

Look into the typical range for roles like yours in general terms, using published guides and similar job listings. Talk in terms of a range rather than a single figure, and explain how you reached it.

What if my manager says no?

Ask what would need to change for the answer to be different, and when you could discuss it again. A clear plan with a date to revisit the conversation is a useful outcome even when the answer is not yet.

Should I mention another job offer?

Only if it is genuine and you are prepared to take it. Using an offer as a bargaining tool can damage trust, and it only works once. A conversation built on your contribution is stronger and safer.

How do I follow up after the conversation?

Keep a note of what was agreed, including any date to revisit the discussion. Thank your manager for the conversation, and follow up at the time you discussed, whether the outcome was yes, no or not yet.
