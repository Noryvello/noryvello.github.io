AI Video Editing Prompts: Cut, Fix, and Polish Videos with AI

By the Noryvello team · Published 20 November 2026 · Updated 20 November 2026

AI video editing works on footage that already exists: it cuts silences, adds captions, reframes a wide video for a vertical screen, and cleans up shaky or dark clips. You tell the editor what to do in plain words, and it makes the cuts for you. This guide explains the jobs AI editors do well, how to write editing prompts, and why you must still check every cut.

Generating a video and editing a video are two different jobs, and this guide is about the second one. The footage already exists — an interview you recorded, a tutorial you filmed, a holiday video on your phone — and the work is to shape it: take out the dead air, add words on the screen, fit it to a vertical frame, and fix what came out shaky or dark. AI editors do these jobs from written instructions, which means the skill is not in learning a timeline, but in writing a clear prompt.

That is good news for anyone who has never edited before. You do not need to learn where every button is, because you can ask for the result in words: remove the pauses, add captions, make it vertical. The editor does the cutting, and you do the judging. The judging is the part that still belongs to you, and this guide ends with why that matters.

AI editing is a starting point, not a finished edit: check every cut before you share the video, because the editor can remove the wrong pause, mishear a word, or crop out something important. What AI video editing is, in one paragraph

AI video editing means giving an editing tool written instructions about footage that already exists, and letting it make the changes: cutting, captioning, reframing, and cleaning up. Unlike a video generator, it does not invent new pictures. It works with the frames you filmed, and its job is to shape them into a cleaner, shorter, better-fitting video.

The jobs AI editors do well

AI editors are strongest at jobs that are repetitive, rule-based, and easy to describe in words. Four of them cover most of what people actually need.

  • Silence removal. The editor finds the pauses and dead air in a recording and cuts them out, so a rambling interview becomes a tight conversation. You set the rule — remove pauses longer than one second — and it applies the rule to the whole video at once.
  • Captions. The editor listens to the speech and writes it on the screen as captions or subtitles, timed to the words. This is the single most useful AI editing job for most people, because captioned videos are watched with the sound off far more often than people expect.
  • Reframing. The editor takes a wide video and remakes it for a vertical screen, following the speaker or the subject so the important part stays in frame. This turns one recording into two videos: the wide original and the vertical short.
  • Cleanup. The editor steadies shaky footage, brightens dark clips, and reduces background noise. These fixes are small and automatic, and they make the difference between a video that looks accidental and one that looks finished.

What these jobs share is that the instruction is simple and the result is easy to check. You can hear whether a pause is gone, see whether the captions match the words, and watch whether the framing follows the speaker. Jobs with a clear right answer are the ones AI does well, and the rest of this guide is about writing instructions that keep the answer clear.

Writing editing prompts

An editing prompt is a short instruction that says what to change, where, and by how much. The formula is: the job, the target, and the number. Remove pauses longer than one second. Add captions in large white text. Reframe for vertical video and keep the speaker centred. Each prompt names one job, and each job has one clear measure of success.

Being specific is what makes the difference. Remove the pauses is vague: the editor has to guess which pauses count, and it may cut a thoughtful silence that was doing real work. Remove pauses longer than one second is a rule the editor can follow exactly, and you can check the result by listening. The same applies to every other job: say the number, the size, or the position, and do not leave it to guesswork.

One more habit helps: ask for one job at a time. A prompt that asks for silence removal, captions, reframing, and colour cleanup in one go gives the editor four chances to misunderstand you, and when the result is wrong you cannot tell which job went wrong. Run the jobs one by one, check each result, and move on only when the video looks right. It feels slower, but it is faster than untangling four mistakes at once.

Captions and subtitles with AI

Captions are words on the screen that match the speech, and they are the most useful thing AI editing can do for an ordinary video. Many people watch videos with the sound off — on a bus, in a quiet room, or while scrolling — and captions are what keeps them watching. They also help anyone who finds the accent hard to follow, or who is watching in a second language.

To add captions, ask the editor to transcribe the speech and place the words on the screen in time with the voice. A good prompt names the style as well as the job: large white text with a dark outline, near the bottom of the frame, two lines at most. Short lines are easier to read than long ones, and two lines at a time is the most a viewer can follow without effort.

Then read the captions all the way through, because the editor will mishear some words. Names, technical terms, and words spoken quickly are the usual casualties, and a wrong caption is worse than no caption, because the viewer trusts the text. Fix the mistakes by hand, check the timing on any line that arrives early or late, and watch the video once with the sound off to make sure the captions alone tell the story.

Reframing a wide video for Shorts and Reels

A wide video is shaped like a cinema screen, and a Short or a Reel is shaped like a phone held upright. Reframing takes the wide video and remakes it for the tall frame, keeping the important part — usually the speaker's face — in view. The editor watches the video, finds the subject, and moves a tall crop window to follow it.

The prompt for reframing should name the target shape and the subject to follow: reframe this video for vertical screens, keep the speaker's face centred, and keep the captions visible. If two people are talking, name the one who matters most, or ask the editor to follow whoever is speaking. The clearer the subject, the steadier the crop.

Watch the reframed video all the way through before you share it, because reframing has two common failures. The first is a crop that cuts off something important — a hand gesture, a whiteboard, a second person at the edge of the frame. The second is a crop that jumps around, following the wrong face or chasing a movement in the background. Both are easy to spot if you watch, and both are invisible if you do not. A reframed video that nobody watched is a video that nobody should share.

Fixing shaky or dark footage

Shaky footage happens when the camera moves during filming: a hand-held phone, a walk-and-talk, a shot taken from a moving car. AI stabilisation smooths the shake by shifting each frame slightly to cancel the movement, and it works well on small shakes — a trembling hand, a gentle walk. It works badly on large shakes, because the frames have to move so far that the edges of the picture start to show.

Dark footage happens when there was not enough light: an indoor room, an evening shot, a subject standing against a bright window. AI brightening lifts the shadows and balances the light, and it works well when the picture has detail hiding in the dark. It cannot invent detail that was never recorded: a face that is fully black in the original will be a brighter blur after brightening, not a face.

The honest rule for both fixes is the same: AI improves what is there, and it cannot create what is not. A slightly shaky clip becomes a steady clip. A very shaky clip becomes a smoother mess. A dim clip becomes a clear clip. A black clip stays a black clip. Use the fixes on footage that is almost good, and re-film footage that is not, because no prompt can rescue a shot that was never captured.

Adding music and voiceover

Music sets the mood of a video, and a voiceover carries the words. Both are added after the cutting is done, because the picture should be finished before the sound is laid on top of it. A common mistake is to add the music first and then cut the picture to fit the song, which usually produces a video that ends awkwardly or rushes its best shots.

For music, pick one track that matches the mood and keep it low enough that every spoken word stays clear. The prompt for an AI editor can name the job plainly: add soft background music, keep it quiet under the voice, and fade it out at the end. If the video has no voice, the music can sit louder, but it should still end cleanly rather than stopping in mid-phrase.

For a voiceover, record the words after the picture is cut, so the voice matches the length of the video instead of the other way round. Read the script out loud a few times before you record, keep the sentences short, and leave a small pause between ideas so the editor has room to breathe. If you use a text-to-speech voice instead, write the script in spoken words — short sentences, plain language — because written sentences sound stiff when a machine reads them.

8 ready editing prompt patterns

Each pattern below is a complete instruction you can copy, adapt, and use as a starting point. Replace the numbers and the describing words with your own, and run one job at a time, checking the result before you move on.

  1. Clean up an interview. Remove pauses longer than one second and cut out the filler words at the start of each answer. Keep the speaker's words unchanged.
  2. Caption a tutorial. Add captions in large white text with a dark outline, near the bottom of the frame, two lines at most, timed to the speech.
  3. Reframe a holiday video. Reframe this video for vertical screens, keep the faces centred, and keep the captions visible at the bottom.
  4. Trim a long recording. Cut the first ten seconds and the last ten seconds, then remove any section where nobody is speaking for more than three seconds.
  5. Steady a walking shot. Stabilise this clip to remove the camera shake, keeping the subject centred, and crop the edges only as much as needed.
  6. Brighten a dark room. Brighten this clip and lift the shadows so the speaker's face is clear, without washing out the background.
  7. Make a highlight clip. Find the three liveliest moments in this video and join them into a thirty-second highlight clip with a short fade between each one.
  8. Add music and an ending. Add soft background music under the voice, keep it quiet, and fade it out over the last three seconds.

Notice what the patterns share: one job, one target, and one number. That shape is the whole technique, and it works for any footage you start from.

What AI editing still gets wrong — check every cut

AI editing is fast and useful, and it is also wrong in predictable ways. The most common error is a cut in the wrong place: a pause removed that was actually a dramatic beat, a sentence trimmed so the meaning changes, a highlight chosen because it was loud rather than because it was good. The editor follows rules, and rules cannot tell a meaningful silence from an empty one.

The second common error is a misheard word in the captions. Names, numbers, and technical terms are the usual victims, and a wrong number in a caption can change the meaning of the whole video. The third is a crop that follows the wrong thing: a face in the background, a moving hand, a pet that wanders through the frame. All three errors are invisible to the editor and obvious to a person who watches the result.

That is why the last step of every AI edit is a full watch-through, from start to finish, with the sound on and then with the sound off. Watch for the cuts, read the captions, and check the framing. Fix what is wrong by hand, and only then share the video. The editor did the heavy lifting, but the judgement is yours, and a video nobody watched is a video nobody should trust.

Questions people ask

Can AI edit my videos?

Yes, AI editors can cut silences, add captions, reframe for vertical screens, and clean up shaky or dark footage, all from written instructions. They work on footage that already exists, and you still need to check every cut before you share the video.

How do I ask AI to remove silences?

Give it a rule with a number, such as remove pauses longer than one second. A clear rule is easy for the editor to follow and easy for you to check, while a vague instruction leaves the editor guessing which pauses count.

Can AI add captions automatically?

Yes, AI editors can transcribe the speech and place the words on the screen, timed to the voice. Read the captions all the way through afterwards, because names, numbers, and technical terms are often misheard and need fixing by hand.

How do I turn a wide video into a vertical Short?

Ask the editor to reframe for vertical screens and name the subject to follow, such as keep the speaker's face centred. Watch the result all the way through, because reframing can cut off something important or follow the wrong face.

Can AI fix dark or shaky video?

It can improve footage that is almost good: steadying a small shake, or brightening a dim clip that still has detail in it. It cannot invent detail that was never recorded, so a very shaky or fully black clip cannot be rescued.

Do I still need to check the edit?

Yes, always watch the finished video from start to finish, once with the sound on and once with the sound off. Check the cuts, read the captions, and watch the framing, and fix anything wrong by hand before you share it.
