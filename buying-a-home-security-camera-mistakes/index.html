7 Mistakes to Avoid When Buying a Home Security Camera

A home security camera only helps if it is in the right place, powered reliably and set up with privacy in mind. This guide walks through the seven mistakes buyers make most often, and ends with a placement and privacy checklist.

Use this checklist when you install your camera, and tick each item off as you confirm it.

Cameras that film neighbours, shared spaces or public areas can break privacy rules in some places. Check your local rules before you install a camera, keep it pointed at your own property, and remember that recording sound has stricter rules than video in many areas.

Why cameras disappoint

Most people buy a home security camera after something happens: a parcel goes missing, a car is scratched, a stranger is seen at the door. The camera arrives, gets screwed to the wall in the most convenient spot, and then quietly disappoints. The picture is too dark, the alerts never stop, or the one moment that mattered was never recorded. Almost every disappointment comes from the same small set of mistakes, and all of them are avoidable before you buy.

The 7 mistakes

1. Putting the camera too high or too low. A camera mounted high up sees the top of heads, not faces, and a camera at eye level is easy to reach, cover or steal. The sweet spot is high enough to be out of easy reach, usually above head height, but low and angled enough to catch a face. Before you fix anything to the wall, hold the camera in place and check the view on your phone. If you can only see the top of your own head, move it.

2. Pointing it at the wrong thing. People often point cameras at the widest view they can get, which is usually the street or the garden. What matters is the entry points: the front door, the back door and ground-floor windows. A camera that films the road will record every passing car and miss the person at your door. Walk around your home and list the ways someone could approach it, then cover those routes first.

3. Forgetting about power. Battery cameras are easy to install and easy to forget, until the battery dies on the one night you needed it. Wired cameras need an outlet or a cable run, which is more work up front but nothing to remember later. Whichever you choose, know the routine before you buy: how often the battery needs charging, how you will reach the camera to do it, and whether the camera warns you before it goes flat.

4. Ignoring the field of view and blind spots. A camera with a narrow view leaves blind spots at the edges, and a very wide view makes everything in it small. Neither is wrong on its own, but you need to know which one you are buying. Check the stated field of view, then test it in place: stand at the edges of the area you want covered and check that you appear in the picture. One well-placed camera beats two that both miss the same corner.

5. Buying for resolution alone. A high resolution number is comforting, but it is not what makes a camera useful at night, in rain or when someone moves quickly. Night vision, motion detection and the speed of the recording matter at least as much. A camera that records a sharp still image but a blurry moving figure has not done its job. Read about the night picture and the motion handling, not just the resolution.

6. Forgetting storage. A camera that cannot save what it sees is a doorbell, not a security camera. Some cameras save to a memory card in the camera, some to a recorder in the house, and some to an online service that needs a subscription. Each has a catch: cards fill up, recorders can be stolen with everything else, and subscriptions add an ongoing cost. Decide how long you want clips kept, check what happens when storage fills, and test that a recording actually plays back.

7. Ignoring privacy, yours and your neighbours. A camera pointed at a neighbour's garden, a shared path or a public pavement can break privacy rules and sour a relationship fast. Keep the view on your own property, check the local rules on recording sound, and change the default password the day you install it. A camera that films your neighbours is a problem you bought yourself, and a camera with its factory password is an open door.

Before you buy

Start with a walk around your home, not a shopping page. Note the entry points, the power outlets, the wireless signal in each corner and the places a camera would be seen by visitors. Then buy one camera, install it, and live with it for a week before you buy more. A week of real use will teach you more about placement, alerts and night vision than any list of features, and it is much cheaper than buying three cameras and learning the same lessons three times.

Questions people ask

Where is the best place for a home security camera?

Cover the entry points first: the front door, the back door and ground-floor windows. Mount the camera above head height so it is out of easy reach, angled down enough to see faces, and away from direct sunlight and bright lights.

Do I need a subscription for a security camera?

It depends on how the camera stores recordings. Some save to a memory card or a recorder in your home with no subscription, while others save clips online and charge for keeping them. Check what storage is included before you buy.

Can my camera record my neighbour's garden?

You should avoid pointing a camera into a neighbour's home or garden, and in many places there are rules against it. Keep the view on your own property, and check your local rules if the camera will see shared spaces.

Should I choose a wired or battery-powered camera?

Wired cameras need an outlet or cable but never run flat, while battery cameras are easy to place anywhere but need regular charging. Choose wired where you have power nearby, and battery where you do not, and know the charging routine before you buy.

How do I keep my camera secure from other people?

Change the default password on the day you install it, use a strong unique password, turn on two-factor sign-in where it is offered, and keep the camera's software updated. A camera with its factory password is easy for others to find.
