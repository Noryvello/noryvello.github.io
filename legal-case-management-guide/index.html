Legal Case Management Guide

This guide explains how a legal case file is organized, from the first meeting to the final outcome. It is general information only, not legal advice, and court rules differ by place, so get a lawyer for anything serious.

What this guide covers and who needs it

A legal case is a long story told in documents. There is the first letter, the contract or the agreement at the centre of the dispute, the messages that passed between the sides, the forms filed with a court or tribunal, the dates for hearings, and the notes from every meeting and call. Without a system, those papers pile up in boxes, inboxes and desk drawers, and the important page is always the one that cannot be found. Legal case management is the discipline of keeping all of it in order, from the day a case opens to the day it closes.

This guide is for two groups of readers. The first is small law firms and solo lawyers who handle many cases at once and need a simple, repeatable way to keep every file complete. The second is self-represented people — someone bringing or defending a small claim on their own — who need to stay organized without a lawyer's office behind them. The method is the same for both: one file per case, one place for dates, one list of tasks, and a habit of filing things the day they arrive.

Good case management does not win a case on its own, but bad case management can lose one. A missed deadline, a missing document, or a forgotten hearing date can end a strong case before it is heard. The pages that follow explain the parts of a case file, the dates that matter most, how to organize documents, and the tasks that come first in a new case. How case management works, step by step

Start by opening one file for the case, with the same structure every time. A case file usually has five parts: the people involved, the key documents, the timeline of events, the dates and deadlines, and the tasks. The people section holds names, roles and contact details for everyone involved: the client, the other side, their lawyer if they have one, witnesses, and any expert. The documents section holds the contract, the letters, the forms and the evidence, each one dated and filed in order. The timeline is a simple list of what happened and when, written in plain words.

The second step is a single list of dates. Every case has dates that cannot move: filing deadlines, hearing dates, dates for exchanging documents, and dates for responding to the other side. Write every date in one place — a diary, a calendar, or a dates page at the front of the file — and set a reminder a week before each one, not the day before. A date that lives only in an email or only in someone's head will be missed sooner or later. Check the dates list at the start of every week, and update it the moment a new date arrives.

The third step is a task list for the case, reviewed weekly. Tasks are the small jobs that move a case forward: draft a letter, gather a document, call a witness, prepare a form, confirm a hearing. Each task needs a named person and a date, and a task without either is not a task — it is a wish. When a task is done, mark it done and file the result the same day. The weekly review takes fifteen minutes for a small caseload and is the habit that keeps every case moving.

The five parts of a case file:

  • People: names, roles and contact details for everyone involved
  • Documents: contracts, letters, forms and evidence, dated and in order
  • Timeline: a plain-words list of what happened and when
  • Dates: every deadline and hearing in one list, with reminders
  • Tasks: the next jobs, each with a named person and a date

Costs, time and what you need before you start

Case management costs very little to set up. A small firm needs a folder per case, whether paper or digital, a shared calendar for dates, and a simple task list. A self-represented person needs the same things on a smaller scale: a folder, a notebook or a simple spreadsheet for dates, and the discipline to file papers the day they arrive. The real cost is time: an hour to open a file properly, and a short weekly review for as long as the case runs. Compared with the cost of a missed deadline, that time is the cheapest insurance a case can have.

Before you open a file, gather what you already have: the contract or agreement, the letters and messages so far, any forms already filed, and the names and contact details of everyone involved. If you are a lawyer taking instructions, the first meeting should end with a clear note of what the client wants, what the facts are, and what the next step is. If you are self-represented, write down your own account of events while it is fresh, in date order, and keep it with the file. Memory fades; a written timeline does not.

What to have ready before you start:

  • The contract, agreement or document at the centre of the case
  • All letters, messages and forms received so far
  • Names and contact details for everyone involved
  • A folder structure, one file per case, the same for every case
  • A calendar or dates page, and a task list you will review weekly

Worked example: organizing a small contract dispute file

Consider a small example. A shop owner, Daniel, has a dispute with a supplier who delivered goods late and in poor condition, and Daniel has refused to pay the final invoice. The supplier has threatened to take the matter to court. Daniel decides to organize his case before anything is filed. He opens a folder and writes a timeline: the order date, the promised delivery date, the actual delivery date, the photos he took of the damaged goods, and the messages in which he complained. Each item in the timeline points to a document in the folder, so anyone reading the file can follow the story.

Next, Daniel makes a dates page. There is no court date yet, but there are dates that matter: the date by which he must answer the supplier's letter, the date his own claim would need to be filed by if he decides to claim, and the date of a meeting he has booked with a lawyer for advice. He sets reminders a week before each one. He also starts a task list: gather the delivery note, print the messages, ask the lawyer about the next step, and write a short summary of what outcome he wants.

When Daniel meets the lawyer, he brings the folder, and the lawyer reads the timeline in ten minutes and understands the whole dispute. The meeting is short, focused and useful, because the file did the explaining. Daniel leaves with a clear next step and a deadline for it, which he writes on the dates page the same day. The folder cost him an evening of work, and it turned a stressful dispute into a managed one.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The first common mistake is keeping the case in your head instead of in a file. A case that lives in memory becomes a different case every week, because memory edits itself. Write the timeline down, file the documents, and let the file carry the facts. The second mistake is filing by pile: papers stacked in the order they arrived, with no sections and no dates page. A pile hides the important document at the bottom and guarantees a frantic search the night before a hearing.

The third mistake is tracking dates in several places at once — some in a diary, some in emails, some on sticky notes. Dates belong in one list, checked weekly. The fourth mistake is letting tasks pile up without owners or dates: a task list that says sort out the contract with no name and no date will still be there next month. The fifth mistake is waiting for the case to become urgent before organizing it. A file opened on the day the case begins is easy; a file rebuilt the week before a hearing is hard, stressful, and often incomplete.

A practical case checklist you can follow

Use this checklist when a new case opens, and work through it again at each stage of the case. It covers the tasks that come first and the documents every file should hold. Adapt it to the kind of case you are handling, and check the court rules where the case will be heard.

  • File opened: one folder per case, with the same five sections every time
  • People listed: client, other side, lawyers, witnesses, experts
  • Timeline written in plain words, in date order, with documents noted
  • Key documents filed: contract, letters, forms, evidence, each dated
  • Dates listed in one place, with a reminder a week before each one
  • First tasks written down, each with a named person and a date
  • Next hearing or deadline confirmed and written on the dates page
  • Weekly review done: dates checked, tasks updated, new papers filed
  • File closed properly: outcome recorded, papers kept for the right time

Tips from real practice

The best tip from real practice is to write the timeline early and keep it current. A timeline is the spine of a case: every letter, every call and every event hangs on it. When a new document arrives, add a line to the timeline before you file the document. When a client calls with a new fact, add it the same day. A timeline that is a week out of date is still useful; a timeline that was never written is a case waiting to go wrong.

The second tip is to keep the file small and current rather than large and stale. A file with fifty well-chosen pages beats a file with five hundred unfiled ones. The third tip is to close a case properly when it ends: record the outcome, note where the papers are kept and for how long, and return original documents to the client. A closed file that is tidy is a gift to your future self, because cases have a habit of reopening years later, and the file is where the answers live.

Questions people ask

What is in a legal case file?

A case file usually has five parts: the people involved, the key documents, a timeline of events, a list of dates and deadlines, and a task list. Each part is kept in the same order for every case, so anyone opening the file can find what they need quickly. The file grows as the case grows, and it is reviewed weekly.

What dates matter most in a case?

Filing deadlines and hearing dates matter most, because missing one can end a case. Next come the dates for exchanging documents and responding to the other side. Keep every date in one list, check it weekly, and set a reminder a week before each date, not the day before.

How should case documents be organized?

One folder per case, with the same sections every time: people, documents, timeline, dates and tasks. File documents in date order, with the date written on each one, and file new papers the day they arrive. A document filed by pile is a document that will be lost when it matters most.

What tasks come first in a new case?

Open the file, list the people involved, write the timeline, gather the key documents, and list the dates that already exist. Then write down the first real tasks, each with a named person and a date. If you are a lawyer, the first meeting should also end with a clear note of what the client wants and what the next step is.

Can I manage my own case without a lawyer?

In many small claims and simple matters, yes, and a well-organized file makes it much easier. Read the court rules carefully, keep every date in one list, and file your papers in order. For anything serious, complicated or high value, get a lawyer — the cost of advice is usually far less than the cost of a mistake.

When should I definitely hire a lawyer?

Hire a lawyer when the case involves a large sum, a criminal matter, a family matter, a property dispute, or anything where losing would cause serious harm. Also hire one when the other side has a lawyer, when the procedure is unfamiliar, or when a deadline is close. A short paid consultation is a good first step.
