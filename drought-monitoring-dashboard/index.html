How drought is measured

Drought is measured in three ways, and each one answers a different question. Rainfall measurement compares the rain that has fallen with the rain that usually falls in the same place at the same time of year. A month with half the usual rain is dry; six months with half the usual rain is a drought. Rainfall is the simplest measure and the one most people think of first, but on its own it does not tell you how much water is left in the ground.

Soil moisture measurement answers that second question. Soil holds water like a sponge, and plants drink from it between rains. When the soil dries out, plants wilt even if rain fell recently, because a short shower wets the surface and leaves the deeper soil dry. Soil moisture is measured by instruments buried in the ground and by satellites that sense how wet the surface is, and it is the measure that matters most to gardeners and farmers.

River levels and reservoir levels answer the third question: how much water is stored and flowing. Rivers fall when rain stops, and reservoirs fall when more water is used than flows in. A reservoir level is the measure that matters most to a water supplier, because it decides whether restrictions are needed. Together, the three measures give the full picture: how much rain fell, how much water the soil holds, and how much water is stored. A drought that shows in all three is a serious one.

Checking official drought maps

Most countries publish an official drought monitor, usually run by a weather service, a water authority or an agriculture department. Search for your country's weather service together with the words drought monitor or drought map, and look for the official service rather than a news article or a random app. The official monitor shows drought levels on a map, usually updated weekly or monthly, and it explains what each level means in its own words.

When you find your area on the map, note three things. First, the level: monitors usually use a scale from normal through abnormally dry to moderate, severe, extreme and exceptional drought, and each level has a definition based on the measurements described above. Second, the trend: is the drought getting worse, staying the same, or easing? A map that shows the change over the last month is often more useful than a single snapshot. Third, the date: drought maps go stale, and a map from last month may not describe today.

Your water supplier is another official source worth checking. Suppliers publish reservoir levels and restriction notices, and they will tell you what you may and may not do with water during a drought. Local news and farming organisations also report on drought, but always check what they say against the official monitor: a headline about a drought can describe a region far from you, and a national picture can hide a local one.

What the levels mean

Drought levels describe how far conditions have moved from normal, and each level brings different effects. Abnormally dry is the first step: rain is below normal, soil is drying, and the risk of drought is rising, but most people notice nothing yet. This is the level to watch, because acting early — saving water, mulching the garden, checking for leaks — is far easier than acting late.

Moderate drought means the dryness is starting to bite. Lawns brown, gardens need more water, and small streams fall. Severe drought means crops and gardens are damaged, water levels in wells and reservoirs fall, and restrictions on water use become likely. Extreme drought means widespread crop failure, serious water shortages, and emergency measures. Exceptional drought, the highest level, is the kind of event that happens rarely and is remembered for years: rivers dry up, reservoirs reach record lows, and water becomes a daily worry for everyone.

The levels are a guide to action, not just a description. At abnormally dry, save water voluntarily and prepare. At moderate, follow any advice from your supplier and cut back on the garden. At severe and above, follow restrictions exactly, because they exist to share a shrinking supply fairly. And remember that the level for your region is an average: your street, your soil and your well may be drier or wetter than the map suggests.

Saving water at home

A household water-saving plan starts with the biggest uses. Showers, toilets, washing machines and gardens use most of a home's water, and each one has a saving that costs nothing. Shorten showers by a minute or two, and turn the water off while soaping. Fix a leaking toilet or tap at once: a slow leak wastes far more water in a day than most people use for drinking. Run the washing machine and the dishwasher only when full, and use the shortest cycle that gets things clean.

In the garden, water early in the morning or late in the evening, when less water evaporates, and water the roots rather than the leaves. Mulch flower beds and vegetable patches with a thick layer of compost or bark, which keeps the soil damp for days longer. Let the lawn go brown: grass usually recovers when the rain returns, and a brown lawn is a small price for a fuller reservoir. If your area restricts garden watering, follow the restriction: it applies to everyone, and it exists for a reason.

Inside the home, catch the water you would otherwise waste. A jug under the tap while the shower warms up gives you water for plants. Water from rinsing vegetables can go on the garden. A bucket in the shower catches water for the toilet. None of these is glamorous, and none of them alone saves much, but together they can cut a household's water use by a large share, and they build habits that stay useful long after the drought ends.

  • Shorten showers and turn the water off while soaping.
  • Fix leaking taps and toilets at once.
  • Run the washing machine and dishwasher only when full.
  • Water the garden early or late, and mulch the beds.
  • Let the lawn go brown: it usually recovers when rain returns.
  • Reuse rinse water and shower warm-up water on plants.

How drought affects gardens, farms and prices

In the garden, drought shows first in the soil. Plants wilt, leaves curl, and growth slows. Vegetables suffer most, because they need steady water to grow, and a dry spell at the wrong time can ruin a crop that looked fine a week earlier. Trees and shrubs cope better, because their roots reach deeper, but a long drought stresses even them, and a stressed tree is more open to disease. The garden advice is simple: water less often but deeply, so roots grow down to the damp soil, and mulch everything you can.

On farms, drought cuts yields, and the effects spread outward. A dry year means less grain, fewer vegetables and less feed for animals, and farmers may have to buy feed or sell animals early. Rivers and wells fall, so irrigation becomes harder and more expensive, and some farms have to leave fields unplanted. The damage is not just one year's harvest: a drought can dry out soil so badly that it takes years to recover, and it can force farmers to change what they grow.

Food prices follow, but slowly and unevenly. A drought in one region may raise the price of the crops grown there, while a drought in a major grain region can raise prices worldwide. The effect on your shopping bill depends on where the drought is, what is grown there, and how much is stored from previous years. Prices rise most for fresh food and for animal feed, and they fall back when the rain returns and the next harvest comes in. Drought is a reminder that food depends on rain, and that the connection is closer than a supermarket shelf suggests.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is watering the lawn in a drought. A lawn uses more water than almost anything else in a garden, and it is the plant that matters least: grass goes brown and recovers, while vegetables, shrubs and trees do not. Water the plants that need it, let the lawn go brown, and spend the saved water where it counts.

The second mistake is thinking one rainy day ends a drought. A short shower wets the surface and leaves the deeper soil dry, and a single day of rain does not refill a reservoir. Drought ends with weeks of steady rain, not one storm, and the official monitor is the place to check whether the drought is truly easing. Celebrate the rain, but keep saving water until the monitor says the drought is over.

The third mistake is ignoring a leak. A dripping tap or a running toilet wastes water every hour of every day, and in a drought that waste matters twice: it drains your supply and it drains the shared supply. Fix leaks first, before any other saving, because a leak undoes every other good habit. The fourth mistake is waiting for restrictions before saving water. Voluntary saving early, when the drought is still moderate, is worth far more than forced saving late, and it may be what keeps the drought from reaching the severe level at all.

Questions people ask

What is a drought?

A drought is a long period of unusually low rainfall that dries out soil, rivers and reservoirs. It is measured against what is normal for a place and a season, so a drought in a wet region can mean more rain than a normal year in a dry one. Droughts develop slowly and end slowly, over weeks or months.

How is drought measured?

Drought is measured in three ways: rainfall compared with the usual amount, soil moisture, and river and reservoir levels. Rainfall tells you how much fell, soil moisture tells you how much water plants can reach, and river and reservoir levels tell you how much water is stored. Together they give the full picture.

Where can I check drought levels?

Use the official drought monitor for your country, usually run by a weather service, a water authority or an agriculture department. Search for the weather service together with the words drought monitor or drought map. Your water supplier also publishes reservoir levels and restriction notices.

How do I save water in a drought?

Start with the biggest uses: shorten showers, fix leaks at once, and run the washing machine and dishwasher only when full. In the garden, water early or late, mulch the beds, and let the lawn go brown. Reuse rinse water on plants, and follow your supplier's restrictions exactly.

How long do droughts last?

Droughts last from weeks to years, and there is no fixed length. A short drought may end with a few weeks of steady rain, while a serious drought can last for years and take longer to recover from. One rainy day does not end a drought: the soil and the reservoirs need weeks of rain to refill.

Does drought affect food prices?

Yes, but slowly and unevenly. A drought cuts crop yields, which can raise the price of the crops grown in the affected region, and a drought in a major grain region can raise prices more widely. Fresh food and animal feed are affected most, and prices usually fall back when the rain returns and the next harvest comes in.
