Product Feedback Collection Portal

Product feedback is what your customers tell you about what works, what frustrates them, and what they wish existed — and it is only useful if you collect it on purpose and act on it. This guide shows you how to gather feedback through surveys, interviews, and in-app prompts, with question templates and a simple way to prioritise what you hear.

Feedback is a sample, not a vote: the people who speak up are not always the people who matter most. Never promise a change in return for feedback, and never share a customer's words outside your team without their permission.

Why feedback matters — and why most of it is wasted

Every product team believes it listens to customers. Most of them collect feedback in scattered places — a few emails, some chat messages, a comment here and there — and then wonder why the feedback never changes anything. The problem is rarely a lack of feedback. It is a lack of a system: nobody gathers it in one place, nobody sorts it, and nobody decides what to do with it.

Feedback matters because customers see what the team cannot. The team knows how the product is supposed to work; customers know how it actually works for them. The gap between those two views is where the most valuable improvements hide: the step that confuses everyone, the feature nobody can find, the small annoyance that makes people leave. No amount of internal discussion finds those things. Only customers can.

The waste happens in three ways. Feedback is collected and never read. Feedback is read and never sorted, so the same complaint arrives ten times and is treated as ten separate surprises. And feedback is sorted and never acted on, so customers stop giving it — why bother telling a team that never listens? This guide covers the whole chain: collect, sort, prioritise, act, and tell people what you did.

Where feedback comes from

Feedback arrives through more channels than most teams realise, and each channel favours a different kind of message. Support tickets carry problems: things that broke, or confused someone enough to write in. They are the richest source of feedback about what is wrong, and the most ignored, because support and product teams often work apart.

Surveys carry opinions at scale: how satisfied people are, which features they use, what they would change. They answer "how many" questions well and "why" questions badly. Interviews carry the why: a thirty-minute conversation with one customer can explain a pattern that a thousand survey answers only hint at.

In-app feedback — a short prompt inside the product itself — catches people at the moment of experience, which makes it vivid and specific. A rating prompt after a task, or a single open question on a page, can produce more useful words than a long survey sent by email a week later. Reviews and public comments carry reputation as well as information: what people say in public shapes what future customers expect.

The practical step is to list your channels and assign each one an owner and a rhythm: who reads the support tickets for feedback each week, who runs the survey each quarter, who talks to customers each month. Feedback without an owner is feedback nobody reads.

Surveys that get answers

A good survey is short, specific, and respectful of the respondent's time. Five questions will be answered; twenty will not. Each question should earn its place: if you would not act on the answer, do not ask the question. And every survey should say, in one line, why you are asking and what you will do with the answers.

Ask about behaviour before opinion. "How often do you use the reports page?" tells you more than "Do you like the reports page?", because behaviour is a fact and liking is a mood. Ask one thing per question: "Was the setup easy and fast?" is two questions, and the answer cannot tell you which one the respondent meant.

Leave room for words. A single open question at the end — "Is there anything else you would like to tell us?" — often produces the most useful answer in the whole survey. People who have something to say will say it there, and the words they use are the words your team should use when it talks about the product.

Finally, close the loop on surveys. When a survey leads to a change, tell the respondents: "You told us the setup was confusing, so we rewrote it." Customers who see their feedback acted on will give more of it. Customers who never hear back will not.

Interview question templates

Interviews are the best way to understand why customers do what they do. A good interview is a conversation, not an interrogation: open questions, follow-ups, and silence while the customer thinks. The templates below are starting points — adapt the words to your product, and let the conversation wander where the customer's answers lead.

Opening template. "Tell me about the last time you used [the product]. What were you trying to do?" Then: "What happened?" and "How did that feel?" These three questions, asked slowly, will fill most of a thirty-minute conversation, because the customer's story will raise the details that matter.

Problem template. "What is the most frustrating part of [the task]?" Then: "What have you tried to fix it?" and "What would a good solution look like for you?" The second question is the important one: it reveals the workarounds customers have invented, and workarounds are the clearest sign of a missing feature.

Closing template. "If you could change one thing about [the product], what would it be?" Then: "Is there anything I should have asked about?" The last question is the one that most often produces the insight the whole interview was for, because it lets the customer raise what matters to them, not what matters to you.

Take notes during the interview, and write a short summary the same day: what the customer does, what frustrates them, what they wish existed, and any exact words worth quoting. A summary written a week later is a summary half forgotten.

In-app feedback: catching people in the moment

In-app feedback asks the customer while they are using the product, which makes it specific in a way no survey can match. A prompt after a completed task — "How was that?" with a simple rating and an optional comment — captures the feeling while it is fresh. A feedback button on every page catches the complaints that would otherwise go to a support ticket, or nowhere at all.

Keep in-app prompts small and rare. One question, one tap to answer, and easy to dismiss. A prompt that blocks the screen, appears on every visit, or asks five questions will be hated, and the hatred will attach itself to the product. The rule is simple: the prompt should cost the customer less than five seconds, and it should never appear twice in the same session.

Ask at the right moment. After a success — a completed task, a finished purchase, a saved file — people are generous and specific. In the middle of a task, or after a failure, they are annoyed, and the feedback will be too. Timing is the difference between "this was easy" and "leave me alone".

Read in-app feedback quickly. It arrives in small pieces, and small pieces are easy to ignore until they pile up. A weekly review — fifteen minutes, one person, all the week's comments sorted by theme — turns the trickle into a picture the team can act on.

Sorting and prioritising what you hear

Raw feedback is noise. The same complaint arrives in different words from different people, urgent requests hide beside trivial ones, and one loud customer can sound like a hundred. Sorting turns noise into a picture. Start by grouping feedback by theme: put every comment about the same problem in one pile, whatever words it used. The piles, not the individual comments, are what you act on.

For each pile, note three things: how many people raised it, how much it matters to them, and how hard it would be to fix. A problem raised by many people, that frustrates them deeply, and that is easy to fix is the obvious first choice. A problem raised by one person, about a minor annoyance, that would take months to fix is the obvious last choice. Most piles fall between the two, and the three numbers are what let the team argue honestly about which comes first.

Watch out for the loudest voice. One customer who writes ten times is one customer, not ten. Count people, not messages, when you judge how widespread a problem is. And watch out for the customers you never hear from: the ones who left quietly, or who never learned the feature existed, do not appear in any pile. Feedback describes the customers who speak; it says nothing about the ones who do not.

Write the priorities down and share them with the team. A short list — "this month we are fixing these three things, because this many customers asked for them" — turns feedback into decisions, and decisions into work. Without the written list, feedback stays a conversation; with it, feedback becomes a plan.

Closing the loop

Closing the loop means telling customers what you did with their feedback. It is the step most teams skip, and the step that matters most for the future supply of feedback. A customer who hears "we fixed the thing you reported" will report the next problem. A customer who hears nothing will stop bothering.

Closing the loop does not require a personal message to every customer. A short note in a release announcement — "you told us the export was slow, so we made it faster" — reaches everyone who raised the issue and everyone who noticed it silently. For customers who reported a specific problem, a direct reply when it is fixed is a courtesy worth the minute it takes.

Also close the loop when you decide not to act. "We heard the request for this feature, and we are not building it, because it would make the product harder for most people to use" is an honest answer, and most customers accept it. Silence, by contrast, reads as indifference, and indifference is what loses customers — not a refusal.

Keep a simple record of what feedback led to what change. It helps the team see that the system works, it helps new team members understand why the product is the way it is, and it gives the team something true to say when a customer asks whether anyone listens.

Common mistakes to avoid

The first mistake is collecting feedback and never reading it. A survey nobody analyses, an inbox nobody opens, a feedback button whose results nobody checks — these are worse than not asking at all, because they promise attention and deliver silence. Do not open a channel unless someone owns reading it.

The second mistake is asking leading questions. "How much do you love our new design?" is not a question; it is a compliment fishing for agreement. Ask neutrally — "What do you think of the new design?" — and accept the answers you get, including the ones you do not like.

The third mistake is treating feedback as a vote. The most-requested feature is not always the right one to build, and the loudest customers are not always the most important. Feedback informs decisions; it does not make them. The team still has to judge, and sometimes the right answer is to decline a popular request.

The fourth mistake is promising a change in return for feedback. "Tell us what you want and we will build it" creates expectations no team can meet, and the disappointment that follows is worse than never asking. Promise only that you will listen and that you will say what you decide — and then keep that promise.

Questions people ask

What is the best way to collect product feedback?

Use several channels together: support tickets for problems, short surveys for opinions at scale, interviews for understanding why, and small in-app prompts for feedback in the moment. Assign an owner and a rhythm to each channel, so that everything collected is actually read, sorted, and acted on.

How many questions should a feedback survey have?

Five questions will be answered; twenty will not. Each question should earn its place: if you would not act on the answer, do not ask it. Ask about behaviour before opinion, ask one thing per question, and end with one open question, which often produces the most useful answer in the survey.

What questions should I ask in a customer interview?

Start with the customer's story: tell me about the last time you used the product, what were you trying to do, what happened, how did it feel. Then ask about frustrations, what they have tried to fix them, and what a good solution would look like. End by asking what you should have asked about.

How do you prioritise product feedback?

Group feedback by theme, then judge each group by three things: how many people raised it, how much it matters to them, and how hard it would be to fix. Count people, not messages — one customer writing ten times is one customer. Write the priorities down and share them with the team.

Should I tell customers what I did with their feedback?

Yes — closing the loop is the step that keeps feedback coming. Tell customers when their feedback led to a change, in a release note or a direct reply, and also say so honestly when you decide not to act. Customers who hear back will keep giving feedback; customers who hear nothing will stop.

Is the most-requested feature always the right one to build?

No. Feedback informs decisions; it does not make them. The most-requested feature may serve a loud minority, or conflict with what most customers need. The team still has to judge, weighing how many people asked, how much it matters, and how hard it would be — and sometimes the right answer is to decline a popular request.
