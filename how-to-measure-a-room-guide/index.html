How to Measure a Room

Measuring a room well saves mistakes when you buy flooring, paint or furniture. This guide walks through length, width, height, doors, windows and angled walls, step by step. It ends with a room-sketch worksheet and a checklist you can tick off as you go.

Measuring a room sounds simple, but a missed opening or a misread tape can mean buying the wrong amount of flooring or paint. This guide walks through every measurement, step by step, so you get it right the first time.

Measurements are only as good as the tape and the care you take. Measure twice, write each number down, and check your sketch before you buy. If a room is very uneven or unusual, a professional survey is worth considering.

What you need

You need a tape measure, a pencil and a sheet of paper. A second person helps for long walls, because one person holds the tape at the start while the other reads the number at the end. A phone camera is useful too, for a quick photo of anything unusual.

Work in metres and centimetres, and write each measurement down as you take it. Do not trust your memory: numbers blur quickly when you are measuring a whole room.

Measuring length and width

Start with the longest wall. Measure along the floor from one corner to the other, keeping the tape straight and level. Write the number down, then measure the shortest wall the same way.

Measure each wall twice, once near the floor and once near the ceiling, if the walls look uneven. Older rooms are rarely perfectly square, and the two numbers can differ by a centimetre or two. Use the larger number when you are buying materials.

Measuring height

Measure the height from the floor to the ceiling in two or three places. Ceilings slope more often than people expect, especially in older homes. Note the lowest and highest heights on your sketch.

If you are measuring for paint or wallpaper, the height matters as much as the length. A tall room needs more paint than a short one of the same floor area.

Doors, windows and other openings

Measure the width and height of every door and window opening. Then measure where each opening sits along its wall, from the nearest corner to the edge of the frame. This tells you how much wall is left for furniture, paint or wallpaper.

Also note radiators, pipes, plug sockets and light switches. They affect where furniture can go and how much wall space is really available.

Angled and awkward walls

Angled walls look hard to measure, but the trick is to split them into straight parts. Measure each straight segment separately, and draw the angle on your sketch with each segment marked.

For a bay window, measure each face of the bay separately. For a sloped ceiling, measure the height at the lowest and highest points, and note where the slope starts.

Drawing your room sketch

Draw a simple rectangle for the room, then mark each wall with its length. Add doors and windows as gaps in the right places, with their widths written in. Mark the height in a corner of the sketch.

Your sketch does not need to be to scale, but it should show every measurement clearly. A clear sketch is worth more than a tidy one, and it is the document you will use when you shop.

Worked example

A room 4.2 metres long and 3.5 metres wide has a floor area of 14.7 square metres. You find this by multiplying length by width: 4.2 times 3.5 equals 14.7. Try it in the helper above with your own numbers.

Measuring for flooring, paint and furniture

Flooring is sold by floor area, so the length-times-width number is the one that matters. Measure the whole floor, including under furniture that will stay, and leave out only fixed fittings such as a built-in wardrobe.

Paint is sold by wall area, so you need the length and height of each wall, minus the doors and windows. Measure each wall separately and add the areas together. For furniture, the doorway matters as much as the room: measure the narrowest door the piece must pass through.

Double-checking your numbers

Measure everything a second time. It takes a few minutes and catches the slips that cost money: a misread tape, a transposed number, a wall measured to the wrong corner.

Check your numbers against each other too. Opposite walls in a rectangular room should be close in length. If they differ by more than a few centimetres, measure again.

Questions people ask

Where do I start measuring a room?

Start with the longest wall, then the shortest. Measure at floor level along the skirting board, and write each number down as you go.

Do I measure inside or outside the skirting board?

Measure the room itself, wall to wall, at floor level. If you are buying flooring, measure to the wall and leave the fitting gap the product guide suggests.

How do I measure a room with an angled wall?

Split the angled part into straight segments and measure each one. Draw the angle on your sketch and mark each segment with its length.

Should I measure doors and windows too?

Yes. Measure the width and height of each opening, and note where it sits along the wall. This matters for paint, wallpaper and furniture.

Why should I measure twice?

A second measurement catches slips and misread numbers. It takes a minute and saves the cost of ordering the wrong amount of material.
