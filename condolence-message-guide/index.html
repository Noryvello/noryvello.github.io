Condolence Message Guide

Finding words after a bereavement is hard, and a short, sincere message is enough. This guide offers 25 original condolence drafts, grouped by relationship and tone, with a builder to find the right one. It also explains what to avoid and when to send your message.

Writing a condolence message is one of the hardest things to get right. The words feel too small for the loss, and the fear of saying the wrong thing can stop you writing at all. This guide is here to help.

Use the builder below to find a draft that fits your relationship and tone, then make it your own. The drafts are all original, written for this page, and the guide explains what to avoid.

These drafts are starting points for a message, not advice about grief. If someone is struggling, encourage them to speak to a doctor, a counsellor or a bereavement support service. Be kind, be brief, and be there.

How to choose the right words

A condolence message does not need to be long or clever. The best ones say three things: I am sorry, I am thinking of you, and I am here. That is enough, and it is more comforting than a long letter full of advice.

If you knew the person who died, name them and share a short, kind memory. It shows the bereaved that their loved one mattered to others too. If you did not know them, keep the focus on your sympathy for the person you are writing to.

What to avoid

Some well-meant phrases hurt more than they help. Avoid saying the person is in a better place, that it was their time, or that the bereaved should stay strong. Avoid comparing losses, offering explanations, or asking about the cause of death.

  • 'They are in a better place.'
  • 'At least they lived a long life.'
  • 'You need to stay strong.'
  • 'I know exactly how you feel.'
  • Any question about how the person died.

If you are unsure whether a line is kind, leave it out. A short, simple message is always safer than a long one with a line that stings.

When to send your message

Send your message as soon as you hear the news, if you can. A short note in the first days shows you care and that you are thinking of them.

If you missed the first days, send it anyway. Support fades after the funeral, and a message weeks later is often the one people remember. A short note saying you are still thinking of them means a great deal.

After you send it

A message is a beginning, not an end. Check in again after a week or two, and again after a month. Grief does not follow a timetable, and steady, quiet support matters more than a single grand gesture.

Practical help is welcome too. A cooked meal, a lift, or help with an errand is often easier to accept than a general offer. Be specific about what you can do, and then do it.

For close family

  1. I am so sorry for your loss. I am thinking of you and your family, and I am here for whatever you need, now and in the weeks ahead.
  2. There are no words that make this easier, but please know I love you and I am here. You do not have to go through this alone.
  3. I was heartbroken to hear your news. I am holding you close in my thoughts, and I will be here whenever you need a shoulder or a quiet cup of tea.
  4. Your loss is felt by all of us who love you. I am here to listen, to help, and to sit with you in the hard days ahead.
  5. I am so sorry. Please take all the time you need to grieve, and know that my love and support are with you every step of the way.
  6. I am so sorry for your loss. I am thinking of you.
  7. My deepest sympathy. I am here for you.
  8. I am so sorry. You and your family are in my thoughts.
  9. With all my love and sympathy at this sad time.

For a friend

  1. I was so sorry to hear your news. I am thinking of you, and I am here if you want to talk, or just sit quietly together.
  2. Please accept my heartfelt sympathy. I cannot imagine how hard this is, but I am here for you, today and in the months ahead.
  3. I am thinking of you and sending all my love. If there is anything I can do, from a meal to an errand, please just ask.
  4. Your friend meant the world to you, and I know this loss is enormous. I am here, and I will keep checking in.
  5. I am so sorry for your loss. Thinking of you.
  6. My sincere sympathy. I am here if you need me.
  7. So sorry to hear your sad news. You are in my thoughts.
  8. With sympathy and love at this difficult time.

For a colleague

  1. I was very sorry to hear of your loss. Please accept my sincere condolences, and know that we are all thinking of you.
  2. Please accept my deepest sympathy at this difficult time. Take the time you need, and know that your colleagues are here to support you.
  3. I am sorry for your loss. If there is anything I can do to help with your work while you are away, please do not hesitate to ask.
  4. My thoughts are with you and your family. I hope you find comfort in the people around you in the days ahead.
  5. I am sorry for your loss. My condolences to you and your family.
  6. Please accept my sincere condolences.
  7. My deepest sympathy at this sad time.
  8. Thinking of you. My sincere condolences.

Questions people ask

What is a good short condolence message?

A short message is enough: say you are sorry, name the person if you knew them, and offer your support. Two or three sincere sentences are better than a long letter.

Should I mention the person who died?

Yes, if you knew them. Naming them and sharing a short, kind memory is comforting. If you did not know them, focus on your sympathy for the bereaved.

What should I avoid saying?

Avoid phrases like 'they are in a better place' or 'at least they lived a long life'. Avoid advice, comparisons and questions about the cause of death.

Is it too late to send condolences after a few weeks?

No. A message weeks later is often welcome, because support fades after the funeral. A short note saying you are still thinking of them means a lot.

Should I offer help in my message?

Yes, but be specific. Offer a meal, a lift or help with an errand, rather than a general 'let me know'. Specific offers are easier to accept.
