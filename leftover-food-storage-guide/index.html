Leftover Food Storage Guide

Cooked food keeps for about 3 to 4 days in the fridge and about 2 to 3 months in the freezer, as a general guide. The key habits are simple: cool food fast, store it in sealed containers, and reheat it until it is steaming hot all the way through.

Cooking once and eating twice saves time, money, and food — but only when the leftovers are stored properly. The habits in this guide are simple: cool food quickly, keep it cold, label it, and reheat it thoroughly.

This page gives general food-safety guidance only. The storage times here are widely published general ranges, not rules that fit every food or every kitchen. Follow the food-safety guidance in your own country for specific foods, and check with a local authority if you are unsure about a particular dish.

The 2-hour cool-down rule

The most important habit in this whole guide is also the simplest: get cooked food into the fridge within 2 hours of cooking. Bacteria grow fastest at room temperature, so the hours food spends sitting on the counter are the hours that matter most. The common mistake is waiting for food to go completely cold before putting it away. That waiting is exactly what gives bacteria time to multiply, and a modern fridge can handle a container of warm food without any trouble.

Cool food quickly instead of leaving it to cool slowly. Spread it in shallow containers rather than leaving it in a deep pot, because a thin layer loses heat far faster than a thick one. Divide a large pot of soup or stew between two or three containers, and stir it a few times so the heat escapes evenly. Once the food has stopped steaming heavily, seal the lid and put it straight in the fridge.

In very hot weather, when the kitchen is above about 30 degrees Celsius, shorten the window to 1 hour. The same applies to food served outdoors: a dish left in the sun at a picnic or a barbecue should go back into the cold sooner, or be thrown away if it has been out for hours.

How long leftovers last in the fridge

As a general guide, cooked food keeps for about 3 to 4 days in the fridge. That range covers most everyday dishes: cooked meat and poultry, stews and curries, soups, pasta dishes, cooked rice, and roast vegetables. It is a general range, not a promise — how long a dish actually lasts depends on how quickly it was cooled, how cold your fridge runs, and how often the container has been opened.

Keep your fridge at or below 4 degrees Celsius. A fridge that is too warm shortens every storage time on this page, so check the dial if food seems to spoil quickly, and keep the door closed as much as you can. Some foods sit at the shorter end of the range: cooked fish and seafood are best eaten within 1 to 2 days, and dishes made with eggs or cream deserve the same caution.

Eat the oldest leftovers first, and keep containers where you can see them. A container you cannot remember cooking is a container to throw away.

Freezer storage times

In the freezer, cooked food keeps for about 2 to 3 months as a general guide. That range is about quality as much as safety: food kept frozen solid stays safe for longer, but its texture and flavour slowly decline, and freezer burn can make it dry and unpleasant.

Freezing stops bacteria from growing, but it does not kill them. They simply wait, and they start growing again as the food thaws — which is why thawing matters as much as freezing. Thaw leftovers in the fridge overnight, in cold water changed every half hour, or in the microwave. Never thaw food on the counter at room temperature.

Freeze food in the portions you will actually eat, so you only thaw what you need. Leave a little headroom in each container because food expands as it freezes, and squeeze the air out of freezer bags before sealing them. Label every container with what it is and the date it went in, because frozen food all looks the same after a month.

Containers and labelling

Use containers with lids that seal properly. Airtight containers keep food from drying out, stop smells from spreading to other food, and keep anything from spilling onto the shelf below. Glass containers and food-safe plastic containers both work well; the seal matters more than the material. Avoid storing food in the tin or can it came from — transfer it to a container instead.

Small portions are better than one large tub. They cool faster, they reheat more evenly, and you can take out exactly what you need without warming up the rest. A label is worth the ten seconds it takes: write what the food is and the day you cooked it on a strip of tape, or keep a marker by the fridge for writing straight on the lid.

Keep raw and cooked food apart. Store leftovers on a shelf above raw meat, poultry, or fish, so that nothing can drip onto them. Wash containers and lids in hot soapy water before refilling them.

Reheating leftovers safely

Reheat leftovers until they are steaming hot all the way through. That means hot in the middle, not just around the edges. Stir soups, stews, and sauces halfway through reheating, and bring them to a rolling boil. For solid foods, cut into the thickest part to check that it is hot right through before serving.

Only reheat the portion you plan to eat. Take out what you need, reheat that, and leave the rest in the fridge. Reheat leftovers once, not again and again: each round of cooling and reheating gives bacteria another chance to grow, and the food loses quality each time as well.

In the microwave, cover the food, stir or turn it partway through, and let it stand for a minute after the microwave stops so the heat spreads evenly. If reheated food smells off or looks wrong, do not eat it — reheating does not make unsafe food safe.

The when-in-doubt rule

The rule that covers every situation on this page is short: when in doubt, throw it out. Throw food away if it smells off, feels slimy, has mould on it, or looks or tastes strange. Throw it away if it has been in the fridge longer than the general range, even if it seems fine. And throw it away if you simply cannot remember when you cooked it.

Do not rely on smell or appearance alone. The bacteria that cause food poisoning do not always change how food looks, smells, or tastes, so food can be unsafe while seeming perfectly normal. The calendar is a better guide than your nose: if the food is past its range, the safe choice is the bin.

Questions people ask

How long can cooked food stay in the fridge?

As a general guide, cooked food keeps for about 3 to 4 days in a fridge kept at or below 4 degrees Celsius. That range is guidance, not a guarantee: fish and seafood sit at the shorter end, and food that cooled slowly or sat out too long should be eaten sooner or thrown away. Label each container with the day you cooked it so you always know how old it is.

How quickly should leftovers be refrigerated?

Get cooked food into the fridge within 2 hours of cooking, and within 1 hour when the weather is very hot. Do not wait for food to go completely cold on the counter, since that is when bacteria grow fastest. Spread food in shallow containers so it cools quickly, then seal the lids and put it straight in the fridge.

Can you reheat leftovers more than once?

It is best to reheat leftovers only once. Each round of cooling and reheating gives bacteria another chance to grow, so take out only the portion you plan to eat and reheat that until it is steaming hot all the way through. If reheated food is left over again, throw it away rather than saving it for a third round.

How do you know if leftovers are no longer safe?

Throw food away if it smells off, feels slimy, has mould, or looks or tastes strange, and always throw it away once it is past the general storage range. But food can be unsafe without any of these signs, so do not rely on smell or appearance alone. The rule that covers everything is simple: when in doubt, throw it out.
