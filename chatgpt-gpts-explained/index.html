What a custom GPT is, in one paragraph

A custom GPT is a version of ChatGPT built for one job. You give it a name, write plain instructions about what it should do, and add a few opening lines to start conversations. From then on, opening that GPT is like opening ChatGPT with the brief already attached: it knows the job, the tone, and the format, and you only have to bring the question.

The key word is one job. A GPT that helps with homework does not also write shop replies; a recipe finder does not also coach interviews. Each GPT is narrow on purpose, because a narrow brief is easier to follow than a broad one. You can make as many as you like, one per job, and switch between them like switching between apps.

You need no coding and no technical skill. The instructions are ordinary sentences, the same kind you would write in a prompt. If you can explain a job to a person, you can explain it to a GPT.

GPTs vs custom instructions vs plain chat — which one to use when

ChatGPT offers three ways to shape answers, and they suit different needs. Plain chat is for one-off questions: you ask, you get an answer, and nothing is remembered for next time. Custom instructions are for standing rules that apply to everything: how long answers should be, what tone to use. A custom GPT is for one job you return to, with its own brief, its own files, and its own opening lines.

Choose by how often the job repeats and how specific it is. A question you will ask once belongs in plain chat. A rule that fits every chat belongs in custom instructions. A job you do every week, with the same shape each time — homework help, shop replies, meal plans — deserves its own GPT, because the brief is worth writing once and reusing.

The three also stack. A GPT can sit on top of your custom instructions, following both its own brief and your standing rules. Start with the simplest option that fits the job, and move to a GPT when the same prompt keeps coming back.

How to create one step by step

Creating a GPT starts in the GPT section of ChatGPT, commonly reached from the sidebar or the explore area. Choose the option to create a new GPT, and you will usually be offered a guided builder that asks questions in plain language, plus a configure view where you type the details directly. Either route ends in the same place.

First, give it a name that says the job: "Homework Helper", "Shop Reply Writer", "Recipe Finder". A clear name helps you find it later and tells anyone you share it with what it does. Then write the instructions — the heart of the GPT — which the next section covers in detail.

Next, add conversation starters: short example questions that appear when someone opens the GPT, like "Help me plan a healthy dinner" or "Reply to this customer review". Starters matter more than they look: they show the user what the GPT is for and set the shape of the first question. Finally, save it, test it with a real question, and adjust the instructions until the answers land the way you want.

Writing good instructions for a GPT

Good GPT instructions are short, specific, and about one job. Short, because every line competes for attention: a page of rules blurs into noise, while five clear lines get followed. Specific, because "be helpful" says nothing and "answer in three bullet points, plain words, one example" says everything. One job, because a GPT asked to do everything does nothing well.

A useful shape is: what the GPT is, who it serves, how it should answer, and what it should never do. For example: "You are a homework helper for a 12-year-old. Explain step by step in simple words. Ask one question at a time. Never give the final answer straight away; guide the student to it." Four sentences, and the GPT has a character, an audience, a method, and a limit.

Write the instructions, then test them with three real questions. If the GPT ignores a rule, the rule is usually too vague — rewrite it as a concrete instruction. If it follows the rules but the answers feel wrong, the brief is wrong, not the model: change the job description, not the wording. Two or three rounds of testing will settle most GPTs into shape.

Adding files for it to read

A GPT can read files you upload, which turns it from a general helper into a specialist on your material. A homework GPT can read the textbook chapter; a shop GPT can read the price list and the returns policy; a recipe GPT can read your family's favourite recipes. The GPT uses the files as reference when it answers, so its replies stay grounded in your own material.

Upload only what the job needs, and keep the files tidy. A short, well-organised document beats a folder of everything, because the GPT has to find the relevant part each time. Give files clear names, and mention them in the instructions so the GPT knows they exist: "Use the attached price list for all prices" works better than silence.

Never upload files with passwords, bank details, addresses, identity numbers, or anything private, and never upload other people's personal information. Files you attach become part of the GPT, and a GPT you share can expose what it was given. If a file would worry you on a shared drive, it does not belong in a GPT.

8 GPT ideas people actually use

These eight ideas are jobs people genuinely repeat — the kind where writing the brief once pays for itself within a week. Each one is narrow, practical, and easy to start.

  • Homework helper — explains schoolwork step by step, asks questions instead of giving answers away, and adjusts to the student's level.
  • Shop reply writer — drafts friendly replies to customer messages and reviews in the shop's voice, ready to copy and send.
  • Recipe finder — suggests meals from the ingredients you list, with clear steps and swaps for anything missing.
  • Interview coach — asks practice questions, listens to your answers, and points out weak words and vague claims.
  • Travel planner — builds a day-by-day plan from your dates, budget, and interests, and adjusts when you change a day.
  • Meeting notes summariser — turns rough notes into a short summary with decisions and next steps listed.
  • Fitness coach — plans simple weekly workouts around the equipment you have, and explains each move in plain words.
  • Bedtime story teller — invents a short story with a child as the hero, in a gentle tone, in about five minutes of reading.

Pick the idea closest to a job you already do, and build that GPT first. One working GPT teaches more than eight half-built ones, and the second is always easier than the first.

Sharing a GPT — who can use it

When you create a GPT, you choose who can use it. The usual choices are: only you, anyone with the link, or everyone through the public GPT listing. Only you is the default for personal jobs. A link suits a GPT you made for family, a class, or a small team. Public listing suits a GPT you want strangers to find and use.

Sharing shares the GPT, not your account. People who use your GPT get its instructions and its opening lines; they do not get your chats or your files unless the files were attached to the GPT itself. That is why the file rule matters: anything attached to a shared GPT should be something you are comfortable with strangers reading.

Think before you share widely. A GPT with your shop's tone and price list is useful to your staff and harmless to strangers; a GPT built around your family's routines is not for the public listing. Start with "only you", share by link when a real person asks for it, and go public only when the GPT is genuinely meant for everyone.

Limits and honest notes

A GPT is still ChatGPT underneath, and it carries the same limits. It can be wrong, confidently and in detail, and its answers must be checked before they matter — before you send the reply, submit the homework, or follow the advice. A good brief reduces mistakes; it does not remove them.

A GPT also cannot do what ChatGPT cannot do. It cannot browse your private files unless you upload them, it cannot run your shop's systems, and it cannot replace a person for decisions that need judgement. Treat it as a keen assistant with a good brief: useful, fast, and worth checking.

Questions people ask

What is a custom GPT?

A custom GPT is a version of ChatGPT built for one job, such as homework help or shop replies. You give it a name, write plain instructions about what it should do, and add opening lines, and it follows that brief every time you open it.

Do I need to know coding to make one?

No. A GPT is made with plain written instructions, the same kind of sentences you would write in a prompt. If you can explain a job to a person, you can explain it to a GPT, and the guided builder asks questions in plain language.

How is a GPT different from custom instructions?

Custom instructions are standing rules that apply to all your chats, such as answer length and tone. A GPT is a separate assistant built for one job, with its own brief, its own opening lines, and optionally its own files. Use instructions for rules that fit everything, and a GPT for a job you return to.

Can I share my GPT with others?

Yes. You can keep it to yourself, share it with anyone who has the link, or list it publicly. Sharing gives others the GPT's instructions and opening lines, not your account, but anything attached as a file should be something you are comfortable with strangers reading.

Can a GPT read my files?

Yes, you can upload files for a GPT to use as reference, such as a price list or a textbook chapter, and it will draw on them when it answers. Never upload files with passwords, bank details, addresses, or anything private, and never upload other people's personal information.

Is my data safe in a GPT?

A GPT only knows what you put into it: its instructions and any files you upload. Keep both free of private details, share carefully, and check the tool's own privacy pages for how your data is handled. If a file would worry you on a shared drive, it does not belong in a GPT.
