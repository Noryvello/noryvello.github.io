7 Mistakes to Avoid When Buying an Electric Bike

The most common mistake when buying an electric bike is judging it by its claimed range instead of your real trips, hills and load. This guide covers the seven mistakes buyers make most often, plus a range and use checklist to work through before you buy.

An electric bike can change how you travel — hills flatten out, longer trips feel short, and arriving without being worn out becomes normal. But it is also a bigger purchase than an ordinary bike, with a motor and a battery to think about, so the buying mistakes are different too.

The key is to buy for your real riding. Your longest trip, your hilliest road, where you will charge the battery and where the bike will sleep at night all matter more than any headline figure. Work through the mistakes below and the checklist, and you will buy a bike that fits your life.

This guide is general buying advice. Rules for electric bikes on roads and paths vary by place — check your local rules before you ride, including where you may ride and what safety gear is required.

The 7 mistakes to avoid

  1. Trusting the claimed range. Range figures are measured in ideal conditions: a light rider, flat ground, gentle assistance and kind weather. Your real riding will include hills, wind, a bag or a passenger, and colder days — all of which reduce how far a charge takes you. Plan around your longest real trip with plenty of room to spare, and treat the claimed range as a best case, not a promise.
  2. Ignoring the weight. Electric bikes are heavy, much heavier than ordinary bikes, because of the motor and the battery. That matters every time you lift the bike up steps, onto a car rack, over a kerb or into storage. Before you buy, lift the bike yourself and ask whether you can manage it in the places you will actually need to. A bike you cannot lift is a bike that limits where you go.
  3. Forgetting where you will charge it. The battery needs a safe, dry spot near a power socket. If you store the bike far from a socket, check whether the battery removes easily so you can carry it inside to charge. Also think about your routine: charging overnight works for many riders, but only if the bike or the battery is somewhere a cable can reach. Sort out the charging spot before the bike arrives.
  4. Buying the wrong frame size. A frame that does not fit makes every ride uncomfortable and harder to control. Check that you can stand over the frame comfortably, reach the handlebars without stretching, and put a foot down easily when you stop. Frame sizes vary between makers, so do not rely on a size label alone — sit on the bike, and ride it if you can, before you decide.
  5. Overlooking brakes and tyres. A heavier bike travelling with motor assistance puts more demand on its brakes and tyres than an ordinary bike. Check the type of brakes and whether they feel strong and smooth, and check the tyre width against the roads you ride — wider tyres suit rough roads, while narrower tyres suit smooth ones. Brakes and tyres are safety parts, so they deserve as much attention as the motor.
  6. Skipping the test ride. A test ride is the only way to feel how the motor assistance kicks in, how the bike handles at low speed, and whether the riding position suits you. If you can, ride the route you will actually use, including a hill. Pay attention to how the assistance levels feel and whether the bike is easy to start, stop and turn. A bike that feels right in the shop can feel very different on your own road.
  7. Not checking servicing and spares. Motors and batteries need specialist servicing, and not every bike shop can do it. Before you buy, check where servicing is available, what it covers, and how easy it is to get a replacement battery or spare parts for your model. A bike with no local servicing can turn a small fault into a long wait, so this check matters as much as the bike itself.

Before you buy

Once you have worked through the checklist, take a final look at the practical side. Budget for a good lock, lights and a helmet if you need them, because these are part of the real cost of riding. Ask the seller what the warranty covers for the motor and the battery, and how long each lasts under normal use. And if you can, ride the bike on your own route before you commit — the right electric bike should make your regular trip feel easier from the very first ride.

Questions people ask

How far will an electric bike go on one charge?

It depends on your trips, the hills, your load and the weather, not just the claimed range. Range figures assume ideal conditions, so plan around your longest real trip with room to spare.

Can I ride an electric bike in the rain?

Most electric bikes can handle light rain, but you should check the guidance from the maker of your bike. Avoid deep water, and dry the bike and battery contacts after a wet ride.

Do I need a licence to ride an electric bike?

Rules vary by place and by the type of electric bike. Check your local rules before you ride, including where you may ride and what safety gear is required.

How long does the battery take to charge?

Charging time varies by battery and charger, so check the guidance from the maker of your bike. Plan your charging around your routine so the bike is ready when you need it.

Can I ride an electric bike without the motor?

Yes, you can pedal an electric bike with the motor off, but it will feel heavy because of the motor and battery. Try it on a test ride so you know what it feels like.
