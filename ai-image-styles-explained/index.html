AI Image Styles Explained: 20 Styles and the Words That Make Them

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

The style of an AI image comes from the words you put in the prompt. This guide explains twenty popular styles, from photorealistic portraits to watercolour landscapes, and gives you the prompt words that produce each one plus a ready-to-try example prompt for every style.

Two prompts can describe the same subject and produce completely different images, because the style words change everything: the texture, the colours, the lighting, and the mood. A lighthouse described as a photorealistic coastal scene looks like a photograph. The same lighthouse described as a watercolour sketch looks like a painting. The subject stayed the same; only the style words changed.

Learning style words is the fastest way to improve your AI images. You do not need a long prompt. You need the right few words, placed early in the prompt, with supporting words for medium, light, and mood. This guide gives you twenty styles to try, each with the words that make it and one example prompt you can copy and adapt.

Honest limit: style words are interpreted differently by different image generators, and results vary from one generation to the next. Treat every example prompt here as a starting point: test it on your own tool, and adjust the words until the results match what you want. Describe styles in general words rather than naming living artists.

The 20 styles

  1. Photorealistic. Prompt words: photorealistic, natural light, sharp focus, realistic skin texture, true-to-life colours. Example prompt: "Photorealistic portrait of an elderly fisherman mending a net on a wooden dock, morning light, sharp focus, natural skin texture, true-to-life colours."
  2. Anime. Prompt words: anime style, cel shading, clean line art, expressive eyes, vibrant colours. Example prompt: "Anime style illustration of a girl with a red umbrella waiting at a rainy train station, cel shading, clean line art, expressive eyes, vibrant colours."
  3. Watercolor. Prompt words: watercolour painting, soft washes, bleeding edges, pale light, paper texture. Example prompt: "Watercolour painting of a cottage garden in spring, soft washes of pink and green, bleeding edges, pale morning light, visible paper texture."
  4. 3D render. Prompt words: 3D render, smooth surfaces, soft studio lighting, clean geometry, glossy finish. Example prompt: "3D render of a small ceramic teapot on a wooden table, smooth surfaces, soft studio lighting, clean geometry, gentle reflections."
  5. Oil painting. Prompt words: oil painting, visible brushstrokes, rich pigments, warm tones, textured canvas. Example prompt: "Oil painting of a mountain valley at sunset, visible brushstrokes, rich pigments, warm golden tones, textured canvas surface."
  6. Cyberpunk. Prompt words: cyberpunk, neon lights, rain-soaked streets, futuristic city, high contrast. Example prompt: "Cyberpunk street market at night, neon signs in pink and cyan, rain-soaked pavement, futuristic city towers, high contrast lighting."
  7. Pop art. Prompt words: pop art, bold flat colours, halftone dots, high contrast, graphic poster. Example prompt: "Pop art poster of a vintage bicycle against a yellow background, bold flat colours, halftone dots, thick outlines, high contrast."
  8. Minimalist flat illustration. Prompt words: flat illustration, minimal shapes, limited palette, clean lines, generous negative space. Example prompt: "Minimalist flat illustration of a cat sleeping on a windowsill, simple shapes, limited palette of teal and cream, clean lines, generous negative space."
  9. Vintage film photo. Prompt words: vintage film photograph, faded colours, soft grain, warm tones, nostalgic mood. Example prompt: "Vintage film photograph of a family picnic in a meadow, faded colours, soft grain, warm afternoon tones, nostalgic mood."
  10. Pencil sketch. Prompt words: pencil sketch, fine hatching, graphite shading, loose lines, sketchbook style. Example prompt: "Pencil sketch of an old oak tree beside a stone wall, fine hatching, soft graphite shading, loose confident lines, sketchbook style."
  11. Pixel art. Prompt words: pixel art, small sprites, limited colours, crisp square pixels, retro game look. Example prompt: "Pixel art scene of a tiny lighthouse on a rocky island, small sprites, limited colour palette, crisp square pixels, retro game look."
  12. Art deco. Prompt words: art deco, geometric patterns, gold and black, symmetrical design, elegant lines. Example prompt: "Art deco poster of a sailing ship under a rising sun, geometric patterns, gold and deep navy, symmetrical design, elegant lines."
  13. Impressionism. Prompt words: impressionist painting, dappled light, loose brushwork, broken colour, outdoor scene. Example prompt: "Impressionist painting of a riverside cafe in summer, dappled light through trees, loose brushwork, broken colour, bright outdoor scene."
  14. Fantasy digital painting. Prompt words: fantasy digital painting, epic scale, dramatic lighting, rich detail, magical atmosphere. Example prompt: "Fantasy digital painting of a floating castle above a sea of clouds, epic scale, dramatic golden light, rich detail, magical atmosphere."
  15. Line art. Prompt words: line art, single continuous line, black on white, elegant simplicity, no shading. Example prompt: "Line art drawing of a dancer in mid-spin, single continuous line style, black ink on white, elegant simplicity, no shading."
  16. Claymation look. Prompt words: claymation style, handmade clay figures, soft textures, stop-motion feel, warm lighting. Example prompt: "Claymation style scene of a small clay fox in a snowy forest, handmade clay figures, soft textures, stop-motion feel, warm lighting."
  17. Neon noir. Prompt words: neon noir, dark shadows, single neon light source, moody atmosphere, cinematic contrast. Example prompt: "Neon noir portrait of a detective under a single pink neon sign, dark shadows, moody atmosphere, cinematic contrast, rain on glass."
  18. Storybook illustration. Prompt words: storybook illustration, gentle colours, whimsical detail, soft edges, warm and friendly. Example prompt: "Storybook illustration of a tiny mouse reading in a library of giant books, gentle colours, whimsical detail, soft edges, warm and friendly mood."
  19. Architectural blueprint. Prompt words: blueprint style, white lines on blue, technical drawing, precise measurements, clean geometry. Example prompt: "Blueprint style drawing of a small garden cottage, white lines on deep blue, technical drawing, precise details, clean geometry."
  20. Cinematic film still. Prompt words: cinematic film still, dramatic lighting, shallow depth of field, film grain, moody colour grade. Example prompt: "Cinematic film still of a lone traveller crossing a desert at dusk, dramatic lighting, shallow depth of field, subtle film grain, moody warm colour grade."

How to combine two styles

Combining styles is where image prompting gets interesting. A cyberpunk city in watercolour, a portrait as a pencil sketch with pop art colours, or a fantasy castle rendered as a blueprint: these combinations create images that no single style would produce. The trick is to keep one style dominant and let the other modify it.

Put the main style first in your prompt, because the first style words carry the most weight. Then add the second style as a modifier. "Watercolour painting of a cyberpunk street market" reads as a watercolour first, with cyberpunk as the subject. "Cyberpunk street market rendered as a watercolour painting" reads the other way. Both are valid; the order decides which style wins.

Keep the combination simple. Two styles work well together. Three or more usually fight, and the result looks muddled rather than creative. If a combination does not work, simplify: keep one style for the medium and use the other only for colour or lighting. "Oil painting with neon lighting" is easier for a model to follow than "oil painting cyberpunk pop art poster."

Some combinations work better than others. Painterly styles mix well with each other, because they share a language of brushwork and colour. Photographic styles mix well with cinematic words. Styles that pull in opposite directions, like minimalist flat illustration and hyper-detailed fantasy painting, need careful wording to avoid a confused result. When in doubt, try the combination with a simple subject first.

Words that change any style

Beyond the style name itself, a handful of supporting words can change how any style comes out. These words describe the medium, the light, the colour, and the mood, and they work across almost every style on the list.

  • Lighting words: soft morning light, golden hour, dramatic side light, studio lighting, moonlight, candlelight. Lighting changes the mood of an image more than almost anything else.
  • Texture words: visible brushstrokes, smooth, grainy, glossy, matte, paper texture. Texture words tell the model how the surface of the image should feel.
  • Colour words: muted palette, vibrant colours, monochrome, pastel tones, warm tones, cool tones. A limited palette often looks more deliberate than a full rainbow.
  • Camera words: close-up, wide shot, bird's-eye view, shallow depth of field, wide-angle lens. These work best with photographic and cinematic styles.
  • Mood words: peaceful, dramatic, playful, melancholy, majestic, cosy. Mood words are vague on their own, but they sharpen a prompt when combined with a style and a subject.

When a result is close but not quite right, change one supporting word at a time rather than rewriting the whole prompt. Swap "soft morning light" for "dramatic side light" and compare the two results. This kind of single-change testing teaches you what each word does on your tool, and it builds a personal vocabulary of words you trust.

How to test a style quickly

The fastest way to learn a style is to run the same subject through several styles and compare the results side by side. Pick a simple subject, a lighthouse, a cat, a teapot, and generate it in three or four styles from the list. The differences will teach you more than any description.

Keep the subject and the supporting words the same across the test, and change only the style words. That way, any difference in the result comes from the style, not from a different subject or a different lighting word. Save the results with clear names so you can compare them later.

Test on the tool you actually use. Style words behave differently on different generators, because each one was trained on different images. A style that looks beautiful on one tool can look flat on another. Your own tests are more useful than any guide, including this one.

When you find a combination that works, save the prompt. A small collection of tested prompts, one per style you use often, is more valuable than a long list of untested ideas. Over time, you will build a personal style library that you can reuse with confidence.

Choosing a style for your purpose

The right style depends on what the image is for. A style that looks wonderful on a poster can be wrong for a product photo, and a style that suits a children's book can look out of place on a business website. Match the style to the job.

For product photos and professional images, photorealistic and clean 3D render styles work best, because they keep the subject recognizable and show it clearly. Painterly styles can distort shapes, labels, and colours, which matters when accuracy is the point.

For illustrations, stories, and social posts, you have more freedom. Storybook illustration, anime, pop art, and watercolour all suit creative work where mood matters more than accuracy. Pick a style that matches the feeling of the content: gentle styles for calm topics, bold styles for energetic ones.

For backgrounds and decorative images, abstract and atmospheric styles work well, because the image supports other content rather than carrying it. A soft watercolour wash or a subtle line pattern makes a good background. A busy, high-contrast image can fight with text placed on top of it.

Consistency matters when you make a set of images. If you are illustrating a story or building a brand, use the same style words across the whole set, and keep the same supporting words for lighting and colour. A consistent style makes a set of images feel like they belong together.

Common style mistakes

A few mistakes come up again and again when people prompt for styles. Avoiding them will improve your results more than any single style word.

  • Too many style words. Listing five styles in one prompt confuses the model. Pick one main style and one modifier at most.
  • Style words buried at the end. Words early in the prompt carry more weight. Put the style near the beginning, right after the subject.
  • Contradictory words. "Minimalist" and "highly detailed" pull in opposite directions. Check that your words agree with each other.
  • Copying a prompt without testing it. Prompts shared online were tuned for a specific tool and a specific model version. Test them on your own tool before relying on them.
  • Naming a living artist. This raises legal and ethical questions, and many tools block it. Describe the style in general words instead.
  • Ignoring the negative. Some tools let you say what you do not want, such as no text, no extra fingers, or no watermark. A short negative list can fix common problems.

What styles cannot fix

Style words change how an image looks, but they cannot fix every problem. If the subject is wrong, the composition is awkward, or the image has a garbled hand or a distorted face, changing the style will not help. Fix the subject and the structure first, then adjust the style.

Text in images is a good example. Most image generators still struggle to render words correctly, and no style word reliably fixes that. If your image needs readable text, plan to add it afterwards in an editing tool rather than asking the generator to draw it.

Faces and hands are similar. A style can hide small flaws, a loose watercolour sketch forgives a wobbly hand better than a photorealistic portrait, but it cannot turn a bad generation into a good one. When a result has a serious flaw, regenerate it rather than trying to style your way out of the problem.

Think of style as the finish on a building: it makes a good structure beautiful, but it cannot hold up a weak one. Get the subject, the composition, and the details right first, and the style will do the rest.

Questions people ask

How do I tell an AI image generator which style I want?

Name the style in plain words inside your prompt, and add two or three supporting words for medium, lighting, and mood. For example, add watercolour, soft washes, and pale light for a watercolour look, or studio lighting, sharp focus, and natural skin texture for a photorealistic portrait.

Can I combine two styles in one prompt?

Yes, and it often works well. Ask for the main style first, then add the second style as a modifier, such as a cyberpunk city rendered as a watercolour painting. If the result looks muddled, simplify: keep one style dominant and let the other influence only the colours or the lighting.

Why does the same prompt give different styles on different tools?

Each image generator was trained on different images and interprets style words in its own way. A word like cinematic may mean warm film tones on one tool and dramatic lighting on another. Test a style on the tool you actually use, and adjust the words until the results match what you want.

Which style is best for product photos?

Photorealistic and clean 3D render styles usually work best for products, because they keep the product recognizable and show it clearly. Avoid heavy painterly styles for product shots, since they can distort shapes, labels, and colours that buyers need to see accurately.

Should I use a famous artist's name to get a style?

It is better not to. Using a living artist's name raises legal and ethical questions, and many tools block artist names. Describe the style in general words instead, such as bold flat colours, loose brushwork, or soft pastel tones, which gives you the look without naming a person.
