Drinking Water Testing Directory

Drinking water testing tells you what is in the water you drink, cook with and wash in, and this guide explains what to test for, how testing works, and how to read the results. You can get water tested through a certified lab, a home test kit, or your water supplier's report, and each route suits a different question. This page lists no labs and takes no samples: it teaches you how to find a certified lab in your own country and how to judge the results you get back.

How to use this guide

Start with the question you actually need answered. If you want to know whether your tap water is safe to drink, you need a different set of tests than if you want to know why your kettle furs up or why your laundry feels stiff. This guide walks through the tests in the order you would use them: first what to test for, then the three ways to get a test done, then how to find a certified lab, then how to take a sample properly, and finally how to read the report when it comes back.

You do not need any science background to use this guide. Every term is explained in plain words the first time it appears, and the worked examples use the kind of numbers a real report shows, without pretending those numbers are yours. Where a number matters, the guide says where it comes from: a lab result, a supplier report, or a rule set by your country's authorities. Nothing here is a substitute for the official guidance in your area, and the guide says so wherever the rules differ by place.

If you are in a hurry, read the sections on the three testing routes and on taking a good sample, then use the checklist at the end of the common mistakes section. A badly taken sample wastes the whole test, however good the lab is, so the sampling section is the one part of this guide worth reading twice. The rest can be read in any order, and each section stands on its own.

What to test for (and why)

Most drinking water questions come down to a short list of substances. Bacteria, especially the group labs call coliforms, tell you whether germs from soil or waste have reached the water. Lead tells you whether old pipes or fittings are leaching metal into the water, which matters most in older homes and in homes with old plumbing. Nitrates, which come from fertiliser and from waste, matter most for private wells near farmland. Hardness, which is mostly calcium and magnesium, is not a health question at all: it is about scale in kettles, stiff laundry and soap that will not lather.

Beyond those four, the usual list includes pH, which tells you whether the water is acidic or alkaline, chlorine, which suppliers add to kill germs and which can affect taste, and total dissolved solids, often shortened to TDS, which is a rough measure of everything dissolved in the water. A high TDS number does not by itself mean the water is unsafe: it may be mostly harmless minerals. What matters is what the dissolved solids are, and that is why a lab report lists substances one by one instead of giving a single score.

What you test for depends on where your water comes from. Water from a public supply is already tested by the supplier, so a home test usually makes sense only when something has changed: a new taste or smell, new pipes, a long holiday with the water standing, or a health worry. Water from a private well or spring is yours to test, because nobody else does it for you, and a well should be tested at least once a year and after any flooding, building work or change in taste, smell or colour. If you are unsure what to test for, a certified lab will suggest a standard panel for your area when you describe your water source.

  • Bacteria (coliforms): the first test for any well or spring, and after any contamination worry.
  • Lead: the first test for an older home, especially one with old pipes or solder.
  • Nitrates: the first test for a well near farmland, livestock or a septic system.
  • Hardness, pH, chlorine, TDS: useful for taste, scale and appliances, and for choosing a filter.

The 3 ways to get your water tested

The first route is a certified laboratory. You take a sample in the bottle the lab provides, send or deliver it, and receive a written report with a result for each substance tested. This is the most accurate route and the only one that counts as evidence if you ever need to show a result to a landlord, a buyer or an authority. It takes the longest, usually days rather than minutes, and it costs the most, but for a health question it is the route to choose. A lab test is also the only route that can measure very low levels of substances such as lead.

The second route is a home test kit. Kits range from simple strips that change colour to small bottles you fill and post to a lab. Strips are cheap and fast, and they are useful for a rough check of things like hardness, pH and chlorine. They are not a substitute for a lab result: a strip can tell you that something is present, but it cannot tell you how much with the accuracy a lab can, and a strip that shows nothing does not prove the water is safe. A postal kit that goes to a real lab is a middle ground, and it is worth checking whether the lab behind it is accredited before you buy.

The third route is your water supplier's report. Public suppliers test the water they deliver and publish the results, usually once a year, and many will send you the report for your area if you ask. This route is free and it covers the water as it leaves the supplier, but it does not cover what happens inside your home: old pipes, a storage tank or a long run of standing water can change the water between the street and your tap. Use the supplier report to understand your water in general, and use a lab test when the question is about the water at your own tap.

Finding a certified lab

A certified lab is one that has been checked by an official accreditation body and found able to test drinking water to a recognised standard. The word to look for is accreditation, not just a smart website or a long list of tests. An accredited lab lists the tests it is accredited for, and it will say so on its report. If a lab cannot tell you who accredits it, or its report carries no accreditation mark, treat its results with caution and look elsewhere.

To find one, search for your country's accreditation body together with the words drinking water testing, and look for the body's own list of accredited labs. Many countries also have a drinking water regulator or a health department that publishes a list, and your water supplier may be able to point you to labs it works with. Local knowledge helps too: a well driller, a plumber or a neighbours' group may know which lab people in your area actually use, but always check the accreditation yourself rather than taking a recommendation on trust.

Before you choose a lab, ask three questions. First, which tests do you need for your water source, and does the lab offer a standard panel that covers them? Second, how do you take and deliver the sample, and does the lab provide the bottle and instructions? Third, what will the report look like, and will it compare your results with the official limits for your country? A lab that answers these questions clearly is a lab you can work with. A lab that will not answer them is not.

Taking a good sample

A water sample is only as good as the way it was taken. Use the bottle the lab provides, because it is clean and often contains a preservative that stops bacteria growing on the way to the lab. Do not rinse it out, and do not touch the inside of the cap or the neck of the bottle. If the lab tells you to run the tap first, run it for the time it says: running the tap clears the water that has been standing in the pipes, and testing standing water when you meant to test the supply is one of the most common sampling mistakes.

Fill the bottle to the line the lab marks, cap it tightly, and label it with the date, the time and the tap it came from. Keep it cool if the lab asks you to, and deliver it as soon as you can: bacteria samples in particular go stale quickly, and a lab will usually want the sample within a day of taking it. If you are testing for lead, the lab may ask for a first-draw sample, taken from a tap that has not been used for several hours, because lead builds up in water standing in pipes. Follow the lab's instructions for the test you ordered, not a general rule from elsewhere.

Take notes as you sample. Write down which tap you used, whether you ran it first and for how long, and anything unusual about the water that day: a smell, a colour, recent building work, heavy rain, or a new appliance. These notes help you and the lab make sense of the result, and they matter if you test again later and want to compare. A sample without notes is a result without context, and context is often what turns a confusing number into a clear answer.

Reading the results

A lab report usually has one row per substance, with four pieces of information: the substance name, your result, the unit it is measured in, and the official limit for your country. Read the report row by row, comparing your result with the limit. A result below the limit means the water meets the standard for that substance. A result above the limit means it does not, and the report will often say so in words. Do not panic at a single number above a limit: ask the lab what it means, and whether a second sample is worth taking to confirm it.

Units matter, and they are easy to misread. The same substance can be reported in milligrams per litre, micrograms per litre or parts per million, and a number that looks alarming in one unit may be ordinary in another. Always read the unit next to the result, and never compare two reports unless they use the same units. If the report uses a unit you do not recognise, ask the lab to explain it in plain words before you draw any conclusion.

Some results need a judgement call rather than a simple pass or fail. Hardness has no health limit, so a hard water result is a question about your kettle and your laundry, not your health. A chlorine result is usually about taste, and letting water stand in a jug often removes the taste. A bacteria result, on the other hand, is always worth acting on: do not drink the water until the lab or your supplier says it is safe, and follow their advice about boiling, disinfecting or retesting. When in doubt, ask the lab: explaining a report is part of their job.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is testing once and never again. Water changes: wells change with the seasons, pipes age, and a result from last year says nothing about the water today. A private well should be tested at least once a year, and any water source should be tested again after flooding, building work, a new pipe, or a change in taste, smell or colour. A single test is a snapshot, and snapshots go out of date.

The second mistake is treating a strip test as a lab result. Strips are useful for a rough check, but they are not accurate enough for a health question, and a strip that shows nothing does not prove the water is safe. If a strip worries you, or if the question is about health at all, get a lab test. The strip is the smoke alarm; the lab is the fire service. Both have their place, and they are not the same thing.

The third mistake is sampling badly: using the wrong bottle, touching the inside of the cap, testing standing water when you meant to test the supply, or letting a bacteria sample sit for days before delivering it. Any of these can make a good water source look bad, or a bad one look good. Follow the lab's instructions exactly, and if anything about the sampling is unclear, phone the lab before you take the sample, not after.

The fourth mistake is reading the report without the units, or comparing results from different labs without checking that they used the same units and the same methods. And the fifth is ignoring the report altogether: a result above a limit is a reason to act, not a reason to file the paper away. If a result worries you, ask the lab what it means and what to do next, and if anyone in the household is unwell, talk to a health professional and your water supplier promptly.

This guide is general information only. It lists no laboratories and takes no samples. Water rules and safe levels differ by country, so check the official guidance for your area and talk to a qualified water professional if a result worries you. If your water smells, tastes or looks wrong, or if anyone is unwell, contact your water supplier or local health authority promptly.

Questions people ask

How do I get my drinking water tested?

There are three routes: a certified lab, a home test kit, or your water supplier's published report. A certified lab gives the most accurate result and is the route to choose for any health question. A home kit is a rough check only, and a supplier report covers the water as it leaves the supplier, not the water at your own tap.

What should I test my water for?

It depends on your water source. For a private well, start with bacteria, nitrates and hardness. For an older home, add lead. For taste or scale worries, add pH, chlorine and total dissolved solids. A certified lab will suggest a standard panel for your area when you describe your water source.

How much does water testing cost?

The cost varies widely by country, by lab and by how many substances you test. A home strip kit is the cheapest option and a full lab panel is the most expensive, with a supplier report usually free. Ask the lab for its price list before you send a sample, and ask exactly which tests the price covers.

How do I find a certified lab?

Search for your country's accreditation body together with the words drinking water testing, and look for the body's own list of accredited labs. Your water supplier or drinking water regulator may also publish a list. Always check the accreditation yourself, and ask the lab which tests it is accredited for before you use it.

What does a lab report mean?

A lab report has one row per substance: the substance name, your result, the unit, and the official limit for your country. Compare your result with the limit, reading the unit carefully. A result below the limit meets the standard for that substance, and a result above it does not. If anything is unclear, ask the lab to explain it in plain words.

How often should I test my water?

Test a private well at least once a year, and test again after flooding, building work, a new pipe, or any change in taste, smell or colour. Public supply water is tested by the supplier, so a home test makes sense when something has changed at your own tap. A single test is a snapshot, and snapshots go out of date.
