Effective Study Techniques Guide

This guide explains six study techniques that teachers and study guides commonly recommend, and helps you match them to the subject you are studying. Use the picker to find the techniques that fit your subject, then build a simple weekly practice plan around them.

The six techniques

Most study advice comes back to the same six techniques, and they work best in combination. Spaced practice means spreading your study over several sessions instead of cramming it into one. Retrieval practice means testing yourself from memory, closing the book and trying to recall what you learned. Elaboration means explaining ideas in your own words and connecting them to things you already know.

Interleaving means mixing different types of problems or topics in one session instead of doing them in neat blocks. Concrete examples means working from a real example of an idea rather than only reading its definition. Dual coding means pairing words with a picture, diagram or sketch so your memory has two ways to reach the same idea. None of these is hard to do; the hard part is doing them regularly instead of falling back on rereading.

Why rereading feels useful but is not enough

Rereading is the most common study method and one of the weakest on its own. It feels useful because the material looks familiar the second time round, but familiarity is not the same as knowing. When you reread, the answers are right in front of you, so your brain never has to work to find them. The test, though, asks you to find answers without the book open.

That is why retrieval practice sits at the centre of this guide. Closing the book and writing down everything you remember is uncomfortable, and you will miss things, but that discomfort is the point: the effort of recalling strengthens the memory. Use rereading for the first pass through new material, then switch to testing yourself for the passes after that.

Match the technique to the subject

Different subjects reward different techniques. Languages and vocabulary respond well to retrieval practice and spaced practice, because the work is mostly remembering and using words. Maths and science respond well to retrieval practice and interleaving, because the work is choosing the right method and carrying it out. Reading-heavy subjects respond well to elaboration, because the work is understanding and connecting ideas.

Use the picker at the top of the page to see the suggested techniques for your subject type. Treat the suggestions as a starting point, not a rule: if a technique feels wrong for you, try another one for a week and compare. The best technique is the one you will actually keep using.

Build a weekly practice plan

Techniques only work if they happen, so give them a place in your week. Short, regular sessions beat long, rare ones: three sessions of twenty-five minutes across a week will do more than one long session on a Sunday. Use the weekly planner above to set a realistic total, then spread the sessions out and leave a day between sessions on the same subject when you can.

Plan what each session will do, not just when it will happen. A session might be ten minutes of retrieval practice on last week's material, then fifteen minutes on something new. Ending each session with a quick test of yourself turns the plan into practice instead of just reading time.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is studying only the things you already know. It feels good to get answers right, but the time is better spent on the parts you get wrong. When you test yourself, note the questions you miss and make those the focus of the next session. Another common mistake is studying in long blocks without a break: attention fades, and the last hour of a three-hour session is worth far less than the first.

A third mistake is changing technique every few days. Each technique needs a week or two of honest use before you can judge it. Pick two techniques, use them properly for a fortnight, and then decide what to keep.

How to know it is working

The sign that a technique is working is not how confident you feel, but how well you do when you test yourself without notes. Set a short self-test once a week on material you studied a few days earlier: if you can recall the main ideas and work the problems, the plan is working. If you cannot, change one thing at a time — more spacing, more retrieval, or shorter sessions — and test again the following week.

Keep a simple record of your self-test results, even just a line in a notebook. Over a month, the record shows you which techniques are paying off and which subjects need more time, far more honestly than memory alone.

Questions people ask

How long should a study session be?

Short and focused beats long and drifting. Twenty-five to forty minutes of real work, followed by a short break, suits most people. If a session runs past an hour, split it into two shorter sessions with a break between them.

Is rereading my notes a waste of time?

Not entirely, but on its own it is weak. Rereading is fine for a first pass through new material. After that, switch to testing yourself from memory, and use rereading only to check what you missed.

How often should I review the same material?

Spread reviews out rather than doing them all at once. Review new material the next day, then a few days later, then again the following week. The gap between reviews is part of what makes the memory stick.

Can I use more than one technique at a time?

Yes, and most good study sessions combine two or three. A session might start with retrieval practice on last week's material, then use elaboration on something new, and finish with a quick diagram for dual coding.

What if I only have a few days before the test?

Prioritise retrieval practice: test yourself on the most important material, note what you miss, and study those gaps. Skip rereading everything from the start, and leave the last evening for a final self-test and an early night.
