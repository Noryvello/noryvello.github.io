A meteor shower happens when Earth passes through a stream of dust left behind by a comet or an asteroid. The dust grains burn up high in the atmosphere and appear as brief streaks of light — shooting stars. Each shower returns at about the same time every year, because Earth crosses the same dust stream on each orbit, and each shower is named after the constellation its meteors seem to radiate from.

2027 is a mixed year for meteor watching. The year opens with its best shower, the Quadrantids, under a thin crescent moon, and the Eta Aquariids in May and the Southern Delta Aquariids in July both enjoy dark skies. But moonlight spoils several of the other big nights: the Perseids in August and the Geminids in December both peak under bright moons. The best nights of 2027

The Quadrantids are the standout shower of 2027. They peak on the night of 3–4 January with a ZHR of about 80–120, and the thin waning crescent moon is only about 10% lit, so moonlight barely interferes. The catch is that the Quadrantid peak is narrow and uncertain — the shower is active for only a short window, so the peak night itself is the one to watch, and the hours around it matter more than for a longer-lived shower.

The Eta Aquariids, peaking on the night of 5–6 May with a ZHR of about 50, and the Southern Delta Aquariids, peaking on the night of 30–31 July with a ZHR of about 25, are the other good bets of the year: both enjoy dark skies in 2027. Their rates are lower than the Quadrantids, but dark skies mean more of the meteors that do appear will actually be visible.

The two showers with the highest rates are both hurt by the Moon in 2027. The Perseids peak on the night of 12–13 August with a ZHR of about 100–110, but a waxing gibbous moon about 85% lit washes out the fainter meteors, making 2027 a poor year for them. The Geminids have the highest rate of all, a ZHR of about 150, but they peak on the night of 13–14 December under a full moon, which makes the peak night very poor — watch on the nights either side of the peak instead, when the Moon rises later or sets earlier.

The Lyrids in April and the Leonids in November are both moonlit and poor in 2027, with modest rates of about 18 and about 15. The Orionids in October and the Ursids in December are the year's quieter showers, with ZHRs of about 20 and about 10.

What ZHR means

ZHR stands for zenithal hourly rate. It is the number of meteors a single observer would count in one hour under a perfect sky: the radiant directly overhead, a completely dark sky with no moonlight and no light pollution, and clear weather. It is a standard for comparing showers with each other, not a promise of what you will see.

In practice, what you actually count under a country sky is usually two to three times lower than the ZHR. The radiant is rarely directly overhead, some sky is always lost to the horizon, and even a dark rural sky is not a perfect one. A shower with a ZHR of 100 might give you 30 to 50 meteors in an hour under good conditions — still a fine show, but not the headline number. Treat the ZHR as a guide to which showers are strong and which are weak, and judge each night by the moon verdict alongside it.

Why the Moon matters

Moonlight is the meteor watcher's main enemy. A bright moon lights up the sky and hides all but the brightest meteors, so the fainter members of a shower — which are always the majority — simply disappear. A shower peaking near new moon, or under a thin crescent, shows far more meteors than the same shower peaking near full moon.

That is why the moon verdict in the table matters as much as the ZHR. In 2027 the Quadrantids benefit from a thin waning crescent moon about 10% lit, the Eta Aquariids and the Southern Delta Aquariids enjoy dark skies, and the Perseids suffer under a waxing gibbous moon about 85% lit. The Geminids have the worst luck of all: their peak night falls under a full moon, which is why the advice for them is to watch on the nights either side of the peak instead.

How to watch a meteor shower

You need no equipment at all. A telescope is useless for meteor watching, because meteors appear across the whole sky and a telescope only shows a tiny patch of it. Your unaided eyes, given a wide view of the sky, are the best tool there is.

Find a dark sky away from street lights and town glow — the darker the better. Let your eyes adapt for 20 minutes before you start counting: avoid phone screens and white lights during that time, because a single glance at a bright screen resets your night vision. Then lie back, look up at a wide stretch of sky, and give the shower time. Meteors come in bursts and quiet spells, so a short watch can easily miss the best of it. Dress warmly, even on a summer night, because you will be still for a long while.

Peak nights and time zones

All peak nights on this page are given in Universal Time. A peak that falls in the early hours of the morning in Universal Time falls on the evening before in North America, so readers there should treat the first date of each peak night as their evening. The same applies in reverse elsewhere: in Asia and Australia, a Universal Time peak in the early hours may fall later on the second date of the pair.

The practical answer is to watch on the night the peak falls in your own time zone, and to keep the neighbouring nights in mind as well. Most showers are active for several nights around the peak, so a night either side of the peak still shows meteors — fewer than the peak night, but often plenty. For the Geminids in 2027, the nights either side are not just a fallback but the better choice, because the full moon spoils the peak night itself.

No major outbursts or storms are predicted for 2027, according to the International Meteor Organization 2027 calendar, so the year's showers should behave much as their usual rates suggest. The best plan for the year is simple: mark the Quadrantids in January, the Eta Aquariids in May, and the Southern Delta Aquariids in July, and treat the rest as nights to watch if the sky is clear and the Moon allows.
