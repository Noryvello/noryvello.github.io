Start a Blog Checklist

Starting a blog is mostly a series of small decisions: a topic, a name, a place to publish, and a plan for the first posts. This checklist walks you through each step in order, with a progress counter so you can see how close you are to launch.

Many blogs never get past the idea stage, not because the idea is weak but because the first steps feel unclear. The truth is that launching a blog is a short list of decisions followed by a short list of actions. You do not need a perfect name, a perfect design or a perfect first post. You need a topic you can write about more than once, a place to publish, and a small plan that gets you to your first post without stalling.

This checklist covers planning and setup in general terms. Publishing platforms change their features and rules often, so check the current guidance of whichever platform you choose before you publish.

How to use this checklist

Work through the list in order, ticking each step as you finish it. The counter at the top of the checklist shows your progress. If a step does not apply to your blog, tick it anyway and move on. The goal is a published first post, not a perfect setup. Most bloggers improve their design, their writing and their posting rhythm after launch, not before it.

Phase one: topic and audience

A blog works best when it has a clear subject. That does not mean a narrow subject: a blog about home cooking is clear, and so is a blog about the books you read. The test is whether you can think of ten post ideas without straining. If you can, the topic has enough room to grow. If you struggle after three, the topic may be too narrow, or you may not enjoy it enough to keep going.

Think about who you are writing for, in plain terms. Are your readers beginners or people who already know the subject? Are they looking for practical steps, honest opinions, or simply something enjoyable to read? You do not need a formal audience profile. A sentence or two about your reader will shape every post you write, from the words you choose to the examples you pick.

Phase two: name and identity

A blog name should be easy to say, easy to spell, and easy to remember. Short names are easier to share by word of mouth, and names that hint at the topic help a new reader know what to expect. Before you settle on a name, check that it is not already in use by another blog or business in the same area, and that the name works as an address people can type without confusion.

Your identity is more than the name. A short line describing what the blog is about helps visitors decide in seconds whether to stay. Keep it to one sentence. You can refine it later as the blog finds its voice; the first version only needs to be honest and clear.

Phase three: platform and setup

There are a few broad types of publishing platform. A free hosted platform gives you a ready-made home for your blog with little setup, and the platform handles the technical side. A self-hosted setup gives you more control over the design and the address, but asks more of you in setup and maintenance. A newsletter-style platform treats each post as a letter sent to subscribers. None of these is the right choice for everyone; the right one is the one you will actually use.

When comparing options, look at what matters to you: how easy it is to write and publish, whether you can shape the look of the blog, whether readers can subscribe or comment, and whether you can move your posts elsewhere later. This page does not recommend any brand or service, and it lists no prices, because both change often and the best choice depends on your own needs.

Phase four: first posts plan

Plan your first few posts before you publish the first one. A short list of ideas stops the blank-page problem that ends many blogs after a single post. Aim for a mix: one post that introduces the blog and what it is about, one post that gives the reader something useful, and one post that shows your own view on the topic. Write the first post in full before launch day, and have notes or drafts for the next two.

Decide how often you will post, and pick a rhythm you can keep. One post a week, kept up for months, builds more readers than a burst of daily posts followed by silence. A simple schedule, written down, turns blogging from a vague intention into a habit.

Phase five: launch steps

Launch day is smaller than it sounds. Publish the first post, check that it reads well on a phone as well as on a computer, and share it with a few people whose opinion you trust. Read it once more after publishing, because mistakes are easier to spot on the live page than in a draft. Then start the second post while the first is fresh in your mind.

After launch: keeping going

The hardest part of blogging is the second month, when the excitement of launching has faded and the posts still need writing. A few habits help. Keep a running list of post ideas and add to it whenever something catches your interest. Write when you have something to say rather than forcing a post to fill a slot. And read your own older posts now and then: they show how far your writing has come, and they often spark ideas for new posts. A blog grows slowly, one post at a time, and that is normal.

Questions people ask

How many posts should I have before I launch?

One finished post is enough to launch, with notes for the next two. Launching with a small plan is better than waiting until you have a large pile of drafts, because the blog only starts growing once it is public.

Do I need to pick a narrow niche?

No. You need a topic with enough room for many posts and an audience you can describe. A clear topic helps readers know what to expect, but it does not have to be narrow.

Should I use a free platform or pay for hosting?

Both can work. A free hosted platform is simpler to start with, while a self-hosted setup gives more control. Choose the option you will actually use, and remember you can move later if your needs change.

How often should I post?

Pick a rhythm you can keep for months. One post a week, kept up steadily, builds more readers than a burst of posts followed by silence. Consistency matters more than frequency.

What if I run out of ideas?

Keep a running list of ideas and add to it whenever something catches your interest. Reader questions, your own mistakes, and things you wish someone had told you are all good sources for new posts.
