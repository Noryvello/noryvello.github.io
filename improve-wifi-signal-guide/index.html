Why Wi-Fi gets weak

Wi-Fi is a radio signal, and like any radio signal it gets weaker with distance and with obstacles in the way. The further your device is from the router, the weaker the signal. Walls make it weaker still, and thick walls made of brick, concrete, or stone weaken it more than thin partition walls. Metal objects, mirrors, and water tanks can block or reflect the signal, creating dead spots in rooms that look like they should be covered.

Other wireless devices can also interfere. A neighbour's router on the same channel, a microwave oven, a baby monitor, or a cordless phone can all crowd the airwaves, especially on the 2.4 GHz band, which many devices share. The result is a connection that works but is slow or drops out at busy times. Most of these causes can be reduced by moving the router, changing the band, or removing the obstacle, which is why the checker starts with questions about your home.

Router placement that helps

The best place for a router is near the centre of your home, out in the open, and raised off the floor. A central spot gives the signal the shortest path to every room, so no room is far away. Open air matters because a router inside a cupboard or behind a television has its signal blocked before it even leaves the room. Raising the router onto a shelf helps because Wi-Fi spreads outwards and slightly downwards, so a router on the floor wastes much of its signal on the floor itself.

Avoid putting the router in a corner, at one end of the house, or next to thick walls. A corner placement sends half the signal outside the house. Also keep the router away from other electronics, especially large metal objects and anything that makes heat. If the router has antennas, point one straight up and one sideways, which covers both flat and upright devices. Small moves can make a surprising difference, so try a new spot and test the signal before deciding it has not helped.

Walls, floors and interference

Walls are the biggest obstacle to Wi-Fi in most homes. A single thin wall is usually fine, but two thick walls can cut the signal down to a trickle. Floors are similar: a router on the ground floor may struggle to reach a room two floors up. If the weak room is far from the router and behind thick walls, moving the router closer to the middle of the home is usually the most effective fix, and it costs nothing to try.

Interference is harder to see but just as real. If your neighbours all use the same Wi-Fi channel, the signals crowd each other, especially in flats. Many routers can choose a less crowded channel on their own if you restart them, or you can pick one in the router settings. The 5 GHz band is usually less crowded than 2.4 GHz, but it does not reach as far, so it suits rooms near the router. The 2.4 GHz band reaches further through walls, which suits distant rooms.

Testing whether a fix worked

After each change, test the signal in the weak room before trying the next fix. Open a web page or a video and see whether it loads smoothly, or check the Wi-Fi signal bars on your device. Test in the same spot each time, because moving even a few steps can change the signal. If a fix helps a little but not enough, try the next fix on the list as well, because the causes often add up.

Keep notes of what you tried, so you do not repeat a fix that did not help. If you move the router, give it a few minutes to settle and let your devices reconnect before judging the result. If nothing on the list helps and the signal is weak even next to the router, the problem may be the internet line or the router itself, and that is the point to contact your provider.

When to ask your provider for help

Some Wi-Fi problems are not yours to fix. If the signal is weak everywhere, even in the same room as the router, the router or the line may be at fault. If the connection drops often at all hours, or if the speed is far below what you expect on every device, the provider can run checks on the line that you cannot run yourself. Before you call, try the fixes on this page and note what you tried, because the provider will ask.

Also ask your provider if the router is very old. Older routers may not support the newer bands or may struggle with many devices at once. The provider can tell you whether a replacement router is available. Do not buy extra equipment until you have tried placement and band changes, because those fixes are free and often solve the problem.

These fixes cover the common causes of weak Wi-Fi in a home: placement, walls, and band choice. They do not cover faults in the internet line or the router itself. This page makes no product recommendations. If the signal is weak everywhere even next to the router, contact your provider.

Questions people ask

Where is the best place to put a Wi-Fi router?

Near the centre of your home, out in the open, and raised off the floor on a shelf or table. A central spot gives the signal the shortest path to every room, and open air lets it spread instead of being blocked.

Do walls really block Wi-Fi?

Yes. Thick walls, especially brick, concrete, and stone, weaken the signal noticeably, and metal objects and water tanks block it further. Each wall between the router and your device makes the signal weaker.

Should I use 2.4 GHz or 5 GHz?

Use 5 GHz when you are close to the router and want the fastest speed. Use 2.4 GHz when you are far away or behind walls, because it reaches further even though it is slower. Many routers offer both.

Does restarting the router help?

It can help when the router has been running for a long time and is behaving oddly. Switch it off, wait half a minute, and switch it back on. If the problem returns quickly, the cause is more likely placement or interference.

When should I ask my provider for help?

Ask when the signal is weak everywhere, even next to the router, or when the connection drops often after you have tried the fixes on this page. The problem may be the line, the router itself, or something only the provider can check.
