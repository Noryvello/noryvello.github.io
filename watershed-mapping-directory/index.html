Watersheds explained simply

When rain falls on land, it runs downhill. The area of land that drains into one river, one lake or one stretch of coast is called a watershed, and in some countries it is called a catchment or a drainage basin. Every place on land sits in a watershed, whether you live on a hilltop or beside a river, because every drop of rain has to go somewhere. The edge of a watershed is usually a ridge or a line of high ground: rain falling on one side flows into one river, and rain falling on the other side flows into another.

Watersheds come in all sizes. A small stream has a small watershed, perhaps a few fields and a wood. A great river has a watershed the size of a country, made of thousands of smaller watersheds fitted together like pieces of a puzzle. Your home sits in several watersheds at once: a small local one that drains into the nearest stream, and larger ones that drain into the river, and eventually into the sea. The small one is the one that matters most for your garden and your street; the large one is the one that matters most for floods and for the water supply.

The key idea is connection. What happens anywhere in a watershed affects the water downstream: a field, a road, a factory and a forest all drain into the same river, and the river carries what they give it. That is why watersheds matter for water quality, for flooding and for wildlife, and why a problem that starts miles upstream can arrive at your tap or your doorstep. Understanding your watershed is understanding the water you live with.

Finding yours officially

The official watershed maps for your country are usually published by the environment agency, the water authority or the geological survey. Search for your country's environment agency together with the words watershed map or catchment map, and look for the official map service. Some countries publish detailed maps where you can enter an address and see the watershed boundaries around it; others publish regional maps that show the main rivers and their watersheds. Use the official map for your country, not a random website, because only the official map uses the official boundaries.

When you find your area on the map, note three things. First, the name of your watershed: it usually takes the name of the river it drains into, and knowing the name makes it far easier to find information about it. Second, the size: are you in a small local watershed or a large river basin? Third, the shape: where is the high ground that forms the edge, and where does the water leave? A watershed that is long and narrow behaves differently from one that is round and flat, and the shape helps explain how fast water reaches the river after rain.

If your country has no public watershed map, or if the map is hard to use, your local water supplier or local authority may be able to tell you which watershed you live in. River trusts, wildlife groups and geography teachers are also good sources: watershed knowledge is local knowledge, and the people who work with rivers usually know the names and the boundaries by heart. Once you know the name of your watershed, searching for it by name will find far more information than searching by your town alone.

Why it matters: where your water comes from

Your tap water comes from somewhere in a watershed, and knowing where changes how you think about it. In many places, tap water comes from a river or a reservoir inside your own watershed, and the quality of that water depends on what happens on the land around it. In other places, water is piped in from a distant watershed, and the river near your home has nothing to do with your tap. Either way, the watershed is the unit that matters: water is managed by watershed, not by town, because rivers do not respect town boundaries.

The land in a watershed shapes the water that comes out of it. Forest and grassland filter rain slowly through soil, and the water that reaches the river is clean and steady. Farmland can add fertiliser and soil to the water, especially after heavy rain. Roads and roofs send rain straight into drains, which makes rivers rise fast and carry oil, litter and grit. A watershed with more hard surfaces floods more easily and delivers dirtier water than a watershed with more soil and plants, and that is why the way land is used matters as much as the rain that falls on it.

Knowing your watershed also helps you understand water rules. Abstraction licences, pollution controls and flood plans are all drawn up by watershed, because the river is the thing being managed. If your supplier announces a restriction, or your council consults on a flood plan, the watershed name tells you whether it applies to you. It is the address of your water, and it is worth knowing.

Where your drain water goes

Water that goes down your drain does not disappear. In most towns it travels through sewers to a treatment works, where it is cleaned and then released into a river, usually in the same watershed it came from. In some places, rainwater from roofs and roads goes through separate drains that run straight into the nearest stream without treatment, which is why oil, paint and chemicals must never be poured down a road drain. What you put down the drain ends up in your watershed's river, and from there in the next watershed downstream.

Homes not connected to a sewer usually have a septic tank or a similar system, which treats waste water on the spot and lets it soak into the ground. A working septic system is a good neighbour to the watershed; a failing one leaks waste into the soil and the nearest stream. If your home has one, have it checked regularly, and never pour grease, chemicals or medicines down the drain, because they kill the bacteria that do the treating.

The habit that matters most is simple: only water, soap and the things that belong in a drain should go down it. Paint, oil, solvents, medicines and wet wipes belong in the bin or at a collection point, not in the drain. Every drain leads somewhere, and in a watershed, somewhere is always downstream, where someone else lives, fishes or draws water.

What lives in a watershed

A watershed is a home as well as a drainage system. Rivers, streams, ponds and wetlands hold fish, frogs, birds, insects and plants, and the land around them holds far more. The health of that wildlife is a good measure of the health of the water: a stream full of mayflies and trout is a clean stream, and a stream with nothing in it is telling you something is wrong. Wildlife groups often monitor streams by counting the small animals that live in them, and their results are a useful second opinion on water quality.

Wetlands deserve a special mention. A wetland is land that is wet for much of the year: a marsh, a reed bed, a flood meadow. Wetlands slow flood water down, filter pollution out of it, and hold water in the landscape long after the rain has stopped. They are among the most valuable parts of a watershed, and they are also among the most often drained or built over. Protecting a wetland protects the whole watershed downstream of it.

You can help your watershed's wildlife without owning any land. Join a local river group, report pollution when you see it, and leave a wild corner in your garden for insects and birds. Plant a tree if you have space: trees slow rain down, hold soil together, and shade streams, which keeps the water cool for fish. Small acts add up across a watershed, because a watershed is nothing more than the sum of what everyone in it does.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is thinking a watershed is a town boundary. Watersheds follow ridges and rivers, not council lines, and your watershed almost certainly crosses several towns. That is why water problems need neighbours to work together, and why a flood plan or a pollution problem cannot be solved by one town alone.

The second mistake is ignoring what goes down the drain. A drain is not a bin, and everything poured into it reaches a river, a treatment works or the soil. Paint, oil and chemicals poured down a drain are pollution, however small the amount seems, and the river downstream does not care that it was only a little.

The third mistake is paving over the garden and wondering why the street floods. Hard surfaces send rain straight into drains, which fill fast and overflow. Soil, grass and plants hold rain and let it soak in slowly. A garden with more planting and less paving is a small flood defence, and a street full of them is a large one.

The fourth mistake is assuming the river near your home is the whole story. Your watershed may be large, and what happens miles upstream — a new housing estate, a drained field, a felled wood — can change the river at your door. When a flood or a pollution incident surprises you, look upstream: the cause is often there, and the watershed map will show you where to look.
