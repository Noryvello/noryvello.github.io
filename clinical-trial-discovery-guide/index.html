Clinical Trial Discovery Guide

A clinical trial is a research study that tests a new treatment, test or way of preventing illness in people who volunteer to take part. This guide explains the trial phases, where official trial registers are, and the questions to ask before joining. It lists no trials and gives no medical advice — always talk to your own doctor before deciding.

What a clinical trial is and who needs this guide

A clinical trial is a carefully planned research study that tests whether a new medicine, treatment, test or way of preventing illness works and is safe. Every medicine and vaccine in use today went through clinical trials before it was approved, and trials are how medicine moves forward. People join trials for many reasons: to try a new treatment when standard treatments have not worked, to help future patients, or to get close medical attention during the study. Joining a trial is always a personal choice, and nobody should ever feel pressured into it.

This guide is for patients and families who want to understand how trials work and how to find reliable information about them. It explains the trial phases in plain words, points to the official registers where trials are listed, and sets out the questions worth asking before you decide. It lists no trials, takes no enrolments, and gives no medical advice — it teaches you how to find and judge the information, so you can have a better conversation with your own doctor.

The most important idea in this guide is that a trial is research, not treatment. A trial tests something that is not yet proven, which means the new treatment may help, may not help, or may have side effects that are not yet fully known. That uncertainty is the reason trials have strict rules, careful oversight and a long list of questions for anyone thinking of joining. Understanding the uncertainty is the first step to making a good decision.

How clinical trials work, step by step

A trial begins long before any patient joins it. Researchers test a new treatment in the laboratory first, and only when the early results look promising and safe enough does it move to testing in people. The testing in people happens in phases, and each phase answers a different question. Phase 1 tests safety in a small group of people and looks for side effects and the right dose. Phase 2 tests a larger group to see whether the treatment works and to learn more about its safety. Phase 3 compares the new treatment with the current standard treatment in a much larger group, often hundreds or thousands of people. Phase 4 happens after approval, and watches for long-term effects in everyday use.

Finding a trial starts with knowing what you are looking for. Trials are listed on official registers, and the two best known are ClinicalTrials.gov, the register run by the United States National Library of Medicine, and the WHO ICTRP, the World Health Organization's search portal that brings together registers from many countries. Both are named here as plain text so you can search for them yourself. On a register you can search by condition, by treatment, by location and by whether the trial is currently recruiting. A listing tells you what the trial is testing, who can join, where it is running, and how to contact the trial team.

If a trial looks relevant, the next step is to talk to your own doctor. Your doctor knows your history, your other treatments and your general health, and can tell you whether the trial makes sense for you and what the risks might be in your case. Your doctor can also contact the trial team or refer you for a screening visit. At screening, the trial team checks whether you meet the trial's eligibility rules — the list of conditions for joining, such as age, diagnosis, past treatments and general health — and explains the trial in full, including the visits, the tests, the risks and what happens if you want to stop.

If you decide to join, you sign a consent form. Consent means you have been told what the trial involves, you understand it, and you agree freely. Consent is not a contract: you can leave a trial at any time, for any reason, without losing your normal medical care. During the trial, you will have regular visits, tests and check-ups, and the trial team will watch for side effects. After the trial, the results are analysed and published, and the trial team should tell you what the study found.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

Joining a trial usually costs the patient nothing for the treatment being tested, and the trial often covers the tests and visits that are part of the study. But there can still be costs: travel to the trial centre, time off work, and care for family members while you attend visits. Some trials help with travel costs and some do not, so ask about this at the screening visit, before you decide. Never join a trial that asks you to pay for the treatment being tested — genuine trials do not charge patients for the experimental treatment.

The time a trial takes varies enormously. Some trials last a few weeks, others run for years, and the number of visits can range from a handful to dozens. Before you join, ask how long the trial runs, how often you will need to visit, how long each visit takes, and what happens between visits. A trial that needs weekly visits to a centre far from home is a very different commitment from one with a visit every three months nearby, and you should decide with the full picture in front of you.

Before you start looking, gather your medical information: your diagnosis, the treatments you have had, the medicines you take, and any test results you have. This information is what the trial team will ask for at screening, and having it ready makes the process faster and the eligibility decision more accurate. Keep a notebook for your trial search: the trials you looked at, what you liked and did not like, the questions you want to ask, and the answers you were given.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The first mistake is treating a trial listing as a promise. A listing describes what a trial hopes to test, not what it will achieve, and many trials do not lead to a new treatment. The fix is to read the listing for what it is — an invitation to find out more — and to judge a trial by the questions you ask at screening, not by the hope in the headline.

The second mistake is skipping the conversation with your own doctor. Your doctor knows your full history in a way no register and no trial team can, and can spot a risk or a conflict that you might miss. The fix is simple: before you contact any trial team, talk to your doctor, and bring the trial listing with you so you can discuss it together.

The third mistake is not asking about the control group. Many trials compare the new treatment with the standard treatment or with a placebo, and patients are assigned to groups by chance. That means you might not receive the new treatment at all. The fix is to ask directly: will I definitely receive the new treatment, or is there a chance I will receive the standard treatment or a placebo? Knowing the answer in advance prevents disappointment later.

The fourth mistake is joining without understanding what happens after the trial ends. Some trials continue to provide the treatment after the study, and some do not. Some trials tell you the results, and some take a long time to publish them. The fix is to ask at screening: what happens to my treatment when the trial ends, and how will I learn what the trial found? The answers matter, and you are entitled to them.

A practical checklist you can follow

Use this checklist when you are thinking about a clinical trial. Work through it in order, and do not skip the step of talking to your own doctor.

  • Gather your medical information: diagnosis, past treatments, current medicines, recent test results.
  • Search the official registers — ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO ICTRP — by condition, treatment and location.
  • Read each listing carefully: what is being tested, who can join, where it runs, and whether it is recruiting.
  • Talk to your own doctor about the trials that look relevant, and bring the listings with you.
  • At screening, ask about the phase, the treatment, the control group, the visits and the time involved.
  • Ask about costs: travel, time off work, and whether the trial helps with expenses.
  • Ask what happens if you want to leave the trial, and what happens to your treatment when it ends.
  • Ask how and when you will learn the results of the trial.
  • Take time to decide. A genuine trial team will never pressure you to sign at once.
  • If you join, keep your notebook up to date with visits, questions and how you feel.

Tips from real practice

Bring someone with you to the screening visit. A family member or friend hears things you might miss, remembers questions you forgot, and can help you think through the decision afterwards. Two pairs of ears are better than one when the information is complicated and the decision matters.

Ask for the information in writing. The trial team should give you a written information sheet that explains the trial in plain words, and you should be able to take it home and read it slowly. If anything in it is unclear, ask. A good trial team welcomes questions, and the quality of their answers tells you a lot about how the trial is run.

Be honest about your health at screening. The eligibility rules exist to protect you, and hiding a condition, a medicine or a past treatment can put you at risk and can spoil the trial's results. The trial team is not judging you; they are checking whether the trial is safe for you, and honesty is the only way that check can work.

Finally, remember that saying no is a perfectly good decision. Not every trial suits every person, and a trial that is wrong for you is not a missed chance — it is a decision well made. Your doctor, your family and the trial team all respect a careful no far more than a hasty yes, and another trial may suit you better in the future.

This page is general information about clinical trials, not medical advice. It lists no trials and takes no enrolments. Always talk to your own doctor before deciding about a trial, and never stop a current treatment without your doctor's advice.

Questions people ask

What is a clinical trial?

A clinical trial is a research study that tests a new medicine, treatment, test or way of preventing illness in people who volunteer to take part. Trials are how new treatments are proven to work and to be safe, and every medicine in use today went through trials before it was approved.

What do trial phases mean?

Phase 1 tests safety in a small group and finds the right dose. Phase 2 tests a larger group to see whether the treatment works. Phase 3 compares the new treatment with the current standard treatment in a much larger group. Phase 4 happens after approval and watches for long-term effects in everyday use.

Where can I find official trial listings?

The two best-known official registers are ClinicalTrials.gov, run by the United States National Library of Medicine, and the WHO ICTRP, the World Health Organization's search portal that brings together registers from many countries. Search them by condition, treatment and location, and talk to your own doctor about what you find.

What questions should I ask before joining a trial?

Ask what phase the trial is in, what treatment you might receive and whether there is a control group, how many visits are needed, what the risks are, what costs you might face, what happens if you want to leave, and what happens to your treatment when the trial ends. Take the answers home and think them over before you decide.

Can I leave a trial after joining?

Yes. You can leave a trial at any time, for any reason, without losing your normal medical care. Consent is not a contract, and a genuine trial team will never pressure you to stay. Tell the trial team that you want to leave, and they will explain any final check-ups they advise.

Who can join a clinical trial?

Who can join depends on the trial's eligibility rules, which list the conditions for taking part, such as age, diagnosis, past treatments and general health. The rules exist to keep participants safe and to make the results meaningful. The trial team checks your eligibility at a screening visit before you join.
