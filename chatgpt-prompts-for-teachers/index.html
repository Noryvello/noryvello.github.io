30 ChatGPT Prompts for Teachers

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

This page gives you 30 original ChatGPT prompts for teachers, organised into five groups: lesson ideas, worksheets, feedback, parent messages, and classroom activities. Every prompt comes with a one-line note on how to adapt it to your class.

Teachers spend hours on preparation that students never see: planning lessons, writing worksheets, drafting feedback, and messaging parents. ChatGPT can take the first draft of that work off your desk, leaving you more time for teaching.

The prompts below are written for real classroom tasks, not for show. Each one names the details you should fill in, such as your topic and age group, and the adaptation note under each prompt shows how to bend it to your subject.

Review all AI output before classroom use. ChatGPT can invent facts, set questions at the wrong level, or use wording that does not suit your students. You are the teacher: the AI drafts, and you decide what reaches the classroom.

How to Use These Prompts

Replace the words in square brackets with your own topic, age group, and limits before you send a prompt. The more specific you are, the better the draft: naming the age group and the lesson length changes the answer far more than any clever wording.

Use the output as a first draft, never as a finished product. Read it with your class in mind, fix the facts, adjust the level, and cut anything that does not fit your students. A prompt that saves you half an hour of drafting is doing its job, even if you rewrite a third of what it gives you.

Lesson Ideas

Use these prompts to plan lessons that start strong and stay on track.

  1. "Suggest three ways to introduce [topic] to [age group], one with a story, one with a question, and one with a short activity."
    Adapt it: name your topic and age group, and change the three approaches.
  2. "Turn this learning objective into a 45-minute lesson plan with a warm-up, a main task, and a closing check."
    Adapt it: paste your objective and change the lesson length.
  3. "Give me five discussion questions about [topic] that go beyond yes-or-no answers."
    Adapt it: name any topic and ask for more questions.
  4. "Plan a lesson on [topic] that uses no screens and no worksheets, only talk and movement."
    Adapt it: name the topic and set your own limits.
  5. "Suggest a real-world hook for teaching [topic] that connects to students' daily lives."
    Adapt it: name any topic that feels hard to make interesting.
  6. "Outline a two-week unit on [topic] with one big question guiding each week."
    Adapt it: name the topic and change the length of the unit.

Worksheets

Use these prompts to draft practice materials you can print and adjust.

  1. "Create a worksheet of 8 questions on [topic], ordered from easy to hard, with an answer key."
    Adapt it: name the topic and change the number of questions.
  2. "Turn this text into a fill-in-the-blanks exercise with 10 blanks, and list the missing words separately."
    Adapt it: paste any short text you want to use.
  3. "Make a matching activity: 8 terms on the left, 8 definitions on the right, shuffled."
    Adapt it: paste your own terms and definitions.
  4. "Write a short reading passage about [topic] at [reading level], then add 5 comprehension questions."
    Adapt it: name the topic and the reading level.
  5. "Create a word problem set for [maths topic] using situations from everyday life."
    Adapt it: name the maths topic and the number of problems.
  6. "Design a quick 10-minute starter activity that reviews yesterday's lesson on [topic]."
    Adapt it: name the topic and change the time.

Feedback

Use these prompts to draft comments that are kind, specific, and useful.

  1. "Draft three encouraging comments for a student who tried hard but got the main idea wrong."
    Adapt it: describe the situation in general terms, without names.
  2. "Rewrite this feedback so it names one strength and one specific next step, in a kind tone."
    Adapt it: paste your own draft feedback.
  3. "Give me five sentence starters I can use when giving feedback on [type of work]."
    Adapt it: name the type of work, such as essays or projects.
  4. "Help me phrase feedback for a student who rushes and skips steps, without sounding frustrated."
    Adapt it: describe the habit, not the student.
  5. "Turn this rubric into plain-language comments I can share with students."
    Adapt it: paste your rubric or marking criteria.
  6. "Suggest a comment for a student who improved a lot this term, naming what changed."
    Adapt it: describe the improvement in general terms.

Parent Messages

Use these prompts to draft clear, warm messages home.

  1. "Draft a short message to parents about [upcoming event], warm and clear, under 100 words."
    Adapt it: name the event and add the date and time.
  2. "Help me write a message to a parent about a student's progress, starting with something positive."
    Adapt it: describe the progress in general terms, without names.
  3. "Draft a note home explaining a change in homework this term, in simple language."
    Adapt it: describe the change and the reason for it.
  4. "Write a message inviting parents to a class showcase, with the date, time, and what to expect."
    Adapt it: add your own event details.
  5. "Help me word a sensitive message about a student who needs extra support, keeping it kind and factual."
    Adapt it: describe the situation without names or labels.
  6. "Draft a thank-you message to parents who helped with [activity]."
    Adapt it: name the activity and what the parents did.

Classroom Activities

Use these prompts to plan activities that get students talking and doing.

  1. "Suggest a 15-minute group activity for [topic] that gets every student talking."
    Adapt it: name the topic and change the time.
  2. "Design a quick classroom game that practises [skill] for [age group]."
    Adapt it: name the skill and the age group.
  3. "Give me three brain-break ideas I can use between lessons, needing no equipment."
    Adapt it: ask for more ideas or a different energy level.
  4. "Plan a debate activity on [issue] with roles for both sides and a closing reflection."
    Adapt it: name the issue and the age group.
  5. "Suggest a pair activity where students teach each other [concept]."
    Adapt it: name any concept students find tricky.
  6. "Create a simple exit-ticket question that shows me who understood today's lesson on [topic]."
    Adapt it: name the topic and the key idea.

Reviewing AI Output Before Class

Before any AI draft reaches your students, read it with three checks in mind. First, are the facts right? ChatGPT can invent dates, figures, and quotations, so compare anything factual with your textbook. Second, is the level right? A worksheet written for the wrong age group wastes a lesson. Third, is the wording right for your class? Remove anything confusing, and add your own examples where the AI used generic ones.

It helps to run the same prompt twice and compare the drafts: the differences show you which parts are solid and which parts the AI is guessing at. When a prompt produces errors often, add the correct facts to the prompt itself and ask the AI to use only those.

Making Prompts Your Own

The prompts on this page are starting points. As you use them, you will find which ones fit your subject and your style, and you will start changing them: different group sizes, different timings, different kinds of questions. Keep a note of the versions that work, organised by task, and you will soon have a small library that saves you real time every week.

Share what works with colleagues, too. A prompt that plans a good history lesson may work just as well for geography with a few words changed, and a feedback prompt that suits one teacher often suits another. The best prompt library is the one your whole department uses.

Questions people ask

Can ChatGPT write my lesson plans for me?

It can draft a lesson plan, but you should treat the draft as a starting point. Check the timing, the level, and the facts, then adjust it to fit your class and your curriculum.

How do I stop AI worksheets from being too easy or too hard?

Name the age group and the level in your prompt, and ask for questions ordered from easy to hard. Read through the result and remove or rewrite any question that misses the mark.

Should I use student names in prompts?

No. Keep student names and personal details out of your prompts. Describe the situation in general terms, such as a student who rushes, and add the name yourself afterwards.

Can ChatGPT mark student work?

It can suggest feedback on a piece of writing you paste in, but it should not replace your judgment. Use it for draft comments and idea banks, and make the final call yourself.

What if the AI invents facts for a worksheet?

Check every fact against your textbook or a trusted source before printing. If a prompt produces errors often, add the correct facts to the prompt itself and ask the AI to use only those.
