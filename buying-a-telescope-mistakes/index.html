7 Mistakes to Avoid When Buying a Telescope

A first telescope should match what you want to look at, where you will use it and how much set-up you will actually do. This guide covers the seven mistakes beginners make most often, with a picker to find your starter type.

Answer one question to get a sensible starting point for your first telescope. No brands, no prices, just the type that fits what you want to see.

Tick off this first-night checklist before you head outside.

Never point a telescope at the Sun. It can blind you instantly, even through a small telescope. Only ever look at the Sun with proper solar filters made for the purpose, and never use an ordinary telescope to look at the Sun.

Why first telescopes gather dust

Many first telescopes end up in a cupboard, and it is rarely because the telescope was bad. It is because the buyer chased the wrong number, bought something too heavy to carry, or expected views from a photograph. A telescope is a tool for looking, and the best first telescope is the one you will actually take outside on a clear night. This guide covers the seven mistakes that put telescopes into cupboards, and how to avoid each one.

The 7 mistakes

1. Chasing the highest magnification. Magnification is the number on the box, and it is the least useful number for a beginner. High magnification makes the image dimmer, shakier and harder to find, and on most nights the air itself limits how much magnification is usable. Low and medium power show you more, more often: the Moon's craters, the rings of Saturn, the moons of Jupiter. Buy for a steady, bright image, not for the biggest number.

2. Buying on aperture alone. A wider telescope gathers more light, which is real and valuable. But aperture comes with size and weight, and a telescope that is a chore to carry is a telescope that stays inside. A smaller telescope used twenty times teaches you more sky than a large one used twice. Think honestly about where you will store it, how far you will carry it and whether you will do that on a cold evening.

3. Skimping on the mount. The mount is the part beginners ignore and owners complain about most. A wobbly mount makes every view shake, and a mount that is hard to aim makes finding anything a struggle. A modest telescope on a steady, smooth mount is a better buy than a fine telescope on a mount that fights you. If you can, try the mount before you buy: it should move smoothly and stay where you leave it.

4. Expecting photographs. The pictures on the box and on the internet are long exposures taken with cameras, often through large telescopes. Your eye sees something different: the Moon and planets are sharp and bright, but deep-sky objects are small, grey and faint. That is normal, and it is still a wonderful thing to see with your own eyes. Go in with honest expectations and you will enjoy the first night instead of feeling let down by it.

5. Buying too big to carry. This is the mistake that fills cupboards. A large telescope needs storage space, a car or a strong back, and ten minutes of set-up in the dark. If any of that is a problem where you live, buy smaller. A compact telescope you can carry in one hand and set up in two minutes will see far more sky over a year than a large one that needs a plan.

6. Skipping the finder and eyepieces. A telescope is a system, not a tube. The finder scope is how you aim it, and the eyepieces decide what you see. A poor finder makes every object hard to find, and a single eyepiece limits what you can do. Budget for a usable finder and at least two eyepieces, one low power and one medium, and learn how to use them before your first night out.

7. Never learning the sky first. A telescope shows you a tiny patch of sky, which is useless if you do not know where to point it. Spend a few evenings with your eyes first: learn the bright stars, the constellations and the planets, with a chart or a sky app. When you can find your way around the sky with your eyes, the telescope becomes a joy instead of a puzzle.

Your first month

Give yourself a month of short sessions rather than one long night. Start with the Moon, then the bright planets, then a star cluster or two. Keep a simple note of what you saw and what worked, and change one thing at a time: a different eyepiece, a darker spot, a steadier set-up. By the end of the month you will know your telescope, your sky and yourself well enough to decide what, if anything, you want next.

Questions people ask

How much magnification do I need as a beginner?

Less than the box suggests. Low and medium power give the brightest, steadiest views and are the easiest to use. Start with a low-power eyepiece for finding things, and use higher power only when the air is steady and the object is bright.

Is a bigger telescope always better?

A wider telescope gathers more light, but it is also heavier and harder to carry and store. A smaller telescope you use often will show you more over a year than a large one that stays in a cupboard. Match the size to where you will use it.

Can I see planets with a small telescope?

Yes. The Moon's craters, the rings of Saturn and the moons of Jupiter are all visible in a small telescope on a steady mount. Planets are bright and rewarding targets, and they are the best place to start.

Do I need to know the constellations first?

It helps a great deal. A telescope shows only a tiny patch of sky, so knowing the bright stars and constellations tells you where to point it. A few evenings with a star chart before your first night will make a real difference.

What should I look at on my first night?

Start with the Moon: it is easy to find, bright and full of detail. Then try a bright planet if one is up, and finish with a star cluster. Keep the session short and relaxed, and use your lowest-power eyepiece first.
