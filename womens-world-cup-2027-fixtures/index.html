Women's World Cup 2027 Fixtures

By the Noryvello team · Published 19 November 2026 · Updated 19 November 2026

The Women's World Cup 2027 runs from Thursday 24 June to Sunday 25 July in Brazil, with 32 teams playing 64 matches in eight cities. FIFA published the schedule framework on 20 August 2026, but the full team-by-team fixture list follows the final draw in December 2026, and kick-off times are not yet published.

The Women's World Cup returns in 2027, and Brazil hosts it for the first time. Thirty-two teams will play sixty-four matches across eight cities, opening at the Maracana in Rio de Janeiro on 24 June and ending with the final at the same stadium on 25 July. It is the biggest tournament in the women's game, and the first to be staged in South America.

This page sets out what is confirmed about the fixtures and what is still to come. FIFA has published the framework of the schedule: the dates, the venues, and the shape of each stage. The details that fans want most, which teams play each other and when, depend on the final draw in December 2026. Until then, this page is the honest picture of what we know.

The full team-by-team fixture list does not exist yet: group memberships and team routes are decided by the final draw in December 2026. Kick-off times have not been published, and qualification is still running. Any fixture list that names team match-ups before the draw is not official.

The tournament at a glance

The Women's World Cup 2027 is played in Brazil from Thursday 24 June to Sunday 25 July. Thirty-two teams take part, playing sixty-four matches in eight host cities. The format is familiar: eight groups of four teams, with the top two in each group reaching the round of 16, followed by quarter-finals, semi-finals, a third-place match, and the final.

The opening match is Brazil against their first group opponents at the Maracana in Rio de Janeiro on 24 June. The final is also at the Maracana, on Sunday 25 July. Between those two dates, the tournament moves through its stages, with matches spread across the eight host cities and the knockout rounds narrowing the field to two finalists.

What the schedule framework gives us

FIFA published the 64-match schedule framework on 20 August 2026. The framework sets out the dates and venues for each match slot in the tournament, so fans know when and where matches will be played, even though the teams in each slot are not yet known. It is the skeleton of the tournament: the shape is fixed, and the draw fills in the names.

What the framework does not give us is equally important. Kick-off times have not been published, so nobody yet knows what time any match starts. Team match-ups are not set either: they depend on the draw and on qualification, which is still running. The framework is a reliable guide to the shape of the tournament, but it is not a fixture list in the usual sense.

The eight host cities and stadiums

The tournament is played in eight cities across Brazil, each with a stadium that also staged matches at the 2014 men's World Cup. The host cities and stadiums were announced on 7 May 2025, and they give the tournament its geography: from Porto Alegre in the south to Fortaleza in the north-east.

Brazil's three group matches

As hosts, Brazil have the clearest schedule of any team. Their three group matches are already fixed by date and venue: the opener at the Maracana in Rio de Janeiro on 24 June, the second match at Arena Itaquera in Sao Paulo on 29 June, and the third at the Estadio Nacional in Brasilia on 4 July. These are the only team fixtures confirmed so far.

The three dates take Brazil across the country: Rio to Sao Paulo to Brasilia, over eleven days. For fans following the hosts, the framework gives a clear route through the group stage. Brazil's opponents in those matches, and the opponents of every other team, are decided by the draw.

How the stages work

The tournament begins with the group stage: eight groups of four teams, with each team playing the other three in its group. The top two teams in each group reach the round of 16, so sixteen teams advance and sixteen go home. The group stage is where the tournament takes shape: every point matters, and the final round of group matches often decides who goes through.

After the groups come the knockout rounds. The round of 16 narrows the field to eight, the quarter-finals to four, and the semi-finals to two. The two losing semi-finalists meet in a third-place match, and the two winners meet in the final at the Maracana on 25 July. Every knockout match is win or go home, which is what gives the later stages their drama.

The shape of the knockout bracket depends on the groups. Group winners and runners-up are placed into the bracket by their finishing position, so a team that wins its group may face a runner-up from another group in the round of 16. That is why the group stage matters beyond simply going through: finishing first can mean a kinder path to the final.

The December 2026 draw: what it will decide

The final draw is scheduled for December 2026, and it is the moment the fixtures become real. The draw places the qualified teams into the eight groups, which in turn fixes who plays whom, where, and in which order. Until the draw, no team can be placed in a group, and no team match-up can be confirmed.

Qualification is still running as the draw approaches, and the last places in the tournament come from an inter-confederation play-off in March 2027. That means the full list of qualified teams is not yet settled either. Once the draw is made, the fixture list will be published in full, and kick-off times will follow. Until then, any list of group matches is guesswork.

Following the fixtures as they are released

The best way to follow the fixtures is to watch for official announcements. After the December draw, FIFA will publish the full fixture list, and kick-off times will be confirmed later. Tickets are not yet on general sale, and when they do go on sale, the official seller is the place to buy them: check the official channels for sale dates and details.

In the meantime, the framework is enough to start planning. Fans can choose which cities to visit, which stage of the tournament to follow, and whether to build a trip around the group stage or the knockout rounds. The fixtures will come, and when they do, the tournament will take its final shape.

If you are planning to attend, the framework also helps with the practical side: which city to stay in, how long to stay, and whether to follow one team or one stage. Book travel and accommodation early where you can, and keep your plans flexible until the draw fixes the details.
