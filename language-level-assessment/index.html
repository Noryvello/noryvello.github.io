What the levels mean

Language levels run from A1 to C2 on a scale used around the world. A1 is the beginning: you know a few words and phrases and can say hello, thank you, and a few everyday things. A2 means you can handle simple everyday situations, such as shopping, asking the way, and talking about yourself in short sentences. B1 is the level where you start to feel independent: you can deal with most situations while travelling, describe experiences and plans, and give simple reasons for your opinions.

B2 is upper intermediate. At B2 you can understand the main ideas of complex texts, talk with a good degree of fluency, and write clear, detailed text on many subjects. Many jobs and universities ask for B2. C1 is advanced: you understand long and demanding texts, express yourself fluently without searching for words, and use the language flexibly for work, study and social life. C2 is near-native mastery, and this assessment does not test for it — twenty quick questions cannot measure that level fairly.

Levels are a guide, not a verdict. Two people at B1 can be very different: one may speak fluently with simple words, while another reads difficult texts but struggles to speak. This assessment tests four areas — vocabulary, grammar, reading and everyday situations — so your result shows where you are strongest and where to focus next.

How this assessment works

There are twenty questions, five in each area. Vocabulary questions test words and their meanings. Grammar questions test the rules that hold sentences together. Reading questions give you a short passage and ask about it. Everyday questions test the polite, practical English of real life: asking the time, replying to thanks, and leaving a meeting early.

Answer one question at a time. After each answer you see at once whether you were right, with a short explanation of the rule or the meaning. There is no time limit, and there is no pass or fail. When you finish all twenty questions, you get your score, your level, and a personal study plan that starts with your weakest area.

The assessment takes about ten minutes. Do it in one sitting if you can, without a dictionary and without help, so the result reflects what you really know. Guessing is fine: a guess that turns out right still tells you something, and the explanation teaches you either way.

How to move up one level

Moving up a level takes steady practice, not a single burst of effort. The most reliable way is a little every day: twenty minutes of reading, listening, speaking or writing, done daily, beats a five-hour session once a month. Your brain needs regular contact with the language to keep it.

Work on your weakest area first, because it holds the rest back. If your vocabulary is strong but your grammar is weak, your sentences stay simple no matter how many words you know. If your reading is strong but you never speak, your speaking stays behind. The study plan at the end of this assessment orders your practice by weakness, so you start where it helps most.

Use the language for real things. Read news about topics you care about, watch a show with subtitles, write a short diary entry, or practise a conversation with a friend. Real use builds memory far better than memorising lists, because each word arrives with a meaning and a feeling attached.

Keep a simple record of what you study. A notebook or a notes file with the date, what you practised, and one thing you learned is enough. After a month, the record shows you how far you have come, and it shows you the gaps: if every entry is reading, you know speaking needs attention. A record also keeps you honest on the days when you feel you are making no progress — the list of what you have done is proof that you are. Set a small weekly goal, such as finishing one article or learning ten new words, and tick it off when it is done. Small goals you can keep beat big goals you abandon.

What to study after your result

Your result gives you a level and a study plan, but here is the general shape of what comes next at each level. At A1 and A2, focus on everyday vocabulary and the most common grammar: present and past tenses, questions, and the words of daily life. Learn the words before the rules, and practise speaking from the first week.

At B1, widen your vocabulary into the topics you actually talk about — work, travel, hobbies, news — and learn to connect sentences: because, although, however, and so. Start reading longer texts and listening to real speech, not only course recordings.

At B2 and C1, the work is precision: choosing the right word instead of a close one, writing clearly, and understanding fast natural speech. Read widely, write regularly, and ask a good speaker to correct you. At these levels, feedback matters more than new rules.

Common mistakes

  • Studying grammar only. Grammar without vocabulary leaves you with perfect sentences about nothing. Learn words and grammar together, in real sentences.
  • Never speaking. Reading and listening are not enough. Speaking uses different skills, and they only grow by speaking — even if that means talking to yourself at first.
  • Memorising lists without using them. A word you have never used is a word you will forget. Use each new word in a sentence of your own the day you learn it.
  • Comparing yourself with others. Everyone learns at a different speed, and everyone has a different starting point. Compare yourself with where you were last month, not with someone else.
  • Stopping after a bad result. A low score is information, not a judgement. It tells you exactly what to study next, which is the most useful thing a test can do.
  • Only studying what is easy. It feels good to practise what you already know, but progress comes from the hard parts. Start with your weakest area, even when it feels uncomfortable.

Official tests

When you need proof of your level — for a job, a university, or a visa — you need an official exam, not a self-check. The best-known English tests are IELTS, the Duolingo English Test, and the Cambridge English exams. IELTS is accepted by universities and employers around the world and tests listening, reading, writing and speaking. The Duolingo English Test is taken online and gives a quick result. Cambridge English exams cover every level from A2 to C2 and never expire.

TOEFL is another well-known test, widely used for university admission. Whichever test you choose, prepare for its format as well as its content: official practice tests show you the question types, the timing, and the way answers are marked. A good score comes from knowing the test as well as knowing the language.

Use this self-check first to find your level, then pick the official test that fits your goal and your budget. Your study plan here is a good place to start preparing.

What this assessment cannot do

This is a self-check, not an official exam. Twenty quick questions can give you a rough level and a useful study plan, but they cannot measure everything: they do not test your speaking, your listening, or your writing, and they cannot give you a certificate or a score that a school or employer will accept.

The level bands are a guide. A score of 12 and a score of 13 fall on different sides of the B1 and B2 line, but the difference between them is one question, and a different set of twenty questions might have given you a different score. Treat your level as a starting point for study, not as a final label. If you need a level you can prove, take an official test.

Questions people ask

What do A1, B2 and C1 mean?

A1 is beginner, B2 is upper intermediate, and C1 is advanced. The scale runs from A1 to C2: A levels are basic, B levels are independent, and C levels are advanced. This assessment gives levels from A1 to C1.

Is this an official test?

No. This is a self-check that gives you a rough level and a study plan. It cannot give you a certificate or a score that a school or employer will accept. For proof of your level, take an official test such as IELTS, the Duolingo English Test or a Cambridge English exam.

How long does the assessment take?

About ten minutes. There are 20 questions, and there is no time limit, so you can take as long as you need on each one.

What should I study after my result?

Start with your weakest area, which your study plan puts first. Then work through the plan in order, a little every day, and retake the assessment in a few weeks to see your progress.

How long does it take to move up a level?

It depends on how much you practise and where you start, but moving up one level usually takes months of regular study, not days. A little practice every day moves you faster than occasional long sessions.

Can I retake it?

Yes, as often as you like. Retake it after a few weeks of study to see whether your score and your level have moved. Your answers are not saved, so each attempt starts fresh.
