How to Fix a Leaky Faucet

A dripping faucet is usually a worn washer or cartridge, and the fix depends on the faucet type. Pick your type below for the right steps, starting with turning the water off.

If you cannot find the shut-off valve, or the valve itself leaks, stop and call a plumber. Never force a stuck part: a snapped fitting turns a small job into a flood.

Before you start

Turn the water off first, and check that it is off. There is usually a shut-off valve under the sink; turn it clockwise until it stops, then open the faucet to let the remaining water out. If the faucet keeps running, the valve may be faulty, and that is a job for a plumber, not a reason to carry on regardless.

Plug the drain before you take anything apart. Faucet screws are small, and a dropped screw down an open drain ends the job early. Lay a cloth in the basin to catch parts and to protect the finish, and photograph each step as you go: the photos are your reassembly guide.

Finding the shut-off

Most sinks have a shut-off valve on the pipes underneath, one for hot and one for cold. Turn both off for a mixer faucet, or just the one feeding the dripping side for separate taps. If there is no valve under the sink, the main shut-off for the house will be near where the water enters the building, often by the meter.

Test that the water is truly off by opening the faucet and waiting a few seconds. A short dribble is normal as the pipes empty; a steady flow means the valve is not holding, and you should stop and call a plumber rather than opening the faucet under pressure.

The fix, step by step

Whatever the type, the rhythm is the same: take the handle off, get to the washer or cartridge, replace it with a matching part, and put everything back in the same order. Work slowly and keep the parts in order on the cloth as they come off.

  1. Turn off the water and open the faucet to empty the pipes.
  2. Plug the drain and lay a cloth in the basin to catch small parts.
  3. Remove the handle: look for a screw under a cap or on the side of the handle.
  4. Unscrew the retaining nut and lift out the washer or cartridge.
  5. Take the old part with you to match it exactly, and fit the new one the same way round.
  6. Refit the parts in reverse order, tightening firmly but not forcing anything.
  7. Turn the water back on slowly and check for drips before you call it done.

Washers and cartridges

The washer or cartridge is the part that actually stops the water, and it is the part that wears out. Washers are small rubber rings that harden and crack with age; cartridges are sealed units that wear inside. In both cases, the fix is a matching replacement, and matching matters: a washer that is slightly the wrong size will drip just as badly as the old one.

Take the old part with you when you buy the replacement, or photograph it next to a ruler. If you cannot find an exact match, a plumber's merchant can usually identify it from the old part or from the faucet's make and model. Do not guess: a wrong cartridge can damage the faucet body.

If it still drips

If a new washer or cartridge does not stop the drip, the seat inside the faucet body may be worn or pitted. The seat is the surface the washer presses against, and a damaged seat will not seal no matter how new the washer is. A seat can sometimes be smoothed with a seat wrench, but if it is badly pitted, the faucet may need replacing.

Also check that you fitted the part the right way round, and that you did not overtighten the handle, which can damage a new washer on the first turn. If the drip continues after a careful second try, it is time to call a plumber rather than keep taking the faucet apart.

Questions people ask

Why does my faucet drip?

Usually a worn washer or cartridge. Washers harden and crack with age, and cartridges wear inside, so the faucet no longer seals fully when closed. Replacing the worn part with an exact match is the usual fix.

How do I find the shut-off valve?

Look under the sink for a valve on the pipes, one for hot and one for cold. Turn it clockwise until it stops. If there is no valve under the sink, use the main shut-off near where the water enters the house.

Do I need to replace the whole faucet?

Usually not. Most drips are fixed by replacing a washer or a cartridge. A whole new faucet is only needed if the faucet body or the seat inside it is damaged, or if parts are no longer available.

What if I cannot identify my faucet type?

Photograph the faucet and the part you removed, and take the old part with you when you buy a replacement. A plumber's merchant can usually identify the type from the old part or from the faucet's make and model.

How do I stop the drip coming back?

Do not overtighten the handle: forcing it damages the washer. Turn the faucet off firmly but gently, and replace washers when they start to harden rather than waiting for a drip.
