30 AI Video Prompt Examples: Shots, Movement, and Style

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

A strong AI video prompt has three parts: the shot, the movement, and the style. This page gives you thirty original prompts in five groups, nature, people, products, cities, and fantasy, each broken into those three parts so you can see exactly what each part does and adapt it to your own videos.

Most weak video prompts fail for the same reason: they describe a subject but not a shot. "A cat in a garden" tells the generator what to show, but not how to show it, how it should move, or how it should feel. The result is a generic clip that could have come from any prompt. Adding a shot, a movement, and a style turns the same idea into a video with a point of view.

Every prompt below follows the same pattern. The bold line is the full prompt, ready to copy. Underneath it, the shot, movement, and style parts are labelled, with a short note on what each part contributes. Once you see the pattern across thirty examples, you will be able to build your own prompts the same way.

Honest limit: prompts are interpreted differently by different video generators, and the same prompt can produce very different clips on different tools. Treat these prompts as starting points: test them on your own tool, change one part at a time, and expect to regenerate a prompt a few times before the clip looks right.

How to read these prompts

Each prompt has three labelled parts. The shot describes what the camera sees: the subject, the framing, and the setting. Words like wide shot, close-up, aerial view, and low angle belong here. The shot decides the composition of the clip.

The movement describes what moves and how. It covers both the movement of the subject, such as walking, swaying, or rising, and the movement of the camera, such as pushing in, panning, or orbiting. Movement words like slowly, gently, and steadily set the pace. This part is what makes a video feel like a video rather than a still image.

The style describes how the clip should look and feel: cinematic, documentary, warm, moody, or playful. Style words cover lighting, colour, and mood. They are the finishing touch that gives a clip its character.

When you adapt a prompt, change one part at a time. Swap the subject while keeping the movement and style, or keep the shot and change the style. Single changes teach you what each part does, and they keep the prompt working while you experiment.

Nature prompts

  1. A wide shot of a waterfall in a green valley at sunrise, mist rising from the pool below, camera slowly pushing in, cinematic golden light.
    Shot: wide shot of a waterfall in a valley at sunrise. Movement: camera slowly pushing in; mist rising. Style: cinematic golden light. The slow push-in gives a calm, majestic feel, and the mist adds gentle motion to a scene that could otherwise feel still.
  2. An aerial view drifting over a pine forest after rain, sunlight breaking through the clouds, camera gliding forward steadily, fresh and vivid colours.
    Shot: aerial view over a pine forest after rain. Movement: camera gliding forward steadily. Style: fresh and vivid colours. Aerial shots suit slow, steady camera movement, and the breaking sunlight gives the clip a clear focal point.
  3. A close-up of a honeybee on a lavender flower, wings blurring gently, shallow depth of field, warm afternoon light, soft and detailed.
    Shot: close-up of a bee on lavender with shallow depth of field. Movement: wings blurring gently. Style: warm afternoon light, soft and detailed. Close-ups work well when the subject is small and the movement is subtle.
  4. A low-angle shot of tall grass swaying in a summer breeze, clouds moving slowly across a blue sky, camera fixed, peaceful and bright.
    Shot: low-angle shot of tall grass under a moving sky. Movement: grass swaying; clouds drifting slowly. Style: peaceful and bright. A fixed camera lets the natural movement of the scene carry the clip.
  5. A time-lapse style shot of stars wheeling over a desert at night, a small tent glowing warmly below, camera fixed, deep blue and amber tones.
    Shot: fixed shot of a desert night sky over a glowing tent. Movement: stars wheeling slowly overhead. Style: deep blue and amber tones. Contrasting a still foreground with a moving sky creates depth and calm.
  6. A tracking shot following a river as it winds through autumn woods, leaves floating on the surface, camera moving smoothly alongside, rich orange and gold colours.
    Shot: tracking shot of a river through autumn woods. Movement: camera moving smoothly alongside; leaves floating. Style: rich orange and gold colours. The tracking camera turns a simple river scene into a journey.

How to adapt any prompt

Every prompt on this page is a template, not a finished product. The fastest way to make them yours is to change one part at a time. Keep the shot and the movement, and swap the subject: the waterfall prompt becomes a prompt about a lighthouse, a windmill, or a city fountain. Keep the subject and the movement, and change the style: the same harbour scene can be cinematic, documentary, or playful.

Changing the movement changes the feeling most. A slow push-in feels calm and intimate. A fast tracking shot feels energetic. A fixed camera feels observational, like watching through a window. If a clip feels wrong, the movement is usually the part to adjust first.

When you adapt a prompt for a specific tool, check whether the tool has its own way of handling camera words. Some tools have camera controls built in, so you can set the movement with a button instead of writing it in the prompt. In that case, keep the prompt focused on the subject and the style, and let the controls handle the camera.

Finally, save the prompts that work. A small collection of tested prompts, grouped by the kind of video you make most often, is worth more than a long list of untested ideas. Over time, you will develop a personal prompt vocabulary that reliably produces the clips you want.

Questions people ask

What are the three parts of a good video prompt?

A good video prompt describes the shot, the movement, and the style. The shot says what the camera sees and how it is framed. The movement says what moves and how. The style says how the clip should look and feel, such as cinematic, warm, or documentary.

How many movements should one prompt ask for?

One or two movements per prompt works best. A single clear movement, such as a slow camera push-in or a gentle pan, gives the model a target it can hit. Asking for many movements at once usually produces a confused clip where nothing moves cleanly.

Can I use these prompts on any video generator?

Yes, the prompts are written in plain words that work on any text-to-video or image-to-video tool. Results will vary between tools, because each one interprets words differently, so treat each prompt as a starting point and adjust the wording for your tool.

How do I keep characters consistent across several prompts?

Reuse the same descriptive words for the character in every prompt, keep the same style words, and use image-to-video from a single starting image when you can. Generate extra takes for each shot so you can pick the clips that match best.

What should I do if a prompt produces a bad clip?

Change one part at a time. Simplify the movement first, then simplify the scene, and try again. A prompt that fails twice usually has too much in it, so strip it back to one subject, one movement, and one style, and build up from there.
