Solar Eclipse 2027: Where to Watch

By the Noryvello team · Published 19 November 2026 · Updated 19 November 2026

The total solar eclipse of 2 August 2027 sweeps from southern Spain across North Africa and the Middle East to north-eastern Somalia, with the longest totality on land near Luxor in Egypt. This guide covers the path, the best places to stand, and how to plan a trip around the day.

A total solar eclipse is one of the most remarkable things the sky can do. For a few minutes, the Moon covers the Sun completely, daylight turns to a strange twilight, and the Sun's outer atmosphere becomes visible around the dark disc of the Moon. It only happens if you are standing inside the path of totality, a band about 258 kilometres wide that the Moon's shadow sweeps across the Earth.

On Monday 2 August 2027 that band crosses southern Europe, North Africa and the Middle East. This guide follows the path from west to east, explains what each region offers, and gives practical advice for planning a trip. Wherever you choose, the one rule is the same: be inside the path, because outside it even a 99 percent eclipse is still daylight.

Never look at the Sun without certified protection, except during the brief minutes of totality. This guide is general information; check local advice and conditions before you travel, and read the safety guide before the day.

The path of totality

The path of totality on 2 August 2027 runs from west to east across three continents. It begins in southern Spain, crosses the Strait of Gibraltar, then sweeps across Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia and Libya, crosses Egypt, continues over Saudi Arabia and Yemen, and ends in north-eastern Somalia. The band is about 258 kilometres wide, which sounds generous until you try to place yourself inside it: a few kilometres either way is the difference between totality and a partial eclipse.

The timing of totality moves with the shadow, so the eclipse happens at different local times along the path. The longest totality on land is about 6 minutes 23 seconds, in the Egyptian desert near Luxor, and it is the longest total solar eclipse visible from land until 2114. Greatest eclipse, the moment of maximum coverage, happens near Luxor at about 10:07 UTC. Wherever you stand, totality lasts only minutes, so plan to be in place well before it begins.

Spain: the eclipse comes to Europe

For most people in Europe, southern Spain is the easiest place to reach totality. The path crosses the provinces of Cádiz, Málaga, Granada and Almería, and it also includes Ceuta and Melilla on the North African coast. Gibraltar, at the southern tip of the Iberian Peninsula, is inside the path too. These are places with good roads, plenty of places to stay, and a long summer season, which makes planning simpler than in more remote parts of the path.

Spain in early August is hot and usually dry, which is good news for eclipse watchers, because clear skies are what matter most. The trade-off is crowds: a total eclipse in accessible Europe will draw large numbers of visitors, so book travel and accommodation early, and plan to reach your viewing spot the day before rather than the morning of the eclipse. Coastal cities and inland towns both offer good views, so choose by what suits your trip rather than chasing a particular town.

North Africa: Morocco to Libya

After Spain, the shadow crosses Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia and Libya. Morocco offers the most accessible viewing in this stretch, with good roads and towns inside the path, and the Atlas Mountains providing dramatic backdrops for those who want them. Algeria and Tunisia are also crossed by the path, and Libya's stretch is long, though travel there needs careful planning and up-to-date local advice.

North Africa in August is hot and generally dry, and the desert regions offer some of the clearest skies on the whole path. The practical advice is the same as anywhere: arrive early, carry water, protect yourself from the sun, and have a backup viewing spot in case of local cloud. In desert areas, distances are long and services are thin, so carry more water and fuel than you think you need.

Egypt and Luxor: the longest totality on land

Egypt is where the eclipse is at its most generous. The longest totality on land, about 6 minutes 23 seconds, happens in the Egyptian desert near Luxor, and in Luxor itself totality runs from about 1:02 to 1:08 p.m. local time, lasting about 6 minutes 20 seconds. The Sun will be about 82 degrees high, nearly overhead, which means you will be looking almost straight up at the moment of totality.

Egypt has the clearest skies in August anywhere on the path, which is why so many eclipse watchers are heading there. The trade-off is heat: Luxor at midday in August can reach about 43 degrees Celsius, so shade, water and sun protection are not optional extras but essentials. Luxor also offers the temples and tombs of ancient Egypt within reach of the path, which makes it the most popular destination of the whole eclipse. Expect crowds, book early, and plan your viewing spot in advance.

Saudi Arabia, Yemen and Somalia

After Egypt, the shadow crosses the Red Sea into Saudi Arabia, then Yemen, and finally north-eastern Somalia, where the path leaves land. These stretches are less travelled by eclipse watchers, and they need more careful planning, including up-to-date travel advice and local knowledge. Saudi Arabia's part of the path crosses desert regions with clear skies, and the same heat and water advice applies as in Egypt.

For most travellers, the practical choice is between Spain, Morocco and Egypt, where access is easiest and the viewing is well understood. The eastern end of the path suits experienced eclipse chasers who know the region and have planned carefully. Wherever you go, the eclipse itself is the same event; what changes is the journey, the weather odds, and how long totality lasts.

Places that miss out

It is worth knowing which famous places are not in the path. Aswan, in southern Egypt, is not in the path of totality and will see only a deep partial eclipse. Cairo, Egypt's capital, sees only a partial eclipse too. Both are worth visiting for their own sake, but neither will show you totality, and the difference between a deep partial eclipse and totality is the difference between an interesting afternoon and an unforgettable one.

The reason is simple: even when 99 percent of the Sun is covered, the remaining one percent is still blindingly bright, and the sky stays blue. Only inside the path does the Sun's disc disappear completely, the sky darken, and the corona appear. If totality is what you want, check your location against the path carefully, and do not trust a rough guess: a few kilometres can matter.

Planning your trip

Start planning early. A total eclipse that crosses accessible Europe and Egypt will draw large crowds, and accommodation near the centre of the path will fill up well before August 2027. Book travel and a place to stay first, then plan your viewing spot. Choose a spot with a clear view of the sky in the direction of the Sun at the time of totality, and arrive the day before if you can.

Pack for heat and sun: water, shade, a hat, and sunscreen. Bring certified eclipse glasses, and a pinhole viewer as a backup way to watch. Have a backup location in mind within the path in case of cloud on the day, and keep your plans flexible enough to move. Finally, remember that the eclipse lasts minutes: practise with your glasses and your camera beforehand, so that when totality comes you spend it watching the sky, not fumbling with equipment.

Questions people ask

What time is totality in Luxor?

In Luxor, totality runs from about 1:02 to 1:08 p.m. local time, lasting about 6 minutes 20 seconds. The Sun will be about 82 degrees high, nearly overhead.

How long does totality last?

The longest totality on land is about 6 minutes 23 seconds, in the Egyptian desert near Luxor. It is the longest total solar eclipse visible from land until 2114. Totality is shorter elsewhere along the path.

Can I watch the eclipse from Cairo or Aswan?

Both cities see only a partial eclipse. Aswan is not in the path of totality, and Cairo sees only a partial eclipse. For totality you must be inside the path.

Is Spain a good place to watch the eclipse?

Yes. Southern Spain, including Cádiz, Málaga, Granada and Almería, is inside the path, along with Ceuta, Melilla and Gibraltar. Spain in early August is usually dry, with good odds of clear skies.

What if it is cloudy on the day?

Have a backup viewing spot in mind within the path, and be ready to move. Egypt has the clearest skies in August anywhere on the path, which is one reason so many watchers are heading there.

Do I need eclipse glasses?

Yes. Only look at the Sun through ISO 12312-2 certified eclipse glasses, except during the brief minutes of totality. Ordinary sunglasses are never safe. Read the safety guide before the day.
