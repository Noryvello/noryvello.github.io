HR Document Guide

This guide explains which HR documents a small employer should keep, how long to keep them, and how to store them safely. It is general information only, not legal advice, and record-keeping rules differ by place, so always check local law.

What this guide covers and who needs it

Every employer collects papers about the people who work for them. A signed contract, a copy of an identity document, a leave request, a training certificate, a note about a pay change — each one is small on its own, but together they tell the story of a person's time at work. HR document management is simply the habit of keeping those papers in order: knowing what to keep, where it lives, who may see it, and when it is safe to let it go. This guide is written for small employers, the owner of a shop, a clinic, a workshop or a small office, who may have no HR department at all and who keep the files themselves.

Good filing is not about neatness for its own sake. It protects the business and the employee alike. When a question comes up about pay, leave, a warning or a reference, the answer is in the file, and it takes a minute to find instead of a day of searching. When an inspector, an auditor or a former employee asks for a record, a complete file answers the question calmly and in full. And when a dispute reaches a lawyer or a tribunal, the file is often the difference between a story and a proof. Keeping documents well is one of the cheapest forms of protection a small business can buy, because it costs mostly time and care.

How HR filing works, step by step

Start with one file per employee, and keep the same structure for everyone. A file usually has four parts: hiring papers, the employment record, pay and leave records, and anything about performance or discipline. Hiring papers are the application, the interview notes, the offer letter and the signed contract. The employment record holds identity and contact details, emergency contacts, and any work-permit or licence copies the job requires. Pay and leave records hold pay slips, tax forms, leave requests and attendance notes. Performance and discipline hold appraisals, training certificates, warnings and any written agreements that followed a problem.

The second step is to decide where each part lives and who may open it. Paper files belong in a locked cabinet, not on an open shelf, and digital files belong in a folder that only named people can open. A simple rule works well: the employee's own line manager and the person who runs payroll see what they need for their job, and nobody else browses the files. Keep a short written note of who has access, and review it when someone changes roles or leaves. Access is not about mistrust; it is about protecting private information from casual eyes and from accidents.

The third step is a routine: file things the day they arrive, review each file once a year, and remove what has passed its keeping time. A yearly review takes an hour or two for a small team and keeps the cabinet honest. During the review, check that every file has a current contract, that contact details are up to date, and that nothing is missing from the year just past. Shred or securely delete what you no longer need to keep, and write down what was destroyed and when, so there is a record of the destruction itself.

A simple file structure for each employee:

  • Hiring: application, interview notes, offer letter, signed contract
  • Identity and contact: ID copy, address, emergency contact, work permit if any
  • Pay and leave: pay slips, tax forms, leave requests, attendance notes
  • Performance: appraisals, training certificates, warnings, agreements
  • Leaving: resignation or termination letter, final pay record, reference copy

Costs, time and what you need before you start

Setting up a proper filing system costs very little money. For paper, you need a lockable cabinet or a locking drawer, a set of folders and labels, and a shredder or a secure destruction service for papers you no longer keep. For digital files, you need a folder structure on a computer or drive you control, with access limited to named people, and a backup that runs regularly. The real cost is time: expect an afternoon to set up the structure and to sort any backlog, and a few minutes each week to file new papers as they arrive.

Before you start, gather three things. First, the rules that apply to your business: how long your local law expects you to keep payroll and tax records, and whether any industry rule adds to that. Second, a list of everyone who works for you now, so no one is missed. Third, a decision about paper, digital or both. Many small employers keep paper originals for signed contracts and use digital copies for day-to-day use. Whatever you choose, the rule is the same: one place, one structure, and no papers left in desk drawers or inboxes.

What to have ready before you start:

  • The local retention rules for payroll, tax and employment records
  • A list of all current employees and recent leavers
  • Folders or a digital folder structure, one per employee
  • A lockable cabinet or access-controlled digital folder
  • A simple log for recording what is destroyed and when

Worked example: setting up files for a team of eight

Maya runs a small bakery with eight employees, and until last year her HR papers lived in three places: a drawer in the office, a box under the counter, and her own inbox. When a former employee asked for a copy of a pay record, Maya spent a whole morning looking and never found it. That weekend she set up a proper system. She bought a locking filing cabinet with eight hanging folders, one per employee, and four dividers in each folder for hiring, identity, pay and leave, and performance. She also created a matching set of folders on her computer for scanned copies.

Sorting the backlog took one Sunday afternoon. She found two contracts that had never been signed, one employee whose emergency contact was five years out of date, and a stack of leave requests that had never been filed. She asked the two employees to sign their contracts, updated the emergency contact, and filed the leave requests in date order. She then wrote a one-page note for herself: file new papers on Fridays, review all files every January, and keep a log of anything destroyed. The note sits on top of the cabinet, where she sees it every week.

Six months later the system paid for itself. A new employee asked how much leave she had left, and Maya answered in a minute from the file. A tax inspection asked for two years of pay records, and Maya handed over a tidy folder the same day. The whole setup had cost one cabinet, one afternoon, and a small weekly habit — and the peace of mind was worth far more than either.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The first common mistake is keeping everything forever. A cabinet full of ten-year-old papers is not a filing system; it is a hiding place. Old papers cost space, slow down every search, and increase the risk if the cabinet is ever opened by the wrong person. Set a keeping time for each type of document, write it down, and destroy what has passed its time. The second mistake is the opposite: throwing papers away too soon. A contract, a pay record or a warning that is gone cannot be produced when it is needed, and its absence can hurt the employer more than the employee.

The third mistake is mixing personal and business papers, or mixing one employee's papers with another's. A file should hold one person's employment papers and nothing else. The fourth mistake is leaving access open: files on a shared drive that everyone can read, or a cabinet that is never locked, invite accidents and breaches of privacy. The fifth mistake is filing by memory instead of by routine. Papers filed the day they arrive are found; papers left in a pile are lost. A small weekly habit beats a yearly rescue operation every time.

A practical filing checklist you can follow

Use this checklist when a new employee joins, and again at each yearly review. It covers the documents most small employers keep, in the order they are usually needed. Adapt it to your own business and to local rules, and treat it as a starting point, not a legal requirement.

  • Application and interview notes filed on the hiring side of the folder
  • Offer letter and signed contract in place before the first working day
  • Identity copy and emergency contact recorded and checked
  • Pay details, tax forms and bank details filed with the pay records
  • Leave requests filed in date order as they are approved
  • Training certificates and appraisals added as they happen
  • Warnings or agreements filed the same day, with dates and signatures
  • Yearly review done: contacts current, contract current, nothing missing
  • Records past their keeping time destroyed and logged

Tips from real practice

The best tip from real practice is to file little and often. A folder that receives one sheet a week stays tidy on its own; a folder that receives a year's worth at once never does. Keep the cabinet or the digital folder close to where the papers arrive, so filing takes seconds rather than a trip. If a document arrives by message or email, save or print it the same day, because messages are the easiest place to lose a record.

The second tip is to write dates on everything. A contract without a date, a warning without a date, or a leave request without a date loses much of its value, because the question is almost always when something happened. The third tip is to keep the system simple enough that someone else could use it. If you were away for a month, could a colleague find a pay record in five minutes? If the answer is no, the system needs simplifying, not more folders. Simple, dated, and filed on the day — that is the whole secret of HR document management.

Questions people ask

What HR documents should I keep for each employee?

Keep the contract, the offer letter, identity and contact details, pay and tax records, leave requests, training certificates, appraisals, and any warnings or agreements. Keep them in one file per employee, in the same order for everyone, so any record can be found in a minute. Do not keep papers that have nothing to do with the employment.

How long should I keep old employee records?

It depends on local law, and the rules differ by place. Common starting points many small employers use are keeping payroll and tax records for several years after the tax year, and keeping contracts and key employment records for some years after a person leaves. Check the rules where your business operates, write down the periods you choose, and destroy records only when their time has passed.

How should HR documents be stored safely?

Paper files belong in a locked cabinet, and digital files in a folder that only named people can open. Keep a backup of digital files, and keep the backup somewhere separate from the original. Never leave HR papers on a shared drive that everyone can read, and never leave a cabinet unlocked overnight.

Who is allowed to see HR documents?

Only the people who need them for their job: usually the employer or manager, the person who runs payroll, and the employee themselves for their own file. Nobody else should browse the files. Write down who has access, and review the list when someone changes roles or leaves.

What happens to documents when someone leaves?

Move the file to a leavers section and keep it for the retention period you have set, then destroy it securely and log the destruction. Keep the final pay record and the resignation or termination letter with the file. Do not delete a leaver's records the day they walk out, because questions about pay or references often come later.

Can I store HR papers online?

You can, but only in a service you trust, with access limited to named people and with a strong password and a second sign-in step where available. This page itself stores nothing online and collects no documents. Wherever you store them, keep a backup and know how to get your files back if the service stops.
