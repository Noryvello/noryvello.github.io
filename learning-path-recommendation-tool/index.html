What this is

A learning path is a step-by-step route from where you are now to a goal you can actually finish. It answers the two questions every learner asks first: what do I do next, and how long will it take? Without a path, learning is a pile of videos, articles and good intentions with no order — and no order means no finish.

This page gives you a working path recommender in your browser. Pick a goal, your level and the time you can give each week, and the tool matches your answers to a fixed library of honest paths — each one a short list of steps with time estimates, from basics through practice to a real project at the end.

One thing to be clear about from the start: this tool matches your answers to a fixed list of paths written in advance. It is not AI, it does not learn, it does not know you, and it cannot judge your ability. A tool that pretended to know you would be a tool you should not trust — this one says plainly what it is, and the path it gives you is a starting point to adjust, not a verdict.

How to use the tool

Start with the goal. Pick the one that matches what you want to be able to do — not the one that sounds impressive, and not the one someone else thinks you should want. The six goals in the tool cover the most common starting points, and each one has the same shape: basics, practice, project.

Then pick your level honestly. Beginner means starting from the beginning, with no assumed knowledge. Intermediate means you know the basics and want the practice. Advanced means you can already do the thing and want a harder project. Picking a level too high is the most common mistake — the path will skip the foundations you actually need.

Then pick the time you can really give each week — not the time you wish you had, but the time your week actually holds. The tool turns your hours into a week-by-week plan, so the path fits your life instead of demanding a life you do not have. Tap Show my path, read the steps, and copy the plan to keep.

Read the first step, then do it. A path only works when the first step happens today, or tomorrow at the latest. The rest of the steps will wait — but a path with no first step is just a list.

Picking a goal you can finish

A good goal is small enough to finish and big enough to matter. 'Learn to code' is too big to finish — it has no edge, no end, and no moment when you can say you did it. 'Build a small website for my local club' is a goal: it has a finish line, a thing you can show, and a date you can aim for.

The test is the finished thing. Ask yourself: when this path is done, what will exist that does not exist now? A page, a song, a photo series, a first customer, a walk completed. If the answer is vague, the goal is vague, and a vague goal is the first thing a busy week will eat.

Give the goal a deadline — not a threat, but a shape. 'By the end of next month' turns a wish into a plan, and a plan into a habit. The tool's week estimates help here: they turn 'someday' into 'about six weeks', and six weeks is a length a person can hold in their head.

One goal at a time. Five paths at once is five unfinished paths, and unfinished paths teach you that you never finish anything. Pick one, finish it, and let the finish teach you that you can.

The path structure (basics, practice, project)

Every path in the tool has the same three-part shape, because learning has the same three-part shape. First come the basics: the small set of ideas and moves everything else rests on. Basics are boring and essential in equal measure — skip them and the practice stage wobbles; rush them and you rebuild them later, slowly.

Then comes practice: doing the thing, badly at first, regularly. Practice is where the learning actually happens — not in reading about it, not in watching someone else do it, but in the doing. The paths put practice in the middle on purpose, with enough hours for the doing to become familiar.

Then comes the project: one finished thing, made by you, that pulls the basics and the practice together. The project is the point of the path — it is the proof, to yourself more than anyone, that you can do the thing. A path without a project ends in knowledge; a path with one ends in something real.

The week estimates follow the same shape: basics take less time than you fear, practice takes more time than you hope, and the project takes exactly as long as it takes. Adjust as you go — the estimates are a map, not a timetable.

When to change path

Change path when the goal stops fitting. Interests change, life changes, and a path you abandon for a better one is not a failure — it is a decision. The skills you gained carry over: the discipline of daily practice, the habit of finishing small things, the knowledge of how you learn best.

Change path when you are stuck in the same place for weeks. Being stuck on one step usually means the step is too big, not that you are too slow. Split it, find a smaller version, or skip ahead and come back — a path is a plan, not a prison, and the order of steps is a suggestion, not a law.

Do not change path because the middle feels hard. Every path has a flat stretch in the middle where the excitement of starting has worn off and the finish is still far away. That stretch is where most learning happens and where most people quit. The way through it is smaller steps and shorter sessions — not a new path, just a smaller next move.

The question to ask is simple: do I still want the finished thing? If yes, keep going, slower if needed. If no, stop honestly, keep what you learned, and pick a goal you do want. Either answer is a good one — the bad answer is drifting, neither going nor stopping.

A worked example

Tom wants to learn to code. He picks the goal 'Learn to code', level Beginner, and 3 to 5 hours a week. The tool gives him six steps over about twelve weeks: how code runs, small programs every day, organising code, a small project of his own, sharing it for feedback, and a next topic.

The first step takes about two weeks at his pace: variables, loops and decisions, the three ideas everything else rests on. He does a little each evening, and by the end of the second week he has written his first small programs — not impressive ones, but his.

The middle steps take longer than the estimates — practice always does — and Tom slows down rather than quitting. He skips nothing, but he stretches the project step across three weeks instead of two. Twelve weeks become fourteen, and nobody minds, because the estimate was a map, not a deadline.

At the end, Tom has a small working program of his own, shared with two friends who tried it and told him what broke. It is not much, and it is everything: the first finished thing, and the proof that the next path is possible too.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is a goal with no deadline. 'Learn photography someday' is not a goal, it is a wish, and wishes wait forever. Give the goal a date, even a rough one, and the path gets a shape.

The second is five paths at once. Five goals started together means five goals competing for the same hours, and none of them finishing. One path, finished, teaches more than five paths started.

The third is picking a level too high. Starting as 'intermediate' because beginner feels embarrassing skips the foundations you actually need, and the practice stage wobbles. Beginner is not an insult — it is a starting line.

The fourth is measuring progress in hours watched instead of things made. Hours of video feel like learning and mostly are not. Measure the path in finished steps and finished projects, and the hours will take care of themselves.

The fifth is treating the path as a contract. A path that cannot bend will break: skip what you know, slow down where you struggle, swap the final project for one you care about. The goal matters more than the route.

Questions people ask

How does the recommendation work?

You pick a goal, your level and your weekly time, and the tool matches your answers to a fixed library of paths, each with steps and time estimates. It is a lookup, not a judgement — the path is a starting point you adjust as you go.

Is this AI?

No. This tool matches your answers to a fixed list of paths written in advance. It does not learn, it does not know you, and it cannot judge your ability — and it says so plainly, because a tool that pretends to know you is a tool you should not trust.

What if the path does not fit me?

Then change it. Skip a step you already know, slow down where you struggle, and swap the final project for one you actually care about. A path is a plan, not a contract — the goal matters more than the route.

How much time do I need per week?

One to two hours a week is enough to make steady progress on most paths, and three to five hours moves noticeably faster. What matters more than the hours is the habit: a little, regularly, beats a lot once in a while.

What is a good first project?

A good first project is small, finished and yours: one page, one song, one photo series, one customer. It should take days, not months, and leave you with something real to show — that is what keeps you going.

Can I change my path later?

Yes, and you should if the goal stops fitting. Learning is not a straight line: interests change, life changes, and a path you abandon for a better one is not a failure. Keep the skills you gained and carry them to the next path.
