How to use this generator


Start at the top of the page. Type the name of your site, then pick its type: a blog, a shop, or a tool site. Then write, in your own words, what visitors may do on your site and what they may not do. If you leave those two boxes blank, the generator fills in sensible defaults — personal, lawful use is allowed, and copying or reselling the site's content without permission is not. Next, pick the payment and refund rule that matches your site: no payments at all, refunds within fourteen or thirty days, exchange only, or no refunds. Finally, add a contact method, so visitors know how to reach you.


Tap the Generate button and your terms appear in the box below, written in plain words with your details filled in. Read them through, slowly, and check that every rule is one you actually follow. If something is wrong, change your answers and tap Generate again — you can do this as many times as you like. When you are happy, tap Copy terms to copy the text, or Download as text to save it as a plain text file you can paste into a page on your site.


Everything happens in your browser. Nothing you type is sent to a server, stored, or shared — the terms are built on your own device, and they leave your device only when you copy or download them. What terms must cover


Good terms cover five things. First, the use of the site: what visitors may do, and what they may not do. This is the heart of the page — the rules that keep the site working for everyone. Second, the content: who owns the words, pictures, and tools on the site, and what visitors may do with them. Third, the limits of what you promise: the site is provided as it is, and you cannot guarantee it will never break or that every word on it is free of mistakes. Fourth, payments and refunds, if your site takes money at all: what happens when someone pays, and what happens if they want their money back. Fifth, changes: that you may update the terms, and that the version on this page is the one that counts.


The use rules should be specific enough to be useful. "Use the site lawfully" is a start, but it helps to name the things you actually care about: no copying the content to another site, no overloading the site with automated requests, no pretending to be someone else, no posting anything harmful. Each rule should be one you are ready to enforce — a rule you would never act on is clutter.


The content section matters because people assume that anything on the web is free to take. Say plainly that the content is yours, or that it belongs to its authors, and say what visitors may do with it — read it, share a link to it — and what they may not do — copy it wholesale, sell it, or claim it as their own.


The limits section is where honesty protects you. No site is perfect. Saying the site is provided as it is, and that you do your best but cannot promise perfection, is more honest — and more useful — than pretending nothing can ever go wrong.


The questions explained


The generator asks six questions, and each one shapes the terms it writes. The site name is the name at the top of your terms. If you leave it blank, the generator writes "Your Site" as a placeholder, and you should replace it with your real name before you publish.


The type of site changes the opening paragraph. A blog publishes articles people read. A shop sells goods and takes orders. A tool site helps visitors do a small task in their browser. Pick the one that fits best; if your site is a mix, pick the type that describes its main job, and edit the finished text by hand afterwards.


The two "may do" and "may not do" boxes are the rules of your site, in your own words. Write them plainly, as you would explain them to a friend. The allowed box might say visitors may read the articles, use the tools, and share links to the site. The not-allowed box might say visitors may not copy the content, overload the site, or pretend to be the site's owner. If you leave either box blank, the generator fills in a sensible default, but your own words are always better, because they describe your real rules.


The refund rule must match what you actually do. If your site takes no payments, pick the no-payments option — a refund rule for a free site is nonsense. If you do take payments, pick the rule you follow in practice: refunds within fourteen days, refunds within thirty days, exchange only, or no refunds. Do not pick a generous rule you will not honour, and do not pick a harsh rule to sound tough — the terms should describe your real practice.


The contact method is how a visitor reaches you about these terms. If you leave it blank, the generator writes the placeholder "[your email]" — replace it with a real contact method before you publish.


A worked example: a tool site's terms


Imagine a free tool site called Quick Note Tools. It offers small writing tools in the browser: a word counter, a text cleaner, and a note pad that saves to the visitor's own device. It takes no payments, has no accounts, and its content is the tools themselves plus a few short guides. Here is how its owner would use this generator.


He types "Quick Note Tools" as the site name and picks "tool site" as the type. In the allowed box he writes that visitors may use the tools for personal or work use, share links to the site, and print their own results. In the not-allowed box he writes that visitors may not copy the tools to another site, run automated requests that overload the site, or claim the tools as their own work. For the refund rule he picks no payments, because the site takes no money at all. He adds his contact address and taps Generate.


The terms that come back open with a paragraph about the tool site, then a section on using the site that carries his allowed and not-allowed rules in his own words. The payments section says plainly that the site takes no payments, so there is nothing to refund. The content section says the tools and guides belong to the site and may not be copied or resold. The terms end with a section on changes and a contact section carrying his address.


Before he publishes, he does two things. First, he reads the whole text once more and checks that every rule is one he actually follows — he removes one rule about accounts, because his site has no accounts at all. Second, he adds a short line saying the note pad saves only on the visitor's own device, because that is a fact visitors ask about. Small, true details like that are what turn a template into terms that fit the site.


Common mistakes


The most common mistake is copying terms from a site in another country and changing the name at the top. Laws differ from place to place, and terms written for one country's rules can promise things that make no sense — or are not allowed — in yours. A short set of terms written for your own site, in plain words, is worth more than a long set borrowed from abroad.


The second mistake is promising what you cannot do. Terms that guarantee the site will always work, always be accurate, or always be available are promises you cannot keep — every site breaks sometimes, and every site carries a mistake somewhere. Promise what you actually do: that you do your best, that you fix problems when you find them, and that the site is provided as it is.


The third mistake is leaving out the contact method. Terms with no way to reach you leave a visitor with a complaint and nowhere to send it. Always include a real contact method, and check that it works.


The fourth mistake is forgetting the refund rule. If your site takes money, the refund rule is the part visitors look for first when something goes wrong. A missing refund rule does not make refunds go away — it makes the argument longer. Say the rule plainly, even if the rule is no refunds.


The fifth mistake is writing terms once and never updating them. Sites change: you add a shop, you start taking payments, you add a feature that stores visitor data. Each change should bring a fresh look at the terms, because terms that describe last year's site do not protect this year's site.


What it cannot do


This generator writes a starting template in plain words. It is not legal advice, and it cannot take the place of a lawyer. It does not know the rules of your country, and terms that are fine in one place may not be enforceable in another. If your site takes money, handles other people's work, or operates as a real business, have a lawyer check the finished terms before you publish them.


It cannot check whether the terms match your site. It only knows the answers you gave it. If your site also runs a shop, takes payments through a service you did not mention, or lets visitors post their own content, the generated terms will not cover those things — and it has no way to warn you. You must read the finished text and add what is missing.


It cannot protect you from everything. Terms set out the rules and limits of your site, but they cannot remove your responsibilities under the law, and a term that is unfair, or that promises something you cannot deliver, may not hold up if it is ever tested. Write terms that are honest and fair, and get legal advice for anything serious.


Finally, it cannot keep itself up to date. When your site changes, the terms you generated do not change with it — you must come back, answer the questions again, and publish the new version. Think of this generator as a first draft, written in honest words, that you finish and keep true yourself.



Questions people ask


What are terms and conditions?
Terms and conditions are the rules of your site, written down. They say what visitors may and may not do, what you promise and what you do not promise, and what happens if something goes wrong.


Does my site need terms?
Any site that offers something — tools, downloads, a shop, or even just articles — benefits from a short set of terms. They set expectations before there is a problem, and they give you something clear to point to if a visitor misuses the site.


Is this legal advice?
No. This generator gives you a starting template in plain words, not legal advice. If your site takes money, handles other people's work, or operates as a real business, have a lawyer check the terms before you publish them.


What should terms say about refunds?
They should say the rule you actually follow: whether you take payments at all, whether refunds are possible and within how many days, or whether you offer exchanges only or no refunds. Pick the option that matches your real practice, because a promise in your terms is a promise you must keep.


Can I change my terms later?
Yes. Most sites change their terms when the site changes. Say in the terms that you may update them, publish the new version on the same page, and, for big changes, tell your regular visitors so nobody is surprised.


Do terms protect me from everything?
No. Terms set out the rules and limits of your site, but they cannot remove your responsibilities under the law, and a court may not enforce a term that is unfair or that promises something you cannot deliver. Write terms that are honest and fair, and get legal advice for anything serious.
