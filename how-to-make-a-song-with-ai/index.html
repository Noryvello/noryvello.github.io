How to Make a Song with AI, Step by Step

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

Making a song with AI means deciding whether lyrics or music come first, generating ideas, picking the best takes, shaping the structure, and adding your own touches. This guide walks through each step, including the copyright questions to check before publishing.

A song used to need an instrument, a recording setup, and years of practice. AI tools have lowered the barrier: today you can generate a melody, a backing track, and even a vocal from a few written prompts. The result will not replace a band, but it can be a real song, one you can share with friends or use in a video.

This guide covers the whole process in order, from the first idea to a finished track. It is written for beginners, and it treats the AI as a collaborator: it generates, you decide. The best AI songs come from people who keep making choices at every step.

Copyright questions around AI-generated music are still unsettled in many places, and the terms of each tool differ. Check the current terms of the tool you use, and the rules of any platform where you plan to publish, before sharing a song publicly. This page is general information, not legal advice.

Lyrics first or music first?

Every song starts with one of two things: words or music. If you have something to say, start with the lyrics. Write the story, the feeling, or the message first, then ask the tool for music that fits it. If you have a sound in mind, start with the music: generate a track, listen to it, and let the words come from the mood it creates.

Lyrics-first works well for personal songs, story songs, and anything where the message matters most. The words set the direction, and the music follows. Music-first works well for dance tracks, background songs, and anything where the feeling matters more than the words. The music sets the mood, and the lyrics fill it in.

Neither order is better in general. Try both on different songs and notice which one you enjoy more. Most songwriters lean one way, and knowing your own habit saves time: you can start where you are strongest and let the AI help with the rest.

Writing lyrics with AI help

A chatbot can help with lyrics in several ways. It can brainstorm themes, suggest a rhyme, offer a different way to say a line, or draft a whole verse for you to edit. The most useful approach is to treat it as a writing partner: give it your idea and your best lines, and ask for variations.

Be specific about what you want. "Write a verse about missing home, in simple words, four lines, with a rhyme on the second and fourth lines" will get you something usable. "Write a song" will get you something generic. The more of your own idea you put into the prompt, the more the result will sound like your song rather than anyone's song.

Always edit what the AI writes. Generated lyrics tend to be vague and full of stock phrases, because the model favours the most common ways of saying things. Replace the generic lines with details only you would know: a place, a name, a small memory. Specific details are what make lyrics feel real.

Style prompts: describing the sound

Once you have lyrics, or a mood in mind, describe the sound you want to the music tool. Name the genre, the mood, the instruments, and the pace. "A gentle acoustic folk song with guitar and soft vocals, warm and hopeful, slow tempo" is a complete brief. "A song" is not.

Match the style to the words. A sad lyric over a fast dance beat will feel wrong, and an energetic lyric over a slow ballad will drag. If you are unsure, generate the same lyrics in two different styles and listen to both. Hearing the difference teaches you more about style than any description.

Keep a note of the prompts that work. When you find a style description that produces the sound you like, save it. Over time you will build a small collection of style prompts you trust, and starting a new song becomes much faster.

Generating and picking takes

Generate several versions of your song, not just one. The same prompt can produce very different tracks, and the differences are often large: one version might have a strong chorus, another a better opening, another a nicer instrumental break. Your job is to listen and choose.

Listen to each version all the way through, and note the moments you like. A track that is weak overall may still contain a great thirty seconds. A track that is good overall may have one section that does not work. Taking notes while you listen keeps the versions straight in your head.

Do not expect the first generation to be the finished song. Think of the first few generations as sketches. You are looking for the version with the best bones: the melody, the feel, and the structure you can build on. The polish comes later, in editing.

Editing the structure

Most songs follow a simple structure: a verse, a chorus, another verse, a chorus, and an ending. Generated songs sometimes wander away from that shape, with sections that repeat too much or end in the wrong place. Editing the structure is where you turn a generated track into a song.

Listen for the strongest section and build around it. If the chorus is the best part, make sure it arrives early enough and returns often enough. If the opening is weak, cut it and start from the strongest moment. A song that starts well keeps listeners; a song that takes a minute to get going loses them.

If your tool offers stems, the separate layers of the track, use them to shape the arrangement. You can drop the drums out for a quiet verse, bring them back for the chorus, or remove a part that clashes with your vocal. Small arrangement changes make a track feel deliberate rather than accidental.

Adding your own recording

The step that makes a song yours is adding something only you can make. That might be your own voice singing the lyrics, a guitar part you play, or even a simple spoken intro. A single human element changes the whole feeling of a track: it stops being a generated file and starts being a performance.

You do not need a studio. A quiet room and a phone microphone are enough for a demo vocal. Record a few takes and pick the best, or record the chorus and let the generated vocal carry the verses. Even a rough recording adds warmth that a fully generated track cannot match.

If you do not sing, there are other ways to add yourself. Record a short spoken message, add a sound from your own life, or play a simple instrument part. The point is not perfection; it is that the song contains something real, something no one else could have made.

Before you publish: copyright questions to check

Before you share a song publicly, check three things. First, the terms of the tool you used: some tools give you full rights to what you generate, some keep certain rights, and some limit commercial use. The terms are the first place to look, and they change, so read the current version.

Second, the rules of the platform where you plan to publish. Streaming services and video platforms each have their own policies for AI-generated content, and some require you to disclose that AI was used. Check the platform's current rules before uploading.

Third, listen to your track with fresh ears and ask whether it sounds too close to a song you know. Generated music is built from patterns in existing music, and a track can resemble a real song without anyone intending it. If a melody sounds familiar, change it before publishing. These are general questions to check, not legal advice, and for anything you plan to sell, professional advice is worth getting.

Questions people ask

Should I write lyrics or music first?

Either works. Start with lyrics when the message matters most, and with music when the mood matters most. Try both on different songs and notice which order you enjoy more.

Can AI write good lyrics?

It can draft lyrics and offer variations, but generated lyrics tend to be vague. Edit them with specific details only you would know, such as a place, a name, or a small memory.

How many versions should I generate?

Generate several and listen to each one all the way through. Note the moments you like in each version, then pick the one with the best bones to build on.

Do I need to sing on my own song?

No, but adding one human element, such as your voice, an instrument, or a spoken intro, makes the song feel like yours rather than a generated file.

Can I publish an AI-made song?

Check the terms of the tool you used and the rules of the platform where you plan to publish. Copyright questions around generated music are still unsettled, so read the current terms first.
