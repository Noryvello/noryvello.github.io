ChatGPT Custom Instructions: Set It Up Once, Get Better Answers Every Time

What custom instructions are, in one paragraph

Custom instructions are two short notes you write once and ChatGPT follows in every new chat. The first note says who you are — a student, a teacher, a shop owner, a parent — and anything that shapes the answers you need. The second note says how you like answers: short or detailed, simple or technical, with examples or without. Write them once, and you stop typing the same lines into every prompt.

Think of them as a standing order at a cafe. Without them, you explain your order from scratch every visit. With them, the order is remembered, and you only speak up when you want something different. The notes are not magic — they are plain sentences, and ChatGPT treats them as standing context for each new conversation.

They are also easy to change. Your notes are not permanent, and you can edit them, switch them off, or clear them whenever your needs change. The rest of this guide shows what to write, gives six ready examples, and explains the one rule that matters most: what never to put in.

Where to find them

Custom instructions live in ChatGPT's settings. On the website, open the menu from your profile icon and look for settings, then find the personalisation or custom instructions entry. In the apps the route is similar, though the labels and the exact place can differ. The steps here describe how things commonly work; the in-app help is the authority if the screens look different.

When you open the page you will usually see two boxes. The first asks what you would like ChatGPT to know about you. The second asks how you would like ChatGPT to respond. Both are optional, and both can be short — a few sentences each is plenty. Fill in one or both, save, and the instructions apply to new chats from then on.

If you cannot find the page, check that you are signed in, and check the in-app help for the current route. Menus move between updates, and the help pages are updated with them. Once you have found the page once, it is easy to return to.

What to write about yourself (role, level, language)

The first box is about you. The most useful things to say are your role, your level, and your language. Role tells ChatGPT what you are doing: "I am a secondary school teacher" or "I run a small bakery". Level tells it how much background to assume: "I am a beginner at spreadsheets" or "I know basic Python". Language tells it which language to answer in and how formal to be.

Keep it factual and short. Two or three sentences beat a life story, because every extra line is a line ChatGPT has to weigh against your actual question. Mention only what changes the answers: your job, your level in the subject, your language, and any constraint that always applies, such as a need for simple words.

Leave out anything private. Your instructions are stored with your account, and they are not the place for addresses, phone numbers, passwords, or bank details. If a detail would worry you on a public form, it does not belong here. The section on what not to put in covers this in full.

What to write about answers (length, tone, format, examples)

The second box is about the answers. The four things worth setting are length, tone, format, and examples. Length: "Keep answers under 150 words" or "Give full detail". Tone: "Friendly and plain" or "Formal, for work". Format: "Use bullet points" or "Write in paragraphs". Examples: "Always show one example" or "No examples unless I ask".

Be concrete rather than vague. "Be helpful" says nothing, because every answer tries to be helpful. "Explain with one everyday example and no jargon" says something a model can actually follow. If you find yourself writing a wish, rewrite it as a rule: instead of "make it good", write "three short paragraphs, plain words, one example".

One rule of thumb: if you type the same instruction into three prompts in a row, it belongs in this box. That is the test for everything here. Custom instructions earn their place by removing repetition, so the lines that repeat are the lines to save.

6 ready examples

These six examples are ready to copy and adapt. Each one fills both boxes: who you are, and how you like answers. Change the details to match your own life — the pattern is what matters, not the exact words.

  • Student: About me: I am a first-year university student studying biology. I am comfortable with science words but new to lab reports. Answers: explain step by step, keep words simple, give one example for each new idea, and end with three quick questions I can test myself with.
  • Teacher: About me: I teach English to teenagers. I plan lessons and mark short essays. Answers: give ideas I can use in a 45-minute lesson, keep activities practical, and suggest one homework task at the end.
  • Small shop owner: About me: I run a small bakery with three staff. I write signs, replies to customers, and social posts. Answers: short and friendly, plain words, ready to copy, and keep each reply under 80 words.
  • Job seeker: About me: I am applying for office jobs and I am preparing for interviews. Answers: give short example answers I can practise aloud, point out weak words, and keep advice practical.
  • Parent: About me: I am a parent of two children aged six and nine. I help with homework and plan weekend activities. Answers: explain simply, suggest one fun activity per answer, and keep everything safe and age-appropriate.
  • Writer: About me: I write short stories in the evenings. I like plain, direct prose. Answers: give honest feedback on my drafts, suggest one stronger word where a weak one appears, and never rewrite a whole paragraph without being asked.

Notice what the examples share: each one is short, specific, and written as rules, not wishes. Borrow the pattern, swap in your own details, and test the result on a real question before you settle on it.

What NOT to put in

Custom instructions are stored with your account, and they are sent with your chats as context. That makes them the wrong place for anything private. Never put in passwords, addresses, phone numbers, bank details, identity numbers, or anything that could identify you to a stranger. If a line would worry you on a public form, leave it out.

The same rule covers other people's private details. Do not write about your children by name, your customers' details, or anyone else's personal information. Describe roles, not people: "I teach teenagers" is fine; a list of names is not.

If you are unsure whether a line belongs, ask whether it changes the answers. Your job changes them, so it belongs. Your street address does not change them, so it does not belong. The test is simple, and it keeps the notes both useful and safe.

How to test whether they are working

After you save your instructions, test them with a question you already know the answer to. Ask something ordinary — a summary, an explanation, a short plan — and check whether the reply follows your rules: the length you asked for, the tone you set, the format you chose. If it does, the instructions are working.

Test with two or three different questions, not one. A single reply can follow your rules by chance; three in a row is evidence. If the replies ignore a rule, rewrite the rule to be more specific — "short" is vague, "under 100 words" is a rule — and test again.

Remember that instructions shape answers; they do not control them perfectly. ChatGPT may still give a long answer to a question that needs one, or skip a format that does not fit. That is normal. The instructions are a strong nudge, not a lock, and a clear prompt in the moment still beats a stored note.

When to turn them off or change them

Instructions are for your usual work, and usual work changes. When you start a different project — a new course, a new job, a holiday plan — the old notes can get in the way, shaping answers for a life you are not living this week. That is the time to edit them, or to switch them off for a while.

You can usually turn custom instructions off for new chats without deleting them, which is the gentlest option: the notes wait for you, and you switch them back on when the old routine returns. Some versions also let you switch them off for a single chat, so one conversation runs without them while the rest keep them.

Review the notes every few months, the way you would review any standing order. Delete lines that no longer apply, add lines for the instructions you keep retyping, and check that nothing private has crept in. Five minutes of review keeps the notes honest.

Custom instructions vs memory — what each one does

Custom instructions and memory sound similar, and people mix them up. The difference is who writes the note. Custom instructions are written by you, once, in your own words, and they apply to new chats as standing rules. Memory is built by ChatGPT itself over time, from things it learns about you across conversations.

Use custom instructions for the rules you can state clearly: how long answers should be, what tone to use, what format to follow. Use memory, where it is available, for the background that accumulates — the project you keep returning to, the preferences that emerge over weeks. They work together: instructions set the frame, memory fills in the background.

If you want the full picture of how memory works — what it remembers, how to check it, and how to clear it — the memory guide covers it in detail. The link is at the end of this page. The short version is enough here: instructions are your written rules, memory is what the model picks up, and you control both.

Questions people ask

What are custom instructions?

Custom instructions are two short notes you write once in ChatGPT's settings: one about who you are, and one about how you like answers. ChatGPT follows them in every new chat, so you stop typing the same lines, like 'keep it short', into every prompt.

Where do I set them?

Open the settings menu from your profile icon and look for the custom instructions or personalisation entry. The exact labels can differ between the website and the apps, and menus change over time, so the in-app help is the authority if the page has moved.

What should I write in them?

In the first box, write your role, your level, and your language. In the second, write rules for answers: length, tone, format, and whether you want examples. Keep both boxes short and specific, and never put in passwords, addresses, bank details, or anything private.

Will they change all my chats?

They apply to new chats started after you save them. Older chats are generally not rewritten, so a conversation you started before setting instructions keeps its own flow. If you want the instructions in an old chat, start a fresh chat on the same topic.

Can I turn them off for one chat?

In most versions you can switch custom instructions off for new chats without deleting them, and some versions let you switch them off for a single chat. Check the settings page or the in-app help for the current option, because the controls differ between the website and the apps.

Are custom instructions private?

They are stored with your account and used as context for your chats, but they are not a safe place for private details. Never put in passwords, addresses, bank details, or other people's personal information, and review the notes now and then to check nothing private has crept in.
