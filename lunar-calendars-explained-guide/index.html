Lunar Calendars Explained

A lunar calendar counts months by the Moon, so its year is about eleven days shorter than a solar year. This guide explains how lunar months work, why festival dates move earlier each Gregorian year, and how lunisolar calendars differ.

How a lunar month works

A lunar month runs from one new moon to the next. The Moon takes about twenty-nine and a half days to go through its full cycle, from new to full and back to new, so a lunar month is about twenty-nine and a half days long. Since a calendar month must have a whole number of days, lunar months alternate between twenty-nine and thirty days, which averages out to about twenty-nine and a half.

The new moon is the natural starting point of a lunar month, and in many traditions the month begins when the new crescent moon is first seen in the evening sky. In other traditions the start is worked out by calculation. Either way, the Moon sets the rhythm, and the months follow the Moon rather than the Sun.

Why the lunar year is shorter

A lunar year has twelve lunar months. Twelve months of about twenty-nine and a half days come to about three hundred and fifty-four days, which is about eleven days shorter than a solar year of about three hundred and sixty-five days. That eleven-day gap is the whole reason lunar festival dates move.

Think of it this way: a lunar year finishes eleven days before the solar year does, so the next lunar year starts eleven days earlier in the solar calendar. Each year the start moves about eleven days earlier, and after about three years the date has moved about a month earlier. Why festival dates move

Because the lunar year is shorter, a festival fixed to a lunar date falls about eleven days earlier in the Gregorian calendar each year. Over time it moves through all four seasons: a festival that falls in summer this year will fall in spring in a few years, then winter, then autumn, and eventually back to summer again. The full cycle takes about thirty-three years.

This is why the dates of Islamic festivals, which follow a lunar calendar, are different every Gregorian year. The festivals themselves stay fixed in their own calendar; it is the Gregorian calendar that they move against. The same is true of any festival tied to a lunar month.

Lunar, lunisolar and solar calendars

A lunar calendar follows the Moon only. Its months track the Moon's cycle, and its year is about three hundred and fifty-four days. The Islamic calendar is a lunar calendar, which is why its festivals move through the seasons.

A lunisolar calendar follows the Moon but also keeps an eye on the Sun. Its months track the Moon, and every few years an extra month is added to pull the calendar back towards the seasons. The Hindu calendar is lunisolar, which is why its festivals move within a narrower range instead of drifting through the whole year.

A solar calendar follows the Sun only. Its year matches the seasons, and its months are fixed lengths that no longer track the Moon. The Gregorian calendar is a solar calendar, which is why its dates stay in the same season every year.

How lunar months are fixed in practice

In practice, the start of a lunar month is fixed in one of two ways. Some traditions rely on sighting the new crescent moon in the evening sky, which means the start can depend on the weather and on where you are. Others use calculation, working out the new moon from astronomy. Both approaches aim at the same thing: a month that begins with the new moon.

Because sighting can vary by place, the start of a lunar month can differ by a day between countries or communities. That is normal for lunar calendars, and it is why festival announcements come from local mosques and community authorities rather than from a single global date.

Why the Moon sets the pace

The Moon is the most visible clock in the sky. Anyone can watch it grow from a thin crescent to a full disc and shrink back again, and the cycle takes about a month. Long before printed calendars, people used the Moon to mark time, plan gatherings and count the months. The words for month and Moon share a common root in many languages, which is a reminder of how closely the two were once linked.

A lunar calendar keeps that link alive. Its months are tied to something you can see in the sky, which makes the calendar easy to follow without any tools. The cost is the drift: because the lunar year is shorter than the solar year, lunar dates move through the seasons. Lunisolar calendars pay a different cost, adding a whole extra month every few years to stay near the seasons. Every calendar is a trade-off between the Moon, the Sun and the need for a simple count of days.

A calendar that follows the Moon for its months but adds an extra month every few years to stay near the seasons. The Hindu calendar is lunisolar.

How far does a date move in three years?

About thirty-three days earlier in the Gregorian calendar, since the date moves about eleven days earlier each year.
