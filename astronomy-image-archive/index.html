Astronomy Image Archive

The most famous space images, from the Pillars of Creation to the deepest views of the early Universe, were taken by the Hubble and Webb space telescopes and by spacecraft that visited other worlds. This guide explains what the best-known images show, how they are made, and where to find them in the official public archives.

How to use this guide

This guide is about pictures, but it holds no pictures of its own. That is deliberate: the images it describes belong in the official archives run by the space agencies, where each one carries its true date, its true colours and a caption written by the people who made it. This guide tells you what the most famous images show, why they matter, and where to find them, so that when you open the archive you know what you are looking at.

Read the sections in order if the subject is new to you. The first part names the famous images and explains each one in plain words. The middle part explains how the images are made, which is the key to understanding why they look the way they do. The last part tells you where the official archives are, how to use the images fairly, and which mistakes to avoid.

Keep one question in mind for every image you meet: what kind of light did the telescope record? A telescope that records visible light gives a picture close to what your eyes would see. A telescope that records infrared light, the heat glow your eyes cannot see, gives data that must be translated into colours before you can look at it. Most of the famous images you know are translations of one kind or another, and the caption always says which.

The most famous space images

The Pillars of Creation is the most reproduced space image ever made. Taken by the Hubble Space Telescope in 1995, it shows tall towers of gas and dust in the Eagle Nebula, about 6,500 light-years from Earth, where new stars are being born inside the dark clouds. The pillars look solid, but they are clouds of gas, and the small bright points at their edges are young stars pushing the gas away with their light. The Webb telescope photographed the same pillars again in 2022, in infrared light, and the new view shows far more stars shining through the dust.

Earthrise is the most important photograph ever taken of our own planet. In December 1968, the crew of Apollo 8 became the first people to travel to the Moon, and as their spacecraft came around the far side, the astronaut William Anders photographed Earth rising above the grey, empty surface of the Moon. The picture shows a small blue and white world floating in blackness, with no borders and no sign of the people on it. It changed how many people thought about Earth, and it is still the image that best explains why protecting our planet matters.

The Pale Blue Dot is the most humbling picture ever taken. In February 1990, the Voyager 1 spacecraft, then about 6 billion kilometres from Earth, turned its camera back toward home and took one last photograph. Earth appears as a single pale blue dot, less than a pixel wide, caught in a band of sunlight. The astronomer Carl Sagan, who had asked for the picture, wrote that everyone who ever lived lived out their lives on that tiny dot, and the image has been used ever since to teach humility and perspective.

The Hubble Deep Field, taken in 1995, showed the Universe in a new way. Hubble stared at one tiny, apparently empty patch of sky for about ten days, collecting light too faint for any shorter look to catch. The result held about 3,000 galaxies, each one a collection of billions of stars, in a patch of sky no bigger than a grain of sand held at arm's length. It was the first clear proof of how crowded the Universe is, and every deep image since, including the Webb telescope's first deep field of the galaxy cluster SMACS 0723 in July 2022, follows the same idea.

Other famous images each taught something new. The Cosmic Cliffs of the Carina Nebula, from Webb in 2022, show towering edges of gas and dust where stars are forming, with hundreds of newborn stars visible. The rings of Saturn, photographed in detail by the Cassini spacecraft during its thirteen years at the planet, show a structure finer and more beautiful than any drawing. The Great Red Spot of Jupiter, a storm wider than Earth that has raged for centuries, appears in images from every spacecraft that has passed the giant planet.

How the images are made

A space telescope does not take a photograph the way a phone does. Its camera records light as numbers, counting how many particles of light arrive from each part of the sky, and those numbers are sent back to Earth as data. Scientists then turn the data into a picture, choosing how to show it so that the information is clear. Every famous space image has been processed in this way, and understanding the processing is the key to reading the picture honestly.

The most important step is colour. A telescope that records visible light can make a picture in natural colour, close to what your eyes would see if you were there. But many telescopes record light your eyes cannot see: infrared light from warm dust, ultraviolet light from hot young stars, or X-rays from the most violent places in the Universe. To show that data, scientists assign a colour to each wavelength, often blue for the shortest and red for the longest. The result is a true map of the data, but it is not what your eyes would see.

The second step is exposure time. A camera on Earth takes a picture in a fraction of a second, but a space telescope may stare at one place for hours or days, adding up the faint light until the dimmest objects appear. The Hubble Deep Field took about ten days of total exposure. Without that patience, the thousands of galaxies in the picture would be invisible, and the patch of sky would look empty.

The third step is combining images. Many famous pictures are made from several exposures taken through different filters, each one recording a different wavelength. The exposures are layered together, and the colours are chosen to show the structure clearly: hot gas in one colour, cool dust in another. The colours are real information, but they are a choice, and a different choice would give a different-looking picture of the same place.

The telescopes behind the images

The Hubble Space Telescope, launched in 1990, made the first generation of famous space images. It orbits above Earth's atmosphere, so its pictures are sharper than any ground telescope could take at the time, and it sees in visible and ultraviolet light and in some infrared. Hubble's pictures of the Pillars of Creation, the deep fields and the planets set the standard for what a space image could be, and the telescope is still working today, more than three decades after launch.

The James Webb Space Telescope, launched in December 2021, is the second generation. It is much larger than Hubble, and it sees in infrared light, which lets it look through dust clouds to the stars forming inside, and look back to the earliest galaxies, whose light has been stretched into infrared by the expansion of the Universe. Webb's first pictures, released in July 2022, included the deepest view of the Universe ever taken and a new look at the Pillars of Creation, and its images now appear beside Hubble's in every archive.

Other spacecraft made the famous pictures of the planets and of Earth itself. The Voyager spacecraft, launched in 1977, photographed Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune on their way out of the Solar System, and Voyager 1 took the Pale Blue Dot. The Cassini spacecraft spent thirteen years at Saturn and sent back the finest pictures of its rings ever made. The Apollo astronauts took Earthrise with a hand-held camera, which makes it the only famous space image taken by a person standing in a spacecraft.

Ground telescopes also make famous images, and they deserve a mention here. The largest telescopes on Earth, on high mountains in dry places, can now take pictures nearly as sharp as Hubble's, and they made the first image of a black hole, released in 2019, by combining telescopes across the world into one planet-sized instrument. But the images this guide describes are mostly from space, because space telescopes see the wavelengths that Earth's air blocks.

Finding the official archives

Every famous space image lives in an official archive, and the archive is the only place that can promise you the real picture with the right date and the right caption. The archives are free, open to everyone, and easy to search. This guide names them in words; search for the name on the official agency site to reach them.

For Hubble images, the official archive is run by the Space Telescope Science Institute, which operates the telescope for NASA and ESA, and its image library is called the HubbleSite gallery. For Webb images, the official home is the Webb telescope site run by the same institute, which releases new pictures with full captions. For the wider NASA collection, including the Apollo photographs and the Voyager pictures, the official NASA image library holds the public archive.

For ESA images, the official ESA image pages hold the European collection, including pictures from ESA missions and from the telescopes ESA shares with NASA. Each archive lets you search by object, by telescope or by date, and each picture page gives the caption, the credit, the date and the details of how the image was made. Download the large version if you want to print or study the picture, and keep the credit line with it.

Use the caption, not the headline, as your source of facts. The caption tells you which telescope took the picture, which wavelengths it recorded, how long the exposure lasted, and what the colours mean. A picture shared on social media often loses the caption, and with it the facts. If you cannot find the caption, go back to the official archive and search for the picture by name.

Using space images

Most images from NASA are free to use, because NASA is a United States government agency and its work is generally in the public domain. You can use a NASA image for teaching, for a school project, for a website or for a presentation without asking permission and without paying. The one firm rule is to keep the credit line: name NASA, and name the telescope or mission and any other institution the caption lists.

Images from ESA have their own terms, and they are usually free for non-commercial and educational use with credit, but you should read the licence note on the ESA page for the picture you want. Images made by a university or an observatory as part of a mission may carry an extra credit, and the caption will say so. When in doubt, the caption on the official archive page is the authority, and it always tells you what you may do with the picture.

There are two uses that are never acceptable. The first is removing the credit and presenting the image as your own work, which is dishonest and breaks the terms of every archive. The second is changing the picture, for example by recolouring it or adding objects, and then sharing it as if it were the real image. Cropping a picture to fit a page is fine, but changing what it shows is not, and a changed image should always be labelled as changed.

If you want to use a space image for a commercial product, such as a book cover or a poster for sale, check the licence note carefully first. Most NASA images allow it, but some images in the archives belong to other organisations, and the caption will say so. A few minutes of checking saves an awkward letter later.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is thinking a coloured image is what your eyes would see. A picture of a nebula in glowing red and blue is usually a translation of invisible wavelengths into colours, and the real view through a telescope would be far fainter and greyer. Read the caption: it always says which wavelengths were used and what the colours stand for.

The second mistake is trusting a fake image from social media. Space pictures are copied, reposted and altered constantly, and a striking picture can be a painting, a computer rendering, or a real image with the wrong caption. If a picture has no credit, no date and no telescope name, treat it as unverified. Search the official archive for the object it claims to show, and compare the real picture with the one you saw.

The third mistake is mixing up the telescopes. Hubble and Webb are different instruments that see different kinds of light, and a picture from one cannot be used to judge the other. The Pillars of Creation look different in Hubble's visible-light picture and in Webb's infrared picture because each telescope sees a different part of the scene, and both pictures are true.

The fourth mistake is forgetting the scale. A nebula that fills a picture may be tens of light-years across, and a galaxy that looks like a tiny smudge may hold hundreds of billions of stars. The caption usually gives the size, and reading it changes the picture from a pretty pattern into something you can actually understand.

This page describes famous images in words and points to the official archives — it does not host an image collection itself. A coloured space image is often a translation of invisible light into colours your eyes can see, not a photograph of what you would see with your own eyes, so read the caption on the official archive page before you share any image as fact.

Questions people ask

What is the most famous space image?

The Pillars of Creation, taken by the Hubble Space Telescope in 1995, is the most reproduced space image ever made. It shows towers of gas and dust in the Eagle Nebula where new stars are forming. Earthrise, taken by the Apollo 8 crew in 1968, and the Pale Blue Dot, taken by Voyager 1 in 1990, are the other two pictures that most people know, and each one changed how people think about Earth.

Where can I find space images?

The official archives are the right place: the HubbleSite gallery for Hubble pictures, the Webb telescope site for Webb pictures, the NASA image library for the wider NASA collection, and the ESA image pages for European missions. Search for the archive name on the official agency site. Every picture page gives the caption, the credit, the date and the details of how the image was made.

Are Hubble images real?

Yes, they are real data from a real telescope, not paintings or computer art. Hubble records light as numbers, and scientists turn those numbers into a picture so you can see them. The colours often stand for different wavelengths, and the picture may combine several exposures, but the shapes, the stars and the galaxies in it are real objects recorded by the telescope.

Why are space images so colourful?

Telescopes record light your eyes cannot see, such as infrared light from warm dust and ultraviolet light from hot young stars. To show that data, scientists assign a colour to each wavelength, often blue for the shortest and red for the longest. Long exposures also reveal faint colour that a quick look would miss. The colours are real information, but they are a translation, not what your eyes would see.

What is the Pillars of Creation?

The Pillars of Creation are tall towers of gas and dust in the Eagle Nebula, about 6,500 light-years from Earth, where new stars are being born. The Hubble Space Telescope photographed them in 1995, and the picture became the most famous space image ever made. The pillars look solid, but they are clouds of gas, and the small bright points at their edges are young stars pushing the gas away with their light.

Can I use space images freely?

Most images from NASA are free to use, because NASA is a United States government agency and its work is generally in the public domain, as long as you keep the credit line. Images from ESA are usually free for educational and non-commercial use with credit, but you should read the licence note on the ESA page for the picture you want. Never remove a credit, and never change a picture and share it as if it were the real image.
