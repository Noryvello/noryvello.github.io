Ramadan Fasting Guide for Beginners

By the Noryvello team · Published 19 November 2026 · Updated 19 November 2026

Fasting in Ramadan means going without food and drink from dawn until sunset, as an act of worship and self-discipline. In 2027 Ramadan is expected to begin on Monday 8 February, depending on the sighting of the moon. This guide walks first-time fasters through a typical day, with practical tips for energy, sleep and prayer.

If this is your first Ramadan, the idea of a whole month without food or water during daylight hours can feel daunting. It helps to know that millions of people fast every year, including many who were nervous the first time, and that the body adjusts faster than most beginners expect. The first few days are usually the hardest; after that, a rhythm sets in, and many fasters say the month starts to feel natural.

This guide is written for someone fasting for the first time, or returning after a long break. It explains what fasting involves, what a day actually looks like from suhoor to iftar, and how to look after your energy, your sleep and your prayers. It is general information, not religious instruction: for questions about the rules of fasting, your local mosque or a scholar you trust is the best guide.

Fasting affects everyone differently. This page is general information only, not medical advice. If you have a health condition, take regular medication, are pregnant, or feel unwell while fasting, speak to a doctor before you begin. Islamic dates depend on the sighting of the moon, so the dates on this page are expected dates.

What fasting in Ramadan means

Fasting, known as sawm, is one of the Five Pillars of Islam, and it is observed during Ramadan, the ninth month of the Islamic calendar. From dawn until sunset, Muslims who are fasting do not eat or drink anything. The fast begins at dawn, with the Fajr prayer, and ends at sunset, with the Maghrib prayer and the evening meal called iftar.

The fast is about far more than going without food. It is a time for self-discipline, patience and reflection, and for feeling closer to God. Many fasters say the month teaches them gratitude for ordinary things — a glass of water, a quiet morning, a shared meal — and gives them a sense of what it is like to go without. It is also a deeply social month: families eat together, neighbours share food, and mosques fill in the evenings.

Before the fast begins each day, Muslims make an intention, called niyyah, to fast for the sake of God. The intention can be made the night before or before dawn, and it is a quiet, personal matter rather than a formal ceremony. If you are new to fasting, do not worry about getting every detail perfect on the first day; the intention and the effort are what matter most.

What a fasting day looks like

A fasting day has a shape of its own, and it helps to know it in advance. It starts before dawn with suhoor, the pre-dawn meal, followed by the Fajr prayer. Many people go back to sleep for a short while afterwards, or start their day early. The morning is often the easiest part of the fast, when energy is still high from suhoor.

The middle of the day is usually ordinary: work, school, errands and chores continue much as usual. The afternoon can feel longer, especially in the first week, when the body is still adjusting. As sunset approaches, the pace changes — food is prepared, the table is set, and families gather for iftar. After the Maghrib prayer and the evening meal, many people go to the mosque for Taraweeh, the special nightly prayer of Ramadan, or pray at home.

In 2027 Ramadan falls in February, when the days are relatively short in the northern hemisphere and longer in the southern hemisphere. Wherever you live, check your local prayer timetable for the exact dawn and sunset times, because they change a little every day through the month.

Suhoor: setting up the day well

Suhoor is the meal that carries you through the day, and it is worth waking up for. The best suhoor is simple and filling: something with slow-release energy, such as oats, wholemeal bread or rice, plus some protein, such as eggs, yoghurt or beans, and a good amount of water. A piece of fruit adds natural sweetness and extra fluid.

What you avoid matters too. Very salty foods can leave you thirsty by mid-morning, and very sweet foods can give a quick burst of energy followed by a slump. A lot of tea or coffee can also leave you feeling thirsty later, so water should do most of the work. Keep suhoor calm and unhurried if you can: set an alarm with enough time to eat properly rather than rushing in the last few minutes before dawn.

If you struggle to wake up, prepare as much as you can the night before — set out the food, fill a water bottle, and lay out anything you need. Some people find it easier to sleep earlier and treat suhoor as a peaceful part of the night rather than an interruption to it.

Getting through the afternoon

The afternoon is the part of the day most beginners worry about, and it is true that energy dips as the hours pass. The trick is to plan for it rather than fight it. Do your hardest work in the morning, when you are freshest, and keep the afternoon for lighter tasks: reading, tidying, gentle errands, or rest if your day allows it.

Headaches are common in the first few days, especially for people used to regular tea or coffee, and they usually fade as the body adjusts. Staying cool helps, as does avoiding heavy physical work in the hottest part of the day. If your day allows a short rest in the afternoon, even twenty minutes with your eyes closed can make the last stretch to iftar much easier.

It also helps to keep your mind occupied with something gentle. Many fasters use the afternoon for reading the Quran, listening to a talk, or doing quiet good deeds. The hours pass more kindly when they have a purpose, and the sense of waiting becomes part of the worship rather than just waiting.

Iftar: breaking the fast gently

Iftar is one of the happiest moments of the fasting day. It is traditional to break the fast with dates and water, following the example of the Prophet Muhammad, and then to pray Maghrib before the main meal. Dates give quick, gentle energy after a long day without food, and water rehydrates the body.

The main meal after prayer is a time for family and good food, but it is also where beginners most often go wrong: after hours without eating, it is tempting to eat a great deal at once, and that can leave you feeling heavy and uncomfortable. Eat slowly, take a sensible portion, and give your body a little time before deciding whether you want more. Soup, salad and a main dish are usually plenty.

Iftar is also a time for sharing. Inviting family, friends or neighbours to break the fast with you is a much-loved Ramadan tradition, and many mosques and community groups serve iftar meals for anyone who comes. If you are fasting alone, joining a community iftar can make the evening feel warm and social.

Energy, sleep and prayer across the month

Ramadan changes your sleep, and that is normal. With suhoor before dawn and Taraweeh in the evening, the night is shorter than usual, and many fasters find a new rhythm: an earlier bedtime, a short sleep after Fajr, and perhaps a rest in the afternoon. The key is to protect your sleep where you can rather than trying to keep exactly the same hours as the rest of the year.

The five daily prayers give the day its shape, and in Ramadan many people add extra prayer and reading. Taraweeh, the nightly prayer prayed after Isha, is the best known of these, and mosques often complete a recitation of the whole Quran across the month. You do not have to do everything: a small amount of prayer or reading done regularly is better than a grand plan that collapses in the first week.

The last ten nights of Ramadan hold a special place in the month, and many fasters give extra time to prayer and reflection then. If this is your first Ramadan, do not feel you must match what others do. Settle into the basics first — the fast, the prayers, the meals — and let the rest grow naturally.

Common mistakes first-time fasters make

The most common mistake is skipping suhoor. Sleeping through the pre-dawn meal feels tempting, especially when you are tired, but it makes the day much harder and the afternoon much longer. Even a small suhoor — a bowl of oats, some bread and water — is far better than none.

The second is overeating at iftar. A huge evening meal after a day of fasting can leave you sluggish and uncomfortable, and it can spoil your sleep. Eat moderately, drink steadily through the evening, and save the bigger meal for later in the night if you are still hungry.

The third is trying to do too much at once: a perfect fast, extra prayers, a full reading plan, and a busy social calendar, all in the first week. Ramadan is a month, not a day, and it rewards steady habits. Start simply, be kind to yourself when a day goes badly, and remember that every faster was a beginner once.

Questions people ask

When does Ramadan 2027 start?

Ramadan 2027 is expected to begin on Monday 8 February 2027. Islamic months begin with the sighting of the new moon, so the exact date is confirmed by moon sighting closer to the time.

What can you not do while fasting?

While fasting, Muslims do not eat or drink anything from dawn until sunset. The fast is also a time for good conduct, patience and prayer. If you are unsure about a specific rule, ask a scholar or your local mosque.

Can I drink water while fasting?

No. The Ramadan fast includes not drinking water during daylight hours, which is why drinking well between iftar and suhoor matters so much.

What should I do if I feel unwell while fasting?

Listen to your body. If you feel seriously unwell, dizzy or faint, break your fast and seek medical help if needed. Islam allows breaking the fast for genuine illness, and the missed days can be made up later. This is general information, not medical advice.

Do children have to fast?

No. Children are not required to fast until puberty. Many families introduce fasting gradually, with short or half-day fasts, so children can join in at their own pace.

How long is each day's fast?

It depends on where you live and the time of year, because the fast runs from dawn to sunset. In February 2027 the days are shorter in the northern hemisphere than in the summer months. Check your local prayer timetable for exact dawn and sunset times.
