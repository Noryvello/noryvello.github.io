Image-to-Video Prompts: Turn a Still Picture into a Moving Clip

By the Noryvello team · Published 20 November 2026 · Updated 20 November 2026

Image-to-video starts from a picture you already have — a photo or an AI image — and asks the generator to add motion to it, instead of describing everything from nothing. Because the picture is already fixed, the character, the style, and the setting stay consistent from clip to clip. This guide explains the prompt formula, the camera and motion words that work, and how to fix the usual flaws.

Text-to-video asks the generator to invent everything: the subject, the setting, the light, and the movement, all from a few sentences. Image-to-video is a smaller, kinder ask. You hand it a finished picture and describe only what should move. The generator keeps the picture and animates the parts you name, which is why image-to-video clips usually look steadier and more consistent than clips made from words alone.

That steadiness is the reason to learn it. A portrait that smiles, a product that turns slowly on a table, clouds drifting over a landscape you already love — these are all one picture plus one motion, and they are much easier to get right than a fully invented scene. This guide is a how-to for writing those motion prompts, not a comparison of the two methods: if you want the differences explained side by side, the comparison page in the links below covers that.

Buttons, menus, and settings inside image-to-video tools change over time, so the exact steps on your screen may look a little different from the ones described here. The in-app help or shortcut list is the authority, and you should check the tool's own site for current pricing.

What image-to-video is, in one paragraph

Image-to-video is a kind of AI video generation that starts from a still image instead of from words alone. You upload a photo or an AI-generated picture, write a short prompt describing the motion you want, and the tool produces a short clip in which that picture comes alive. The picture sets the subject, the style, and the setting, and your prompt sets the movement, the camera, and the mood.

Why start from an image

The biggest advantage of starting from an image is consistency. In text-to-video, every clip is a fresh invention, so a character can change face, clothes, or age between shots without warning. In image-to-video, the character is already drawn: the same face, the same clothes, the same room, in every frame, because every frame grows out of the same picture. That makes it the right choice whenever the subject matters more than the surprise.

It is also the right choice when you already have a picture you love. An old family photo, a product shot you took yourself, a landscape from a holiday, or an AI portrait that came out well — all of them can become short clips without being redrawn. You keep what is good about the picture and add only the motion, which is a much smaller risk than asking the generator to rebuild the whole scene from a description.

There is one more advantage: control. When the picture is fixed, your prompt only has to describe motion, so it can be short and specific. Short prompts fail less often than long ones, and when something does go wrong, it is easier to see which word caused it. You are steering with a small wheel instead of a large one, and small wheels are easier to hold steady.

The prompt formula: subject + motion + camera + mood

A good image-to-video prompt has four parts, in this order: the subject, the motion, the camera, and the mood. The subject is already in the picture, so you name it briefly to anchor the prompt — the woman in the portrait, the red teapot, the old stone bridge. Then comes the motion, which is the heart of the prompt: what moves, and how. Then the camera, which says how the view itself moves. Then the mood, which sets the feeling of the clip.

Here is the formula applied to a portrait: the woman in the portrait smiles gently and turns her head slightly to the side. The camera stays still. The mood is warm and calm. Every part is short, every part does one job, and nothing in the prompt fights the picture. The generator does not need to be told what the woman looks like — it can see her — so the words are spent on the smile and the turn, which are the only new information.

Keep the prompt to two or three sentences. A longer prompt tempts you to redescribe the picture, and redescribing it can confuse the generator into redrawing it. Trust the image to do its half of the work. Your job is the motion, the camera, and the mood, and three sentences are enough for all three.

Camera moves in words

The camera is the second thing your prompt controls, after the subject. The moves that work best in image-to-video are slow and simple, because the generator has to invent the parts of the scene the camera reveals, and slow moves give it less to invent at once.

  • Slow zoom in. The camera moves gently closer to the subject. Good for portraits and products, where the detail is the point.
  • Slow zoom out. The camera pulls back and shows more of the scene. Good for landscapes and wide shots, where the setting is the point.
  • Pan left or pan right. The camera turns slowly to one side. Good for showing a room, a street, or a view.
  • Orbit around. The camera moves in a slow circle around the subject. Good for products and statues, where seeing all sides matters.
  • Tilt up or tilt down. The camera looks slowly upward or downward. Good for tall subjects like trees, buildings, and waterfalls.
  • Camera still. The camera does not move at all, and only the subject moves. Often the best choice, because it gives the generator the least to invent.

Pick one camera move per clip, and say it plainly. Two moves in one prompt — zoom in while panning left — usually produce a wobbly compromise, because the generator cannot do both cleanly at once. If you want two moves, make two clips and join them with a cut.

Subject motion

Subject motion is what the thing in the picture does. The safest motions are small, slow, and natural: a person breathing, smiling, blinking, or turning their head; a pet lifting its head or flicking an ear; hair moving gently in the wind; water flowing; clouds drifting; a candle flame flickering. These motions have a simple shape the generator can follow, and they read as alive without asking for much.

Bigger motions are possible but riskier. A person walking across a room, waving, dancing, or turning right around asks the generator to invent new poses, new angles of the face, and new parts of the background, all at once. It can work, and when it works it looks wonderful, but it fails more often than a small motion does. If you want a big motion, generate several versions and keep the best one, and be ready to simplify if none of them holds together.

One useful habit is to name the motion and then name its speed. A slow smile is different from a quick grin, and gentle waves are different from rough ones. Words like slowly, gently, softly, and steadily tell the generator to keep the motion calm, and calm motion warps less than fast motion. When in doubt, ask for less speed than you think you want: a slow clip that holds together beats a fast clip that falls apart.

What to keep still

A good image-to-video prompt says what should not move, as well as what should. The background is the usual thing to hold still: if the trees behind a portrait start swaying and the wall starts rippling, the clip looks haunted rather than alive. A short phrase — the background stays still — is often the most valuable line in the prompt.

Faces and hands deserve the same protection. A face that is meant to smile should smile, but the rest of the face should stay as it is: the eyes should not drift, the nose should not change shape, and the ears should stay where they are. Hands are even more fragile, because the generator has to invent fingers it cannot see clearly. If the hands are not the subject of the clip, say so: the hands stay still.

The rule is simple: move one thing, and hold everything else steady. A portrait where only the smile moves looks alive. A portrait where the smile moves, the hair moves, the background moves, and the camera moves looks like the picture is melting. Restraint is not a limitation here; it is the technique.

Motion strength — too much vs too little

Many image-to-video tools have a motion strength setting, sometimes called motion amount or animation strength. It controls how far the generator lets things move from the original picture. Low strength keeps the clip close to the photo, with small gentle motion. High strength lets things move further, with bigger gestures and more change.

Too little motion is the common beginner problem: the clip looks like a still photo with a slight shiver, and the viewer cannot tell anything is happening. If your first clip feels dead, raise the strength a little, or add one more motion word to the prompt — a smile that grows, a head that turns further. The fix is usually a small increase, not a large one.

Too much motion is the common opposite problem: the face warps, the background ripples, and the subject drifts away from the original picture. If your clip falls apart, lower the strength, shorten the prompt, and hold more things still. The sweet spot is usually in the middle: enough motion to feel alive, little enough that the picture stays recognisable. Generate the same prompt at two or three strengths and compare them side by side; the right one is usually obvious at a glance.

8 ready prompt patterns

Each pattern below is a complete prompt you can copy, adapt, and use as a starting point. Replace the describing words with your own subject, and keep the structure: subject, motion, camera, mood.

  1. Portrait comes alive. The woman in the portrait smiles gently and turns her head slightly to the side. The camera stays still, the background stays still, and the mood is warm and calm.
  2. Product spins. The red teapot on the wooden table turns slowly so the handle comes into view. The camera orbits slowly around it, the background stays still, and the mood is bright and clean.
  3. Landscape clouds move. Clouds drift slowly across the sky above the green hills. The camera stays still, the hills stay still, and the mood is peaceful and open.
  4. Pet looks up. The brown dog lifts its head and looks toward the camera with a gentle expression. The camera stays still, the background stays still, and the mood is soft and friendly.
  5. Candle flame flickers. The candle flame flickers gently and the light moves softly across the table. The camera stays still, the background stays still, and the mood is quiet and warm.
  6. Water flows. The stream flows steadily over the smooth stones, with small ripples catching the light. The camera pans slowly to the right, the banks stay still, and the mood is calm and fresh.
  7. Old photo breathes. The young man in the old photograph blinks slowly and smiles a little. The camera stays still, the background stays still, and the mood is gentle and respectful.
  8. Flower opens. The pink flower opens slowly, petal by petal, in the morning light. The camera zooms in slowly, the leaves stay still, and the mood is soft and hopeful.

Notice what the patterns share: one motion, one camera move, and one thing held still. That shape is the whole technique in miniature, and it works for almost any picture you start from.

Fixing common flaws

Warping. The face or the subject slowly changes shape during the clip. The usual cause is too much motion, or a prompt that asks for too many things at once. Lower the motion strength, shorten the prompt to one motion, and add a stillness phrase for the face or the subject.

Melting faces. The features drift, blur, or merge into each other. This happens most with big motions and fast movement. Ask for a smaller motion — a gentle smile instead of a laugh, a slight turn instead of a full turn — and keep the camera still so the generator has less to invent.

Extra limbs. An extra hand, arm, or finger appears where it should not be. This is a sign that the generator is inventing body parts it cannot see. Hold the hands still in the prompt, keep the motion away from the edges of the picture, and regenerate: the flaw is random, so a fresh generation often fixes it.

Flicker. The light, the colours, or the details shiver from frame to frame. This happens most with busy backgrounds and high motion strength. Simplify the prompt, hold the background still, and lower the strength. If the flicker stays, try a shorter clip: the first few seconds are usually cleaner than the last few.

Questions people ask

What is image-to-video?

Image-to-video is AI video generation that starts from a still picture instead of from words alone. You upload a photo or an AI image, describe the motion you want, and the tool produces a short clip in which that picture comes alive.

How is it different from text-to-video?

Text-to-video invents the whole scene from a description, while image-to-video starts from a picture that already fixes the subject, style, and setting. Image-to-video clips are usually steadier and more consistent, because the generator only has to add motion, not invent the scene.

What motion words work best?

Small, slow, natural motions work best: smile gently, turn slightly, drift slowly, flow steadily, flicker softly. Words like slowly, gently, and steadily keep the motion calm, and calm motion warps less than fast motion.

How do I keep the face from warping?

Ask for one small motion, keep the camera still, and add a stillness phrase for the face and background. If the face still warps, lower the motion strength and shorten the prompt to a single motion.

Can I animate an old photo?

Yes, old photos animate well, because the picture is already fixed and the motion you add is small — a blink, a slight smile, a gentle turn of the head. Keep the motion gentle and the mood respectful, and use only photos you have the right to use.

How long should an image-to-video clip be?

Short clips work best, usually a few seconds each. The first few seconds are usually the cleanest, and longer clips give the generator more time to drift away from the original picture.
