Why there are 13 full moons in 2028

Most years hold twelve full moons, one per month, but 2028 holds thirteen. The reason is that a lunar cycle runs a little shorter than a calendar month. The full moons drift a little earlier each month, and over the course of a year that drift adds up to a whole extra full moon. December 2028 catches the extra one: the month opens with a full moon on 2 December and closes with another on 31 December.

Years with thirteen full moons come around every few years, and they always bring a Blue Moon with them, because the extra moon has to fall in a month that already has one. In 2028 that month is December, which is why the year ends with a Blue Moon on New Year's Eve.

The traditional names

Each full moon carries a traditional name drawn from long-standing folk naming, mostly from farming and hunting life. January's Wolf Moon, February's Snow Moon, March's Worm Moon, April's Pink Moon, May's Flower Moon, June's Strawberry Moon, July's Buck Moon and August's Sturgeon Moon all take their names from the month they fall in, and the names describe the season: snow on the ground, flowers opening, fruit ripening, deer growing antlers.

The autumn moons follow a different rule. The Harvest Moon is the full moon nearest the autumn equinox, which in 2028 falls on 22 September. The September full moon, on 3 September, sits nineteen days before the equinox, while the October full moon, on 3 October, sits eleven days after it, so the October moon is the nearer one and takes the Harvest name. The Hunter's Moon is the full moon after the Harvest Moon, so in 2028 it falls on 2 November.

That is why 2028 has no Beaver Moon. In most years the November full moon is the Beaver Moon, but this year the November moon takes the Hunter's name instead, because it follows the Harvest Moon. The naming system has only so many names to go round, and in a thirteen-moon year one of them drops out. December keeps its usual Cold Moon for the full moon of 2 December, and the second December moon takes the Blue Moon name.

Supermoons, micro moons and the Blue Moon

A supermoon is a full moon that falls near the Moon's closest approach to Earth, so it looks slightly larger and brighter than an ordinary full moon. The difference is real but modest: you would need to compare it side by side with another full moon to notice. In 2028 the supermoons fall on 10 February and 11 March, the first two full moons of the year.

A micro full moon is the opposite: a full moon near the Moon's farthest point from Earth, so it looks slightly smaller than usual. In 2028 the micro full moons fall on 3 September and 3 October. The terms supermoon and micro moon describe how close the Moon happens to be, not a different kind of moon: every full moon is the same Moon, lit the same way, at a different distance.

A Blue Moon, in the sense used here, is simply the second full moon in a single calendar month. The Moon does not turn blue: the name marks the rarity of the event. The Blue Moon of 2028 falls on Sunday 31 December, the second full moon of December, and it shares the night with the total lunar eclipse, which is covered by the eclipse guide in the links below.

Questions people ask

How many full moons are there in 2028?

Thirteen. Most years have twelve, but a lunar cycle runs a little shorter than a calendar month, so 2028 fits in an extra full moon, in December.

When is the Blue Moon in 2028?

Sunday 31 December 2028, the second full moon of December. It is also the night of the total lunar eclipse.

What is a supermoon?

A full moon that falls near the Moon's closest approach to Earth, so it looks slightly larger and brighter than usual. In 2028 the supermoons fall on 10 February and 11 March.

Why is there no Beaver Moon in 2028?

The full moon after the Harvest Moon takes the name Hunter's Moon, and in 2028 that moon falls in November, so the Beaver name is not used this year.

What is a Harvest Moon?

The full moon nearest the autumn equinox. In 2028 the equinox falls on 22 September, so the full moon of Tuesday 3 October is the Harvest Moon.

Why can my local date differ from the UTC date?

The full moon happens at one instant worldwide. Near midnight UTC that instant can fall on different calendar dates in different timezones, so your local date can be a day earlier or later.
