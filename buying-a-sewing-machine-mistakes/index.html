7 Mistakes to Avoid When Buying a Sewing Machine

The most common mistake when buying a sewing machine is paying for features you will never use, or buying a machine that cannot handle the fabrics you sew most. This guide covers the seven mistakes beginners make most often, with a picker for your projects and a first-machine checklist.

A sewing machine can last for years, which is exactly why the first purchase matters so much. Buy the right machine and you will reach for it happily; buy the wrong one and it will sit in a cupboard while you put off every repair and project. The difference usually comes down to matching the machine to your real sewing, not to the longest feature list.

Before you shop, think about what you will actually sew. Hems and repairs need very little. Dressmaking needs a few more options. Quilting and heavy fabrics need a machine built for the job. Once you know your projects, the choice gets much simpler.

This guide is general buying advice, not a product review. Machines vary between models and makers, so check the details of any machine before you buy.

The 7 mistakes to avoid

  1. Buying too many stitches. A long stitch list looks impressive, but most sewers use a straight stitch, a zigzag stitch and a buttonhole stitch for nearly everything they make. Dozens of decorative stitches sound exciting in the shop and then sit unused for years. Choose a machine for the stitches you will actually use, and spend the money you save on decent fabric and thread instead.
  2. Ignoring the fabrics you actually sew. A machine that glides through light cotton can struggle with thick denim, canvas or several layers of fabric at once. Think about the thickest thing you will sew, not the thinnest, and check that the machine can handle it. If you mostly sew heavy fabrics, you need a stronger machine with a motor that does not strain on thick seams.
  3. Skipping the test sew. A machine can look perfect and still feel wrong in use. If you can, try the machine before you buy it — ideally on a piece of your own fabric. Listen to how it sounds, watch how even the stitches are, and notice how easy it is to thread. A short test sew tells you more than any description, and it is the best way to spot a machine that fights you.
  4. Forgetting the feet and accessories. Presser feet change what a machine can do: a zipper foot, a buttonhole foot and a walking foot each open up new projects. Check which feet come in the box and which ones you would need to buy separately. Also check for bobbins, needles and a seam ripper, because starting a project only to find you are missing a basic part is frustrating.
  5. Choosing the wrong size and weight. A heavy machine is steady while you sew, but it is a chore to lift out of a cupboard and put away again — and a machine that is a chore to set up is a machine that stays in the cupboard. A very light machine, on the other hand, can wobble on bigger projects. Think about where the machine will live and whether you will carry it to classes or a friend's house.
  6. Overlooking the bobbin and threading. Threading a machine and winding a bobbin are things you will do every time you sew, so they should feel easy, not fiddly. Some machines drop the bobbin in from the top where you can see it, while others load from the front. If you are a beginner, an easy threading path and a clear bobbin area will save you a lot of early frustration.
  7. Not checking servicing and the manual. Even a good machine needs servicing now and then, and a jammed machine needs help. Before you buy, check where servicing is available and whether the manual is clear and complete. A good manual with plain diagrams is worth a great deal when you are learning, and a machine with no local servicing can turn a small problem into a long wait.

Before you buy

Once you know the type you need, take one last slow look. Check the box contents against the checklist above, and make sure you know what you still need to buy — thread, needles and spare bobbins are the usual extras. If you are buying online, read the returns policy carefully, because a machine that looks right on screen can feel wrong in your hands. And give yourself a first project to look forward to: a simple hem or a small pouch is a much better first sew than a complicated garment.

Questions people ask

Should a beginner buy a mechanical or a computerised sewing machine?

A simple mechanical machine suits most beginners because it is easy to learn and has fewer parts to go wrong. A computerised machine suits sewers who want more stitch options and automatic features, once they know which features they will actually use.

Which stitches does a beginner really need?

Most beginners use a straight stitch, a zigzag stitch and a buttonhole stitch for nearly everything. Extra decorative stitches are nice to have, but they should not decide your purchase.

Can a basic sewing machine sew denim?

Many basic machines can sew light denim with the right needle, but thick seams and heavy denim need a stronger machine. If you sew heavy fabrics often, check that the machine is built for them before you buy.

What should come in the box with a new sewing machine?

Most machines include a few presser feet, bobbins, needles, a seam ripper and a manual. Check the list before you buy, so you know what you still need to get.

Do I need lessons to use a sewing machine?

You can learn the basics from the manual and practice, and many beginners do. A short class or a patient friend can help you get started faster and avoid common threading and tension mistakes.
