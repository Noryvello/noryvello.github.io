Candle Making for Beginners Guide

Candle making is a rewarding craft, and your first batch needs only wax, a wick, a container, and a little patience. This guide starts with safety, then walks through choosing a wax, pouring your first candle, and fixing common problems.

Safety first: never leave melting wax unattended, never melt wax directly over a flame, and never pour water on a wax fire — put a lid on the pan and turn off the heat. Keep children and pets away from hot wax, and work in a clear, well-ventilated space.

Before you start: safety first

Candle making involves hot wax, and hot wax burns. Never leave melting wax unattended, and never melt wax directly over a flame — use a double boiler or a wax melter, with the wax in a pouring jug set in a pan of water. Keep a lid nearby: if wax ever catches fire, put the lid on the pan and turn off the heat. Never pour water on a wax fire.

Work in a clear, well-ventilated space, away from children and pets. Wear an apron and closed shoes, and keep a cloth nearby for drips. Hot wax on skin hurts, so pour slowly and keep your hands clear of the jug's spout. If wax spills on a surface, let it cool and harden before you scrape it off — it comes away cleanly once it is cold.

What you need for your first batch

Your first batch needs only a few things: wax, a wick, a container, a pouring jug, a thermometer, and something to stir with. Choose a simple container — a heat-safe jar or tin — and a wick sized for it. The wick size matters more than beginners expect: a wick that is too small will not melt the wax to the edges, and a wick that is too large will burn too fast and smoke. Your wax or wick supplier can suggest a size for your container.

Weigh your wax rather than guessing. The amount depends on the size of your container and the wax you use — the wax calculator on Noryvello can work out the amounts for you. If you want a scented candle, measure your fragrance oil in advance and add it at the temperature your wax maker suggests, stirring gently so it mixes evenly.

Pouring your first candle

  1. Prepare the container. Wash and dry it, warm it slightly, and fix the wick in the centre with a wick sticker or a drop of melted wax.
  2. Melt the wax slowly. Melt the wax in a pouring jug over a pan of water, stirring now and then, until it is fully liquid.
  3. Check the temperature. Use a thermometer and wait for the pouring temperature for your wax — the picker above gives a rough guide.
  4. Add fragrance if you are using it. Stir it in gently and evenly, away from the heat.
  5. Pour slowly. Pour the wax into the container in a slow, steady stream, keeping the wick centred. Leave a small gap at the top.
  6. Hold the wick straight. Use a chopstick or a wick holder across the top of the container to keep the wick centred while the wax sets.
  7. Cool slowly. Leave the candle in a flat, draught-free spot to cool and set. Do not move it, and do not put it in the fridge.
  8. Trim and test. Once the candle is fully set, trim the wick short and burn the candle for an hour or two to check the melt pool reaches the edges.

Common first-batch problems

A dip around the wick after cooling is the most common problem, and it is easy to fix: melt a little more wax and pour a thin top-up layer. Air bubbles usually come from pouring too fast or from a cold container — pour slowly and warm the container first. A candle that tunnels on its first burn usually has a wick that is too small, or it was not burned long enough the first time. Let the melt pool reach the edges on the first burn, and the candle will burn evenly after that.

Do not be discouraged if your first candle is not perfect. Candle making is a craft that improves with practice, and even an imperfect candle burns well. Keep notes on what you did — the wax, the wick, the temperatures — so you can repeat what worked and change what did not.

Questions people ask

What wax is best for beginners?

Soy wax is a popular beginner choice: it is easy to work with, widely available, and forgiving of small mistakes. Paraffin is the traditional choice and holds scent well. The wax picker above compares the main types so you can choose.

Do I need a thermometer for candle making?

Yes, a thermometer is one of the most useful tools for a beginner. Pouring at the right temperature affects how the candle sets and how it burns. The picker above gives rough pouring temperatures — always follow your wax maker's instructions.

Why did my candle sink in the middle?

A dip around the wick is common, especially with paraffin, which shrinks as it cools. Melt a little more wax and pour a thin top-up layer over the cooled candle, and it will set smooth.

How long should I burn a candle the first time?

Burn it long enough for the melt pool to reach the edges of the container — usually an hour or two for a jar candle. This sets the candle's memory, so it burns evenly afterwards instead of tunnelling down the middle.

Can I use any jar as a candle container?

Use a heat-safe jar or tin made for candles, or a sturdy glass jar with thick walls. Thin glass, cracked jars, and plastic containers are not safe — they can crack or melt. Always put a burning candle on a heat-proof surface.
