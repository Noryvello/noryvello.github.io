Contract Template Directory

This directory explains the five contract types small businesses use most, what each one must cover, and how to use a template safely. Every outline here is a starting point for your own agreement, not a finished legal document.

Templates and information only — this page is not legal advice and it does not promise any legal outcome. A template is a starting point; for a large deal, a dispute, or anything you do not understand, get advice from a qualified lawyer in your own country.

What a contract template is (and is not)

A contract template is a ready-made outline of an agreement, with the usual sections already in place and blank spaces for the details of your deal: the names of the parties, the work or goods involved, the price, and the dates. It saves you from starting with a blank page, and it reminds you of the points that people often forget — what happens if the work is late, who owns the finished work, and how either side can end the agreement.

A template is not a finished contract, and it is not legal advice. No template can know your country's laws, your industry's customs, or the details of your particular deal. The outlines in this directory are written in plain language so that anyone can understand what each section is for, and they are meant to be read, adapted, and — for anything important — checked by a lawyer before you sign. Used that way, a template is one of the most useful documents a small business can keep on file.

The five contract types in this directory cover the agreements that freelancers and small businesses reach for most often: a service agreement for work you do for a client, a freelance contract for project work, a rental agreement for a home or a workspace, a non-disclosure agreement for sharing private information, and a sales agreement for selling goods. Each section below explains what the contract is for, what it must cover, and gives a short outline you can follow.

Service agreement: what it must cover

A service agreement is the contract between someone who provides a service and someone who pays for it. A cleaner, a web designer, a bookkeeper, a gardener, and a consultant all work under service agreements, even when the work is small. The agreement protects both sides: the provider knows what is expected and when they will be paid, and the client knows what they will receive and what it will cost.

A good service agreement names the parties, describes the service in enough detail that a stranger could tell whether it was done, states the price and the payment schedule, and sets the start date and the end date or the notice period. It should also say who provides the tools and materials, how changes to the work are agreed, what happens if either side wants to end the agreement early, and who owns any work produced along the way. If the service involves entering a client's home or handling their private information, the agreement should cover that too.

Short template outline for a service agreement:

  1. Names and contact details of both parties, and the date of the agreement.
  2. A clear description of the service, including what is included and what is not.
  3. The price and how it is paid, for example a total fee of 1200 paid in two instalments of 600, with the dates each part is due.
  4. The start date, the end date or the notice period, and the working hours or days.
  5. Who provides tools, materials, and access, and who pays for extras.
  6. How changes to the service are requested and agreed, in writing.
  7. How either side can end the agreement, and what happens to work already done.
  8. Who owns the work produced, and any confidentiality promise.
  9. Signatures of both parties and the date of signing.

Freelance contract: what it must cover

A freelance contract is a service agreement written for project work: a designer hired for one website, a writer hired for ten articles, a photographer hired for one event. The difference is emphasis. Project work has a clear finish line, so the contract must describe the finished work precisely, set the number of revision rounds, and say when the work is considered complete and the final payment is due.

The most common freelance disputes are about scope and revisions. The client asks for one more change, then another, and the freelancer has already spent twice the agreed time. A good freelance contract prevents this by describing the deliverables in detail, stating how many revision rounds are included, and saying that extra work is charged separately and agreed in writing before it starts. It should also say who owns the work — usually the client owns it once it is paid for in full — and whether the freelancer may show it in their portfolio.

Short template outline for a freelance contract:

  1. Names of the freelancer and the client, and the date of the agreement.
  2. A description of the project and the exact deliverables, with sizes, formats, or quantities where they matter.
  3. The total fee and the payment schedule, for example 50 percent to start and 50 percent on delivery.
  4. The deadline for each deliverable and the date the project is complete.
  5. The number of revision rounds included, and how extra revisions are charged.
  6. Who owns the finished work, when ownership passes, and portfolio rights.
  7. What happens if the project is cancelled, and what is paid for work already done.
  8. Signatures of both parties and the date of signing.

Rental agreement: what it must cover

A rental agreement, sometimes called a lease, sets out the terms under which one person lets a home, a room, or a workspace to another. It is one of the most important contracts most people ever sign, because it governs where they live or work. A clear rental agreement prevents most landlord and tenant disputes, because both sides know the rules from the start.

Every rental agreement should name the landlord and the tenant, describe the property and what is included with it, state the rent and the day it is due, and say how long the agreement lasts and how it can be ended. It should also cover the deposit — how much it is, where it is held, and when it is returned — who pays for repairs, whether pets or smoking are allowed, and what the tenant must do before moving out. In many countries, the law sets rules about deposits, notice periods, and eviction that no private agreement can override, so always check your local rules.

Short template outline for a rental agreement:

  1. Names of the landlord and the tenant, and the address of the property.
  2. A description of the property and what is included, such as furniture or appliances.
  3. The monthly rent, the day it is due, and how it is paid.
  4. The deposit amount, where it is held, and the conditions for its return.
  5. The start date, the length of the agreement, and the notice period for ending it.
  6. Who is responsible for repairs, and how urgent repairs are reported.
  7. Rules about pets, smoking, guests, and subletting, stated plainly.
  8. What the tenant must do when moving out, and how the final inspection works.
  9. Signatures of both parties and the date of signing.

Non-disclosure agreement: what it must cover

A non-disclosure agreement, usually called an NDA, is a promise to keep certain information private. Businesses use NDAs when they share a new product idea with a manufacturer, when they discuss a partnership, or when they show a freelancer their client list. The agreement does not stop people from talking about general knowledge — it covers the specific confidential information that is named in it.

A useful NDA defines the confidential information clearly, says what the receiving party may and may not do with it, and sets a time limit. It should also say what is not covered — information that is already public, or that the receiver already knew — and what happens if the information is shared by accident or demanded by a court. An NDA that tries to cover everything forever is harder to enforce than a focused one that names the information and sets a fair time limit, so keep it specific and reasonable.

Short template outline for a non-disclosure agreement:

  1. Names of the party sharing the information and the party receiving it, and the date.
  2. A clear definition of the confidential information covered by the agreement.
  3. What the receiving party must do to keep the information private.
  4. What the receiving party may not do, such as copying it or sharing it with others.
  5. The exceptions: information that is already public or already known.
  6. How long the promise lasts, for example two years from the date of signing.
  7. What happens if the information must be shared because a court or a regulator asks for it.
  8. Signatures of both parties and the date of signing.

Sales agreement: what it must cover

A sales agreement records a sale of goods: a shop buying stock from a supplier, a craftsperson selling a batch of products to a retailer, or a business selling equipment to another business. Even a simple sale benefits from a written agreement, because it answers the questions that cause disputes — what exactly is being sold, in what condition, for how much, and when it will be delivered.

A good sales agreement describes the goods in enough detail to identify them, states the price and the payment terms, and sets the delivery date, the delivery place, and who pays for delivery. It should say when ownership of the goods passes to the buyer, what happens if the goods arrive damaged or different from the description, and whether there is a warranty or a right to return them. For larger sales, it should also cover what happens if either side fails to keep their part of the deal.

Short template outline for a sales agreement:

  1. Names of the seller and the buyer, and the date of the agreement.
  2. A description of the goods, including quantity, quality, and any serial numbers.
  3. The total price and the payment terms, for example full payment on delivery or two instalments.
  4. The delivery date, the delivery place, and who pays for delivery.
  5. When ownership of the goods passes from the seller to the buyer.
  6. What happens if the goods are damaged, incomplete, or different from the description.
  7. Any warranty or return period, stated plainly with its length.
  8. Signatures of both parties and the date of signing.

Clauses every contract should have

Whatever the type of contract, a handful of clauses appear in almost every good agreement, and it is worth knowing what each one does. The payment clause states the price, the currency in words, the payment dates, and what happens if payment is late. The term clause states when the agreement starts, when it ends, and how it can be renewed or ended early. The change clause says that changes must be agreed in writing — a spoken change is hard to prove later.

The liability clause limits what each side owes the other if something goes wrong. The dispute clause says how disagreements will be handled — by discussion first, then by mediation or by the courts, and in which country or region. The confidentiality clause protects private information shared during the work. And the whole-agreement clause says that the written contract is the full deal, so that earlier emails or promises do not change it. You do not need to memorise the legal words for these clauses, but you should check that your contract covers the ideas behind them.

Two habits make any contract stronger. The first is to write dates, amounts, and quantities as numbers and words together where they matter, so that a smudged figure cannot be misread. The second is to read the whole contract out loud before signing, slowly, and to ask about any sentence you cannot explain in your own words. If a clause confuses you now, it will confuse you later, when it matters more.

How to use a template safely

Start by choosing the template that matches your situation, not the one that looks longest. A freelance contract fits project work; a service agreement fits ongoing work; a sales agreement fits goods. Then fill in every blank with the real details of your deal, and delete any section that does not apply rather than leaving it blank. A blank section invites argument later, because neither side knows what was intended.

Next, read the finished draft as if you were the other side. Is the work described clearly enough that you could tell whether it was done? Are the payment dates fair and clear? Does the cancellation clause protect both sides? If you find a sentence you do not understand, rewrite it in plain words or ask a lawyer to explain it. A contract you understand is worth more than a contract that sounds impressive.

When both sides are happy, each side signs and dates the agreement, and each side keeps a copy — paper or digital, in a place where it can be found later. If the deal changes, write the change down, date it, and have both sides sign it, rather than relying on memory. And remember the limit of any template: it is a starting point written for general use. For a large amount of money, a long commitment, or a deal that crosses country borders, a short review by a qualified lawyer is money well spent.

Questions people ask

What is a contract template?

A contract template is a ready-made outline of an agreement with the usual sections already in place, such as the parties, the price, the dates, and the cancellation terms. You fill in the details of your own deal, adapt the wording, and sign it. It is a starting point, not a finished legal document, and it should be checked by a lawyer for anything important.

Do I need a lawyer to use a template?

For a small, simple deal between people who trust each other, a well-filled template is often enough. For a large amount of money, a long commitment, a deal across country borders, or anything you do not understand, get advice from a qualified lawyer in your own country before you sign. A short review is far cheaper than a dispute later.

What should a service agreement cover?

A service agreement should name both parties, describe the service in detail, state the price and payment schedule, and set the start date and the notice period. It should also say who provides tools and materials, how changes are agreed, how either side can end the agreement, and who owns any work produced.

What is an NDA?

An NDA, or non-disclosure agreement, is a written promise to keep certain information private. It names the confidential information, says what the receiving party may and may not do with it, lists the exceptions, and sets a time limit. It covers the specific information named in it, not general knowledge.

Is a signed template legally binding?

A signed agreement can be binding if both sides agreed freely, the terms are clear, and the deal is lawful, but the exact rules depend on your country's law. A template does not guarantee that a court will enforce every clause. If the outcome matters to you, have the agreement reviewed by a lawyer before you rely on it.

What if the other side breaks the agreement?

First, re-read the agreement to see what it says about the problem, and raise it with the other side in writing, calmly and with dates. Many disputes are settled by discussion. If that does not work, the agreement may point to mediation or to the courts. For anything serious, get legal advice before you act, and keep copies of every message and document.
