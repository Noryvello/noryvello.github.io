Flat-Pack Furniture Assembly Guide

Flat-pack furniture goes together well when you prepare first, follow the instructions in order, and never force a part into place. This guide gives you a pre-assembly checklist, a clear assembly method, and fixes for the mistakes that turn a simple build into a wobble.

Flat-pack furniture has a reputation for frustration, but most of the trouble comes from rushing the first ten minutes rather than from the furniture itself. The pieces are cut to fit together in one order, and the instructions are written for that order. When a build goes wrong, it is usually because a panel was fitted upside down, a fixing was tightened too early, or a step was skipped that only made sense three steps later.

The good news is that the fix is simple and it costs nothing: slow down at the start. Checking the parts, reading the whole instruction sheet before you pick up a tool, and setting up a proper workspace will save far more time than they take. This guide walks through that calm way of working, from opening the box to the final check of doors and drawers.

Tall and heavy flat-pack furniture can tip over. Build large pieces with a helper, never climb on finished furniture, and anchor tall bookcases and wardrobes to the wall as the instructions describe. Keep small fixings and plastic bags away from children.

Checking the parts list matters more than it looks. Finding a missing panel halfway through a build means undoing finished work, and finding a damaged panel at the end means living with a scratched door. A quick count at the start catches both problems while they are still easy to deal with.

Setting up your workspace

Build on a flat, clear floor rather than on a bed or a carpet with a deep pile. A hard floor keeps panels level and lets you see when a piece is square. Lay down a soft blanket, an old sheet or the opened box itself to protect both the floor and the furniture surfaces from scratches.

Good light makes a real difference. Many flat-pack panels look alike, and the difference between two similar panels may be only the position of a few holes. Working in daylight or under a bright lamp helps you spot those differences before a panel is fitted in the wrong place.

Keep the small hardware where you can see it. A muffin tray, a few small bowls or even the upturned box lid will do. Screws and dowels have a habit of rolling under furniture at the worst moment, and sorting them by type at the start means you never have to guess which screw a step needs.

The assembly method, step by step

  1. Read the step you are about to do, then glance at the next one. Knowing what comes next stops you fitting a panel that blocks the following step.
  2. Fit dowels and cam-lock pins into the panels first, before joining anything. Tap dowels in gently with the palm of your hand, never with a hammer.
  3. Join panels loosely. Push the pieces together by hand and leave the fixings slightly loose until the whole piece is together.
  4. Work on the floor for large panels. Building a wardrobe upright is much harder than building it flat and standing it up at the end.
  5. Check that the piece is square before you fit the back panel. Measure the diagonals or simply check that the corners meet cleanly and the frame does not rock.
  6. Fit the back panel. It looks unimportant, but it is what keeps the piece square and stops it racking sideways.
  7. Stand the piece up with your helper, lifting from the base rather than pulling on the top.
  8. Level the piece using the adjustable feet if it has them, and check that doors and drawers open and close cleanly.
  9. Now tighten every fixing, working from the centre outward. Tighten firmly, but stop as soon as a screw meets resistance.
  10. Fit shelves, handles and drawer fronts last, then anchor tall pieces to the wall as the instructions describe.

Common errors and how to fix them

A panel fitted backwards is the most common mistake, and the easiest to fix if you catch it early. The finished face usually has a smoother surface and no raw edges, while the hidden face may show plain board. If a panel looks wrong, take it off and turn it before you tighten anything. Forcing the next step to fit a wrong panel only spreads the problem.

Cam locks cause trouble when they are turned the wrong way. The arrow or line on a cam lock should point toward the edge where the pin enters. If the lock will not turn or the joint stays loose, turn the cam back, check the arrow, and try again. A cam lock that has been forced can crack, so stop at the first sign of resistance.

An overtightened screw in particle board strips the hole and leaves the fixing useless. If a screw spins without gripping, remove it, push a wooden dowel or a few toothpicks with a little wood glue into the hole, let it set, and then re-drive the screw. Prevention is simpler: stop turning as soon as the screw feels snug.

A wobbly finished piece usually means the frame was not square when the back panel went on, or the floor is uneven. Loosen the fixings, square the frame, re-fit the back panel, and tighten again. If the floor is the problem, adjust the feet until the piece stands steady.

Finishing touches

When the build is complete, walk around the piece and check every joint, door and drawer. Doors should close without rubbing, drawers should slide without catching, and shelves should sit level. Small adjustments to hinges and drawer runners are normal at this stage and are much easier to make now than after the piece is loaded.

Keep the spare fixings and the instruction sheet. Spare screws and dowels are worth keeping in a labelled bag, and the instructions carry the part numbers you would need if you ever had to order a replacement panel. Store them where you will find them, such as taped inside a drawer or in a folder with other household papers.

Finally, give the piece a gentle clean with a dry cloth to remove dust from the build. Avoid wet cloths on raw edges, and let any glue set fully before you load shelves or hang clothes. A careful finish is what makes a flat-pack build feel solid rather than temporary.

Questions people ask

Should I tighten all the screws as I go?

No. Leave fixings slightly loose until the piece is fully assembled and square, then tighten them all at the end. Tightening too early can pull panels out of line and make later holes hard to reach.

What do I do if a part is missing or damaged?

Stop and check the box and packaging once more, including the cardboard flaps where small bags hide. If the part is still missing, contact the seller with the part number from the instructions before you improvise with a substitute.

Can I assemble flat-pack furniture on my own?

Small pieces, yes. For anything tall, wide or heavy, build it with a helper. One person can hold panels steady while the other fits the fixings, and lifting a finished piece upright alone can strain both you and the joints.

Why does my finished piece wobble?

The usual causes are an uneven floor, fixings that were tightened before the piece was square, or a missing back panel. Check the floor with a level, loosen the fixings, square the piece, then tighten again.

Do I need to anchor tall furniture to the wall?

Yes, if the instructions say so. Tall bookcases, wardrobes and chests of drawers can tip forward, especially in homes with children. Use the wall strap or bracket supplied and follow the instructions for your wall type.
