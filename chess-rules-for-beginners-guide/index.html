Chess Rules for Beginners Guide

Chess is a game of two armies on a board of 64 squares, and the aim is to trap the other king so it cannot escape. Learn how the six pieces move first, and the rest of the rules fall into place.

Chess has been played for more than a thousand years, and its rules have barely changed in the last few centuries. Two players, sixteen pieces each, one board of 64 squares — and a game that never plays the same way twice. It looks complicated from the outside, but the basic rules can be learned in a single sitting.

This guide explains the board, how each of the six pieces moves, what check and checkmate mean, and the three special rules that confuse beginners most: castling, en passant and pawn promotion. This guide covers the standard rules of chess. Friendly games sometimes use house rules — agree on them before the first move.

The board and the aim

A chessboard has 64 squares in eight rows and eight columns, alternating light and dark. Set the board so each player has a light square on their right-hand corner — "light on the right" is the easy way to remember it. Each player starts with sixteen pieces: one king, one queen, two rooks, two bishops, two knights and eight pawns.

The pieces start on the two rows nearest each player: the back row holds the king, queen, rooks, bishops and knights, and the row in front holds the eight pawns. The queen starts on a square of her own colour — white queen on a light square, black queen on a dark square. White always moves first, and the players then take turns moving one piece at a time.

The aim is checkmate: trapping the other player's king so that it is under attack and has no way to escape. You win the moment your opponent's king is checkmated. The king is never actually captured — the game ends one move before that would happen.

How the pieces move

Each piece moves in its own way, and that is most of what you need to learn. The king moves one square in any direction. The queen moves any number of squares in any direction and is the most powerful piece. The rook moves any number of squares in a straight line, horizontally or vertically. The bishop moves any number of squares diagonally and always stays on the same colour. The knight moves in an L shape — two squares in one direction, then one to the side — and is the only piece that can jump over other pieces.

Pawns are the odd ones out. A pawn moves one square straight forward, or two squares on its very first move, but it captures differently from how it moves: it takes an enemy piece one square diagonally forward. A pawn can never move backwards, which makes every pawn move a small commitment — once it has advanced, it can never come home.

When a piece moves onto a square occupied by an enemy piece, the enemy piece is captured and removed from the board. You cannot capture your own pieces, and no piece except the knight can jump over another piece. Use the picker below to study each piece's movement in detail.

Check and checkmate

When a king is under attack, it is in check, and the player must get out of check on their next move. There are three ways to do it: move the king to a safe square, capture the attacking piece, or block the attack by putting a piece between the attacker and the king. A knight's attack cannot be blocked, because knights jump, so the only answers to a knight check are moving the king or capturing the knight.

You are never allowed to make a move that leaves your own king in check, and you are never allowed to move your king onto a square where it would be under attack. If a player is in check and has no legal move to get out of it, that is checkmate, and the game ends at once.

There is also stalemate: if a player is not in check but has no legal move at all, the game is a draw. Stalemate usually happens when one side has almost no pieces left and its king is cornered but not attacked. A game can also end in a draw by agreement, by repetition, or when neither side has enough pieces left to checkmate.

Castling

Castling is a special move that lets you tuck your king to safety and bring a rook into play at the same time. The king moves two squares towards a rook, and the rook jumps over the king to the square next to it. You can castle on the king's side (kingside) or the queen's side (queenside), and it counts as a single move.

Castling is only allowed if neither the king nor that rook has moved yet in the game, if all the squares between them are empty, and if the king is not in check, does not pass through a square under attack, and does not land on a square under attack. Most players castle early, because a king in the corner behind a wall of pawns is much safer than a king in the centre.

En passant

En passant is French for "in passing", and it is the rarest rule in chess. When a pawn moves two squares forward from its starting square and lands next to an enemy pawn, that enemy pawn may capture it as if it had only moved one square. The capturing pawn moves diagonally onto the square the enemy pawn skipped over, and the enemy pawn is removed.

The capture must be made on the very next move, or the chance is gone. En passant exists to stop pawns from slipping past each other with the two-square first move, and although it rarely comes up, it is worth knowing so it does not surprise you in a real game.

Pawn promotion

When a pawn reaches the far side of the board, it is promoted: the player swaps it for a queen, rook, bishop or knight of their own colour, and the new piece acts at once. Most players choose a queen, because it is the most powerful piece, but sometimes a knight is better — a knight can give check in a way a queen cannot, and that can win the game on the spot.

Promotion is one of the reasons pawns matter far more than beginners expect. A single pawn that reaches the far side becomes a whole new piece, which is why endgames often turn into races to push a pawn forward while stopping the opponent from doing the same.

Questions people ask

Which piece is the most powerful?

The queen is the most powerful piece, because she moves any number of squares in any direction, like a rook and a bishop combined. The king is the most important piece, because losing it ends the game.

Can a pawn move backwards?

No. A pawn only ever moves forward — one square at a time, or two squares on its first move. It captures one square diagonally forward, and it can never go back.

What is the difference between check and checkmate?

Check means the king is under attack and must get out of it. Checkmate means the king is under attack and has no legal way to escape, so the game ends at once.

When can I castle?

You can castle only if neither the king nor the rook has moved yet, the squares between them are empty, and the king is not in check and does not pass through or land on an attacked square.

What happens if neither player can win?

The game is a draw. Draws also happen by stalemate, by agreement, by repetition of the same position, or when neither side has enough pieces left to checkmate.
