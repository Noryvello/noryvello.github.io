Flood Risk Information Portal

Flood risk is the chance that water will reach your home in a given year, and this guide explains how that chance is worked out, where to check it officially, and how to prepare. A static page cannot check your address, so this page shows you where to look instead and teaches you how to read what you find. It gives you the ten things to do before flood season and the steps to take during a flood warning.

This guide is for homeowners, renters and anyone thinking of buying or renting a home near water. How flood risk is worked out

Flood risk is worked out from three things: how likely water is to reach a place, how deep it would get, and what would be damaged if it did. The first part comes from geography and weather records: how close the land is to a river, a coast or a low-lying area, how high it sits, how rain drains away, and how often heavy rain has fallen there in the past. The second part comes from modelling: experts run water through a model of the land to see where it would spread at different levels of rain. The third part is about what is there: homes, roads, schools and businesses.

The result is usually shown as a probability, most often as a chance in a given year. The best-known label is the 1-in-100 flood, which means a flood of that size has a 1 per cent chance of happening in any single year. It does not mean the flood happens once every hundred years, and it does not mean you are safe for ninety-nine years after one happens. A 1-in-100 flood can happen twice in one decade, or not at all for a century. The number describes a chance, not a timetable, and that misunderstanding is one of the most dangerous in all of flood preparation.

Risk maps are drawn by official bodies in each country, using the best records and models available, and they are updated as the records grow and the climate changes. A map is a guide, not a promise: it shows the best estimate of risk, and real floods sometimes reach places the map marks as safe, especially when rain is heavier than anything in the records. Treat a map as the starting point for your own preparation, not as the final word.

Checking your area officially

The only reliable way to check your flood risk is through the official flood map for your country. Search for your country's environment agency or flood authority together with the words flood map, and look for the official map service. Most countries publish one: you enter an address or a postcode and the map shows the risk band for that spot, often with separate layers for river flooding, coastal flooding and surface water flooding after heavy rain. Use the official map, not a property website or a random app, because only the official map uses the official data.

When you find your area on the map, note three things. First, the risk band: maps usually use words such as high, medium, low and very low, and each band has a definition in terms of yearly chance. Second, the type of flooding: river, coastal and surface water flooding behave differently, and a home can be safe from one and exposed to another. Third, the date of the map and the data behind it: a map based on old records may not reflect recent building, new defences or a changing climate, so check whether a newer version exists.

If your country has no public flood map, or if the map does not cover your area, ask your local authority or your water supplier what flood information exists for your street. Insurers also hold flood data, and a home insurance quote often reflects the risk the insurer sees, which can be a useful second opinion. Whatever source you use, remember what it cannot tell you: no map can say whether your home will flood next year. It can only tell you the chance, and the chance is what you prepare for.

What the levels mean

Risk bands sound simple, but they are easy to misread. A high risk band usually means a chance of flooding of around 3 per cent or more in any year, which is roughly a 1-in-30 chance. That sounds small until you multiply it by the years you will live in a home: over thirty years, a 3 per cent yearly chance adds up to a much larger chance of at least one flood. A medium band is usually around a 1 per cent yearly chance, the familiar 1-in-100. A low band is usually around a 0.1 per cent yearly chance, or 1-in-1000, and very low is below that.

The important point is that no band means no risk. A low risk home can still flood, and when it does, the damage is often worse because nobody prepared. The bands are a guide to how much preparation makes sense, not a reason to prepare nothing. A high risk home needs serious preparation: defences, a plan, and insurance checked every year. A low risk home needs the basics: knowing where the water would come from, keeping valuables off the ground floor, and knowing what to do in a warning.

Risk also changes over time. New building upstream can send more water your way. New flood defences can reduce the risk. A changing climate can bring heavier rain than the records show. Check your risk every few years, and check it again whenever you buy, rent or renovate a home. A risk band from ten years ago may not describe your home today, and the official map is updated for exactly that reason.

Preparing your home: 10 things to do

Preparation is a set of small jobs, none of which takes long, and all of which are easier before the water comes than during it. Work through this list before flood season, and tick each job off as you do it. The first three are about knowing: know your risk band from the official map, know where water would come from, and know the flood warning service for your area and how to receive its alerts.

The next four are about the house itself. Keep important papers and valuables upstairs or in waterproof containers, and photograph every room and every valuable item, because photographs are what an insurance claim needs. Know where your electricity, gas and water shut-offs are, and make sure everyone in the household knows too. Clear gutters and drains so rain can run away, and consider flood barriers or air-brick covers if your risk band is medium or high. Move anything you would hate to lose off the ground floor before the rainy season starts.

The last three are about the household. Make a flood kit: torches, batteries, a charged phone, drinking water, medicines, warm clothes and the phone numbers of your insurer, your utility companies and your local authority. Agree a meeting point and a plan for pets, children and anyone in the household who needs extra help. And check your insurance: read what it covers, what it excludes, and what it expects you to have done, because a claim can fail if the policy asked for something you never did.

During a flood warning

When a warning is issued, act on it at once. Warnings mean flooding is expected, not merely possible, and the time between a warning and the water arriving can be short. Move valuables upstairs, turn off electricity, gas and water at the mains if you can do so safely, and move your car to higher ground. Put the flood kit by the door, charge your phone, and tell someone outside the area where you are and what you plan to do.

If you are told to leave, leave. Do not wait to see how bad it gets, and do not try to drive through flood water: a small amount of moving water can knock a person over and can float a car, and the road under the water may have washed away. If you stay, keep away from the water, keep children and pets inside and upstairs, and listen to the official advice on the radio or your phone. Never walk through flood water if you can avoid it: it may hide open drains, sharp objects and sewage.

After the water goes down, the danger is not over. Do not turn the electricity back on until it has been checked, do not drink tap water until your supplier says it is safe, and photograph all damage before you clean up, because the photographs are what your insurance claim needs. Wear gloves and boots when cleaning, and throw away food that touched flood water. If anyone feels unwell after contact with flood water, seek medical advice promptly.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is thinking that because it never flooded here, it never will. Flood risk is a probability, not a history lesson, and a home that has been dry for fifty years can flood in a single storm that is heavier than anything in the records. The past is evidence, not a guarantee, and the official map is a better guide than memory.

The second mistake is having no photographs of valuables. After a flood, an insurance claim needs proof of what you owned and what it was worth, and memory is a poor substitute for photographs. Take pictures of every room, every valuable item and every receipt you can find, and keep the photographs somewhere safe: on your phone, in the cloud, or with a relative. Ten minutes of photography before a flood can save weeks of argument after one.

The third mistake is waiting to act until the water is at the door. Warnings are issued early precisely so people have time to move valuables, shut off utilities and leave safely. Every minute spent deciding is a minute the water does not give back. Act on the first warning, not the last, and remember that leaving early and finding the flood was smaller than expected is a far better outcome than leaving late.

The fourth mistake is driving or walking through flood water. It is the most dangerous mistake on this list, and it kills people every year. Moving water is far stronger than it looks, and still water can hide hazards you cannot see. Turn around, find another route, and wait for the water to go down. No journey is worth the risk, and no photograph is worth it either.

Questions people ask

How do I check my flood risk?

Use the official flood map for your country: search for your country's environment agency or flood authority together with the words flood map. Enter your address or postcode and note the risk band and the type of flooding shown. This page cannot check your address, so the official map is the place to look.

What does a 1-in-100 flood risk mean?

It means a flood of that size has a 1 per cent chance of happening in any single year. It does not mean the flood happens once every hundred years, and it does not mean you are safe for ninety-nine years after one happens. A 1-in-100 flood can happen twice in one decade or not at all for a century.

Is my home in a flood zone?

Only the official flood map for your country can answer that for your address. Look up your address on the map and note the risk band and the type of flooding. A home can be safe from river flooding and still exposed to surface water flooding after heavy rain, so check all the layers the map offers.

How do I prepare for a flood?

Know your risk band, sign up for flood warnings, photograph your valuables, keep papers upstairs, and make a flood kit with torches, water, medicines and phone numbers. Clear your gutters and drains before the rainy season, and check your insurance every year so you know what it covers.

What should I do during a flood warning?

Act at once: move valuables upstairs, turn off electricity, gas and water if you can do so safely, and move your car to higher ground. If you are told to leave, leave, and never drive or walk through flood water. Keep your phone charged and tell someone outside the area where you are.

Does home insurance cover flooding?

It depends on your policy and your country: some policies cover flooding as standard, some offer it as an extra, and some exclude it in high-risk areas. Read your policy, check the exclusions, and ask your insurer what it expects you to have done. Photographs of your belongings make any claim far easier.
