Screw vs Nail Guide

Screws and nails both hold things together, but they do it in different ways — and picking the wrong one means a joint that works loose, splits the wood, or cannot be taken apart again. This guide compares them in plain language and picks the better fixing for your job.

Which one for your job

This is general guidance, not a rule for every job. Always check the fixing maker's instructions for the screws or nails you buy, and get a professional for structural work — anything that holds up a building, a roof, or a heavy load overhead.

Screws vs nails at a glance

SpeedSlower — each one needs driving in with a screwdriver or drill.Fast — a few hammer blows and the job is done.
Taking things apartEasy to unscrew and redo without much damage.Hard to pull out cleanly; often bends or tears the wood.

Quick rules of thumb

  • If it needs to come apart again, use screws. A screw comes out cleanly and goes back in; a nail usually does not.
  • If the joint needs to flex, use nails. A frame that moves with the seasons is better held by nails, which bend rather than snap.
  • Outdoors, use screws that will not rust. Rain and damp attack plain steel fixings, so pick screws made for outdoor use and check the label before you buy.
  • Near the end of a board, drill a pilot hole first. It takes a minute and stops the wood splitting, whether you use a screw or a nail.

Questions people ask

When should I use a screw instead of a nail?

Use a screw when the joint needs to hold tight against pulling apart, or when you may need to take it apart again. Screws grip with their threads, so they resist being pulled out, and they can be unscrewed and redone. Nails are better when speed matters and the joint mostly faces sideways force.

Can I use nails for decking?

You can, but screws are the better choice for decking boards. Screws hold the boards down firmly and resist working loose as the wood swells and shrinks with the weather. Nails can slowly lift out over time, leaving raised heads.

Why do nails sometimes split wood?

A nail pushes wood fibres apart as it goes in, and near the end of a board there is little wood to hold them together, so the board can split. Drilling a small pilot hole first, or using a screw, lowers the risk. This is why flat-pack boards, which are thin, are made for screws.

Do I need a wall plug for a screw in a wall?

Yes, in most walls. A screw on its own has nothing to grip in plasterboard, brick or concrete, so a wall plug holds it in place. The plug matches the wall type: use a plasterboard plug for plasterboard and a masonry plug for brick or concrete. In solid wood, a screw needs no plug.
