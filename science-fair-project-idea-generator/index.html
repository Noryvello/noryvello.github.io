Turning an idea into a project

Start by writing your question as a guess. For example, if your question is which soil helps seeds sprout fastest, guess which one it will be and why. That guess is your hypothesis, and testing it is the heart of the project.

Change one thing at a time. If you test three soils, keep the seeds, the water and the light the same for all of them. Record what happens each day, even when nothing seems to change, because a careful record is what makes a project scientific.

Stay safe. Ask a teacher or an adult before you start, never taste or heat anything unless an adult says it is safe, and keep chemicals, hot water and sharp tools out of the project unless a teacher supervises.

Presenting your results

A good display tells the story of your project: the question, the guess, what you did, what happened and what you learned. Use photos, charts or drawings to show your results, and keep the words short.

Be honest about surprises. If the result was not what you expected, say so and explain what you would do differently next time. Judges and teachers value honest reporting more than a perfect result.

Questions people ask

How many project ideas are in the bank?

Thirty original ideas, covering plants, water, physics, chemistry, earth and food for both younger and older students.

What does each idea include?

Each idea gives you a question to test, a short method outline and a materials note, so you can see what the project needs before you start.

How do I pick the best idea?

Choose one you are curious about, that you can finish in the time you have, and that uses materials you can get easily. Curiosity matters more than complexity.

Do I need a hypothesis?

Most fairs expect one. Write a guess before you start, such as which seed will sprout fastest and why, then test it and report what actually happened.

What if my experiment does not work?

That is still a result. Write down what happened, think about why, and say what you would change next time. Fair judges value honest reporting.
