Satellite Launch Calendar

A satellite launch calendar is a list of planned rocket launches and the satellites they will carry into orbit. Launch dates move often, because rockets, weather and orbital mechanics all have to line up before a launch can happen. This guide explains how launch schedules work, why dates slip, and where to check the next launch through the official source.

How to use this guide

This guide explains launch schedules in plain words: what a launch calendar shows, why the dates on it change, and how to find out when the next launch really is. You do not need any background in rockets or physics to read it. Each section takes one idea, from the launch window to the launch site, and explains it with everyday examples.

What this page can do is teach you how schedules work, so that when you check the official source, you understand what you are looking at.

How launches are scheduled

A launch schedule starts with the satellite, not the rocket. The satellite's owner decides what orbit it needs, and that orbit fixes the direction the rocket must fly and the time it must leave the ground. A satellite heading for a low orbit over the poles needs a different launch time from one heading for a high orbit over the equator, and the two cannot swap times.

Once the orbit is known, the launch provider picks a date and a window, which is the period of time on that day when the rocket can launch and still reach its planned orbit. The provider then books the launch pad, the tracking stations and the range safety team, and coordinates with air traffic and sea traffic so that no plane or ship is under the flight path. All of that is arranged weeks or months ahead.

The schedule you see published is the result of all those arrangements, and it usually shows the date, the time, the rocket, the satellite and the launch site. Times are often given in UTC, which is the world clock that space schedules use, so you may need to convert to your own local time. Some calendars also show a backup date, which is the next day the launch could fly if the first attempt is delayed.

Why dates move

Weather is the most common reason a launch slips. Rockets cannot fly through lightning, high winds or thick cloud, and the rules are strict because a rocket is a tall metal tower full of fuel standing in an open field. A thunderstorm near the launch site, or even a strong wind high up in the atmosphere, is enough to stop a launch for the day.

Technical checks are the second reason. Before every launch, engineers run through a long list of tests on the rocket and the satellite, and if any reading is outside its expected range, the launch stops. That is not a failure: it is the system working as it should. A launch that is delayed by a careful check is far better than a launch that goes ahead with a doubt.

Other delays come from the range, the sea and the sky around the launch site. A ship that has wandered into the safety zone, a plane on the wrong course, or another launch booked close by can all push a launch back. Sometimes the satellite itself is not ready, or the customer asks for more time. Any one of these can move a launch by a day, a week or a month, which is why a date from last week should never be trusted.

Launch windows, explained simply

A launch window is the period of time when a rocket must leave the ground to reach its planned orbit. Think of it like catching a train: the orbit is the train, and it only passes the station at certain times. If the rocket launches inside the window, it meets the orbit. If it misses the window, it has to wait for the next one.

Windows can be very short or quite long, depending on the mission. A flight to the International Space Station has a window of only a few minutes, because the station is a small target moving fast, and the rocket must arrive at exactly the right place at the right time. A satellite heading for a high orbit over the equator may have a window of several hours, because the target is much more forgiving.

When a window closes without a launch, the team does not simply try again an hour later. The next window may be the next day, or several days away, depending on how the orbit lines up. That is why a launch delayed by weather in the morning often cannot fly in the afternoon: the window has gone, and the next one is tomorrow.

The launch sites of the world

Rockets launch from sites around the world, and each site has its own character. Cape Canaveral and the Kennedy Space Center in Florida are the busiest, launching eastward over the Atlantic Ocean. Vandenberg in California launches southward over the Pacific, which suits satellites heading for polar orbits. Baikonur in Kazakhstan has launched crews to space since the first human flight in 1961.

The location of a launch site matters because the Earth spins. A rocket launched eastward from near the equator gets a free push from the Earth's rotation, worth about 465 metres per second at the equator itself. That is why the European spaceport at Kourou in French Guiana, close to the equator, is so useful for satellites heading to high orbits over the equator.

Newer sites have joined the older ones. China launches from several inland and coastal sites, India from Satish Dhawan on its east coast, and Japan from Tanegashima island. Smaller launch sites and sea-based platforms are also appearing, built for small rockets carrying small satellites. Wherever a rocket launches from, the same rules apply: a clear path over sea or empty land, tracking stations along the route, and a team watching the weather.

Checking the next launch

The only reliable way to know the next launch is to check an official schedule close to the day. The launch provider publishes its own schedule, and the space agency that owns the satellite publishes mission updates. Those two sources agree with each other, and when a date moves, they move it first. Everything else on the internet copies from them, sometimes slowly.

When you read a launch schedule, look for four things: the date, the time with its time zone, the rocket and the satellite. If a page shows a date but no time, the launch is probably still being arranged, and the date may move. If a page shows a countdown, check where the countdown gets its data: a countdown fed by the official schedule is useful, while a countdown from a page that never updates is just a guess wearing a clock.

A useful habit is to check twice: once a few days ahead, to know the plan, and once on the day itself, to know whether the plan still holds. Launches are often confirmed or delayed only hours before the window opens, so the morning of launch day is when the schedule is most likely to change. A date you saw last week is a rumour, not a fact.

Watching a launch live

Most launches today are streamed live and free, usually by the launch provider or the space agency. The stream normally starts an hour or so before the window opens, with commentary explaining what is happening: the fuelling, the final checks, the weather report and the countdown. Watching the stream is the best way to know whether a launch is going ahead, because the commentary will say so.

A launch broadcast has a rhythm of its own. The early part is slow, with the rocket standing on the pad and the team working through checks. The last ten minutes are the busiest, with a series of go or no-go calls from each team. Then comes the moment of ignition, the rocket lifting off, and the first minutes of flight as it climbs through the sky and sheds its lower stages.

If you cannot watch live, the replay is usually available within hours, and the launch provider publishes a short summary of what happened. The most useful part of any launch coverage is not the pictures, impressive as they are, but the confirmation that the satellite reached its orbit, which comes some time after the rocket leaves the pad.

Common mistakes about launch dates

The most common mistake is trusting a date from months ago. Launch schedules are living documents, and a date published in January may have moved three times by March. If you read about a launch in an old article or an old video, check the official schedule before repeating the date to anyone, because the chances are high that it has changed.

The second mistake is trusting a countdown from an unofficial site. A countdown looks precise, which makes it feel reliable, but a countdown is only as good as the data behind it. If the site does not update when the official schedule moves, its countdown will cheerfully count down to a launch that is not happening, and you will be left watching an empty pad.

The third mistake is thinking that a delayed launch is a failed launch. Delays are a normal part of launching rockets, and most launches are delayed at least once. A team that stops a countdown because of a worrying sensor reading is doing its job well. The launches to worry about are the ones that go ahead with a known problem, and those are rare precisely because the rules are so strict.

Questions people ask

When is the next satellite launch?

There is no single answer that stays true for long, because launches happen several times a week around the world and dates change often. The only reliable way to know is to check the official schedule of the launch provider or the space agency close to the day. A date printed on a static page would be out of date within days, so this page teaches you how to check instead of giving you a date that will soon be wrong.

Why do launch dates change?

Launch dates change because of weather, technical checks, range safety and satellite readiness. Rockets cannot fly through lightning, high winds or thick cloud, and if any sensor reading is outside its expected range, the launch stops for checks. A ship in the safety zone or a satellite that is not ready can also push a launch back. Delays are a normal part of launching rockets, not a sign of failure.

Where can I check launch schedules?

Check the official schedule published by the launch provider or the space agency that owns the satellite. Those two sources agree with each other and update first when a date moves. Look for the date, the time with its time zone, the rocket and the satellite, and check again on the day itself, because launches are often confirmed or delayed only hours before the window opens.

What is a launch window?

A launch window is the period of time when a rocket must leave the ground to reach its planned orbit. It can be very short or several hours long, depending on the mission. A flight to the International Space Station has a window of only a few minutes, because the station is a small, fast-moving target. When a window closes without a launch, the team waits for the next one, which may be the next day or several days away.

How often do satellites launch?

Satellites launch several times a week around the world, and there are now well over 200 orbital launches a year. Most launches carry one or more satellites, and a single rocket can carry dozens of small satellites at once. The pace has grown fast in recent years, as more countries and companies build rockets and more satellites are needed for communications, weather, navigation and science.
