Knife Sharpening Guide

A dull knife is harder to control than a sharp one, and sharpening it at home is a skill anyone can learn. This guide shows three methods — whetstone, honing rod and pull-through sharpener — and the right angle for each kind of knife.

Sharpening makes a blade genuinely sharp, so work slowly and keep your fingers behind the edge at all times. Never test sharpness by running a finger along the blade. If a knife is badly chipped or you are unsure, a professional sharpening service is the safer choice.

Honing is not sharpening

The rod that comes with many knife sets is a honing rod, not a sharpener. With use, the fine edge of a blade folds over to one side, like a page curling. Honing pushes that edge back straight, so the knife cuts well again. It removes almost no metal, which is why you can hone often without wearing the blade down.

Sharpening is different. It grinds a thin layer of metal away to create a fresh edge. A knife needs sharpening when honing no longer helps: when it slides over a tomato skin instead of biting, or when it crushes herbs instead of cutting them. Most home cooks sharpen a few times a year and hone in between.

Knowing which one you need saves time and metal. If the knife still cuts but feels less keen than it was, hone it. If it will not cut cleanly even after honing, sharpen it. Neither tool replaces the other.

How to sharpen on a whetstone

A whetstone gives the best edge and the most control, and it is the method worth learning if you cook often. Use a stone with two sides, coarse and fine, and work slowly.

  1. Soak the stone in water until the bubbles stop, then set it on a damp cloth so it cannot slide.
  2. Hold the knife at the angle shown by the picker above, with the edge facing away from you. A handy guide: 15 degrees is roughly the angle of a matchbox standing on its edge.
  3. Starting at the heel of the blade, draw the knife across the stone towards you in a smooth arc, as if you were slicing a thin layer off the stone. Keep the angle steady.
  4. Repeat along the whole length of the blade, moving from heel to tip, then flip the knife and do the same number of strokes on the other side.
  5. Switch to the fine side of the stone and repeat with lighter pressure and fewer strokes.
  6. Wipe the blade clean, then test it on a sheet of paper. It should slice cleanly without tearing.

The two mistakes beginners make are pressing too hard and changing the angle mid-stroke. Light, steady pressure and a fixed angle matter far more than speed. Ten careful strokes beat fifty rushed ones.

How to use a honing rod

A honing rod is for maintenance between sharpenings. Use it often, with a light touch, and your knives will stay keen for much longer.

  1. Hold the rod upright with the tip resting on a folded cloth on the worktop, handle at the top.
  2. Place the heel of the blade near the top of the rod at the same angle you would sharpen at.
  3. Draw the blade down and across the rod in a smooth arc, ending near the tip of the blade.
  4. Repeat on the other side of the blade, alternating sides with each stroke.
  5. Use about six to ten light strokes per side, then wipe the blade clean.

Keep the strokes slow and controlled. The fast, flashy honing you see in films is a good way to cut yourself and a bad way to treat the edge. Slow and steady keeps the angle true.

How to use a pull-through sharpener

A pull-through sharpener is the simplest option. It has fixed slots set at a preset angle, so you do not need to judge the angle yourself. It removes more metal than a stone, so it is best for everyday knives rather than fine ones.

  1. Hold the sharpener firmly on the worktop with one hand.
  2. Place the heel of the blade in the coarse slot and pull the knife through towards you in one smooth motion.
  3. Repeat a few times, then move to the fine slot and repeat with lighter pressure.
  4. Wipe the blade clean and test it on paper.

Never use a pull-through sharpener on a serrated knife: the fixed slots grind the teeth flat. Serrated blades need a tapered ceramic rod worked through each tooth, or a professional.

Testing and keeping the edge

After sharpening, test the edge before you put the knife away. The paper test is the simplest: hold a sheet of paper by one edge and slice down through it. A sharp knife cuts cleanly; a dull one tears or catches. A tomato is another good test: a sharp knife glides through the skin under its own weight.

Keep the edge by cutting on wood or plastic boards, never glass or stone. Wash knives by hand and dry them straight away, and store them in a block, on a magnetic strip or in blade guards so the edges do not knock against each other. A knife that is looked after stays sharp far longer than one that is not, and sharpening becomes a small job instead of a rescue mission.

Questions people ask

How often should I sharpen a kitchen knife?

It depends on how much you cook. A home cook who uses a knife daily might sharpen a few times a year and hone it often in between. Sharpen when honing no longer brings the edge back.

What is the difference between honing and sharpening?

Honing straightens the edge of the blade without removing much metal, so it is quick maintenance. Sharpening removes a thin layer of metal to create a new edge, which is what a dull knife needs.

Can I sharpen a serrated bread knife?

Not on a flat whetstone. A serrated edge needs a tapered ceramic rod worked through each tooth, or a professional service. A pull-through sharpener will damage the serrations.

What angle should I use?

Most kitchen knives sharpen at 15 to 20 degrees per side. Finer blades like a santoku sit near 15 degrees, while heavy blades like a cleaver sit near 20 to 25 degrees. Use the picker on this page for your knife.

How do I know the knife is sharp?

Try the paper test: a sharp knife slices a sheet of paper cleanly without tearing. A sharp knife also glides through a tomato under its own weight, without pressure.
