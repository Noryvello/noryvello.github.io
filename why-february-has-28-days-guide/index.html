Why February Has 28 Days

February has 28 days because of decisions made in ancient Rome, where it began as the last month of the year and was given an even number of days when every other month got an odd number. Later calendar reforms kept February short and made it the home of the extra leap day.

Every other month has 30 or 31 days, and then there is February, sitting quietly at 28, with a bonus day once every four years. It looks like a mistake, or like somebody forgot to finish the job. It is neither. February is short because of how the Roman calendar grew up, and once the pattern was set, nobody ever saw a good reason to change it.

The short answer is tradition. The longer answer is a story that runs from the founding of Rome, through a king who added two new months, through Julius Caesar's great calendar reform, and on to the rules we still use today. February kept its short length at every step, and it also became the place where the extra leap day goes.

The Roman calendar before February existed

According to Roman tradition, the earliest Roman calendar had only ten months, and the year began in March. The months ran from March to December, and the cold stretch of winter that followed December simply had no months at all. It was a gap in the calendar, uncounted and unnamed, which tells you how much the early calendar was a farmer's and soldier's tool rather than a neat piece of arithmetic.

That ten-month year covered about 304 days, which is well short of a full trip around the Sun. The calendar drifted against the seasons, and festivals that were meant to fall in spring slowly slid into winter. Something had to give, and the fix came from the second king of Rome, Numa Pompilius, who is traditionally said to have ruled around 700 BC. He added two new months to cover the winter gap: January at the start of the year and February at the end of it.

February took its name from Februa, a Roman festival of purification held in that month. It was a time for cleansing, for rites for the dead, and for preparing for the new year that began in March. That sombre, end-of-year character mattered, because it helps explain the strange number of days February received.

Why February got the short straw

The Romans had a strong preference for odd numbers, which they considered lucky, and they disliked even numbers. When Numa's calendar was set out, the months were given odd lengths of 29 or 31 days. February, as the last month and the month of purification for the dead, was the one month that was given an even number: 28 days. It was the odd one out in every sense.

There is a neatness to this once you see it. The calendar needed a place to put the leftover days, and the month that closed the year was the natural spot. February's 28 days were not a punishment or an accident. They were the result of a calendar built around odd numbers, with one month left to take the even remainder.

February also became the month where the calendar-makers did their tidying. When the Roman year drifted out of line with the seasons, priests would insert an extra month, and they did it after February, at the end of the year. February was already the calendar's adjustment drawer long before the leap day came along.

The Julian reform and the leap day

By the time of Julius Caesar, the Roman calendar was in a mess. The extra months had been added irregularly, sometimes for political reasons, and the calendar had drifted months away from the seasons. In 45 BC, Caesar reformed the calendar on the advice of astronomers. The year became 365 days long, the months were given the lengths they still have today, and an extra day was added every fourth year to keep the calendar in step with the Sun.

That extra day went to February, because February was already the short month and the traditional place for adjustments. The Romans did not add a 29th of February as we think of it. They doubled the 24th of February, counting it twice. The doubled day was called the bissextile day, from the Latin for twice sixth, because the 24th of February was counted as the sixth day before the start of March. Our word bissextile, an old name for a leap year, comes from that doubled day.

Caesar's reform also fixed the start of the year on the 1st of January, which moved February from the end of the year to second place. But February kept its 28 days and its role as the leap month. The reform changed the calendar's shape without changing February's character.

The Gregorian refinement

Caesar's leap rule was close but not perfect. Adding a day every four years makes the average year slightly too long, and over centuries the calendar drifted again, by about ten days by the 1500s. In 1582, Pope Gregory XIII refined the rule: years divisible by 100 are leap years only if they are also divisible by 400. So 1600 and 2000 were leap years, but 1700, 1800 and 1900 were not.

That small change keeps the calendar in step with the seasons for thousands of years. It also means that February's length follows a simple three-step rule, which the checker above walks through: divisible by four, except centuries, except centuries divisible by 400.

Most of the world adopted the Gregorian calendar over the following centuries, and it is the calendar in general use today. February kept its 28 days through the whole change, and the leap day stayed where Caesar had put it.

Why February stays short today

There is no astronomical reason for February to have 28 days. The months are human inventions, and their lengths are the result of history, not nature. The seasons are set by the Earth's orbit and tilt, and any arrangement of month lengths that adds up to 365 days would work just as well.

February stays short because calendars are hard to change. Every timetable, every computer system, every birthday and anniversary depends on the months staying as they are. Moving a day from another month into February would fix nothing and break a great deal. The short month is a piece of living history, a reminder that the calendar we use every day was built by Romans more than two thousand years ago.

So February's 28 days are not a flaw in the system. They are the system's memory. The month began as the last, unlucky month of a ten-month year, and it has carried that history with it through every reform since. Once every four years it grows a day, and then it goes back to being the shortest month on the calendar.

The early history of the Roman calendar comes from later Roman writers and is part tradition rather than certain record, so the details of the ten-month year and Numa's reform are given here as the traditional account. The leap-year rules in this guide are the Gregorian rules in general use today.

Questions people ask

Why does February have 28 days instead of 30 or 31?

February was the last month of the early Roman year, and the Romans gave it an even 28 days while the other months got odd lengths of 29 or 31 days. Later reforms kept February short and made it the home of the leap day.

Which years are leap years?

A year is a leap year if it is divisible by 4, except that years divisible by 100 are not leap years unless they are also divisible by 400. So 2028 is a leap year, 1900 was not, and 2000 was.

Why does the extra day go in February?

February was already the short month and the traditional place where the Romans made calendar adjustments. Julius Caesar's reform in 45 BC put the extra day there, and it has stayed there ever since.

What does bissextile mean?

Bissextile is an old word for a leap year. It comes from the Roman practice of doubling the 24th of February, which was counted as the sixth day before March, so the doubled day was called bissextile, meaning twice sixth.

Did February always have 28 days?

In the early Roman calendar February had 28 days, and in some versions of the old calendar it had 29 days in certain years. The Julian reform of 45 BC fixed it at 28 days in ordinary years and 29 in leap years, which is the pattern we still use.
