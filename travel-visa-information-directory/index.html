Travel Visa Information Directory

A visa is permission to enter a country, and the rules are different for every nationality and every destination. This directory explains the main visa types in plain words and points you to the official source for each country, so you can check the real rules before you book.

What a visa is

A visa is official permission to enter a country, given by that country's government. It is usually a stamp or a sticker in your passport, or an electronic record linked to your passport number. A visa says that the country has looked at your reason for visiting and has agreed to let you in, usually for a limited time and for a limited purpose, such as a holiday, study or work.

A visa is not a ticket and it is not a guarantee. The final decision is made at the border, where an officer can still ask questions and can still refuse entry, even if you hold a visa. That is why the documents you carry and the answers you give at the border matter as much as the visa itself.

Not every trip needs a visa. Many countries let visitors from some nationalities enter without one, for a short stay. Others ask for a visa from almost everyone. The rule depends on three things: your nationality, the country you are visiting, and the reason for your visit. A holiday, a business meeting and a course of study can each have a different rule for the same two countries. That is why a friend's experience is never a safe guide: your friend may hold a different passport, or may have travelled for a different reason.

The main visa types

Visas come in several kinds, and the kind you need depends on why you are travelling. These are the four most common ones.

  • Tourist visa: for holidays, visiting family and friends, and sightseeing. It usually does not allow you to work or to study on a long course.
  • Work visa: for taking a job in another country. It usually needs an offer from an employer there, and the employer often has to take part in the application.
  • Student visa: for a course of study, such as a degree or a long language course. It usually needs proof that a school or university has accepted you.
  • Transit visa: for passing through a country on the way to somewhere else, for example when you change planes at an airport there. Some countries need one even if you never leave the airport, and some do not.

There are other kinds too, for business visits, medical treatment, and family reasons, and the names change from country to country. The important habit is to match the visa to the real reason for your trip. Travelling on the wrong kind of visa, for example working on a tourist visa, can lead to a fine, a cancelled visa, or a ban on future visits.

Visa-free, visa on arrival and e-visa

Visa-free travel means you can enter a country without applying for a visa in advance. You still need a valid passport, and you still go through border control, but there is no application to make before you travel. Visa-free stays are usually short, often counted in days or weeks, and they usually cover holidays and short family visits only.

A visa on arrival means you apply when you land. You fill in a form at the airport or the border, show your documents, and pay a fee if one is asked for. It sounds simple, and it often is, but it is not guaranteed: if your documents are not in order, or if the officer is not satisfied, you can be refused at the border, after a long flight. It also helps to know in advance whether your nationality can use this route at all, because it is not open to everyone.

An e-visa is a visa you apply for online before you travel. You fill in a form on an official website, upload documents, and get the decision by email or on the same website. An approved e-visa is usually linked to your passport number, so there is nothing to stick in the passport. Apply only on the official site: many copycat websites charge extra for the same form, or collect your details for no good reason.

A fourth word you may meet is an electronic travel authorisation. It is not quite a visa and not quite visa-free travel: you fill in a short online form before you fly, and the country checks you in advance. If it is needed for your trip and you skip it, the airline may not let you board. Treat it with the same care as a visa application.

Documents you usually need

Every application is different, but the same documents come up again and again. Having them ready before you start saves time and avoids a refused application over a missing paper.

  • A passport that is valid for the whole trip, with enough empty pages, and with enough time left on it. Many countries want six months or more left after your planned return, so check this early.
  • Passport photos in the size and style the country asks for. The rules about background colour, head size and glasses are strict, and a wrong photo can delay the whole application.
  • Proof of where you will stay, such as a hotel booking or an invitation letter from the person you are visiting.
  • Proof of your travel plans, such as a flight booking or a travel plan, and proof that you can pay for the trip, such as bank statements.
  • Proof of the reason for the visit, such as a letter from an employer for a work trip, or an acceptance letter from a school for a student visa.
  • Travel insurance, where the country asks for it, and a completed application form with honest answers.

Keep a copy of everything you send, on paper and on your phone. If a question is asked twice, answer it the same way both times: small differences between forms are a common cause of delays. And never pay anyone who promises a guaranteed visa. No agent can guarantee a decision, because the decision is made by the country's own officers.

The 4 checks before you book

Run these four checks in order, before you pay for a flight or a hotel. They take less than an hour, and they can save you the cost of a trip you cannot take.

  1. Check the rule for your passport: open the official visa site or the embassy page for the country you plan to visit, and read the rule for your nationality. Do not trust a blog, a video, or a friend's story over the official page.
  2. Check your passport: look at the expiry date and the empty pages. If the passport runs out soon, renew it first, because a visa in a passport that is about to expire may not help you.
  3. Check the timing: find out how long the application usually takes, and add extra time for delays. Some visas take days, others take weeks, and busy seasons make everything slower. Apply as early as the rules allow.
  4. Check the documents: gather the papers listed in the section above, and read the application form once before you start filling it in, so you know what will be asked.

Only when all four checks are done should you book the flight. A cheap ticket is not cheap if you cannot board the plane.

Common mistakes

  • Booking a flight before checking the visa: this is the most expensive mistake on this page. A ticket you cannot refund is worth nothing if you cannot enter the country.
  • Trusting a random blog over the embassy: blogs go out of date, and many are written for a different nationality. The embassy page is the only source that counts.
  • Applying for the wrong visa type: a tourist visa does not cover work or study, and using the wrong type can end a trip at the border.
  • Leaving the application too late: processing times are estimates, not promises. A delay of a few days can cost you the whole trip.
  • Forgetting the passport rules: an almost-empty passport, or one that expires soon, can stop a trip even when the visa itself is fine.
  • Paying a stranger who promises a guaranteed visa: nobody outside the government can decide your application, and a fee paid to a stranger is usually gone for good.

What this page cannot do

This page cannot tell you whether you need a visa. Only the embassy or the official site of the country you plan to visit can answer that for your passport and your trip, and the answer can change with little notice.

This page cannot apply for a visa for you, cannot check the status of an application, and cannot speed up a decision. It also cannot cover every country, every nationality, or every special case, such as dual nationality or a refugee travel document. For those, the embassy is the only safe place to ask.

What this page can do is teach you the words, show you the general shape of the rules, and point you to the official sources. Use it to plan, then verify, then book. That order keeps your money and your trip safe.

Questions people ask

Do I need a visa to travel?

It depends on your nationality, the country you are visiting, and the reason for your trip. Some visitors can enter some countries without a visa, and others need one. The only safe answer comes from the embassy or the official site of the country you plan to visit, so check there before you book.

What is a visa on arrival?

A visa on arrival is a visa you apply for when you land at the airport or the border, instead of before you travel. You fill in a form, show your documents, and pay a fee if one is asked for. It is not open to every nationality and it is not guaranteed, so check the official rules first.

What is an e-visa?

An e-visa is a visa you apply for online before you travel, on an official website. You fill in a form, upload documents, and receive the decision online. Apply only on the official site, because copycat websites charge extra for the same form.

How long does a visa take?

It varies widely by country and by visa type. Some applications take a few days and others take weeks, and busy seasons make everything slower. Check the official processing time for your case, add extra time for delays, and apply as early as the rules allow.

What documents do I need?

It depends on the country and the visa type, but the usual list is a valid passport, passport photos, proof of where you will stay, proof of your travel plans and that you can pay for the trip, and proof of the reason for the visit. The official application page lists the exact documents for your case.

Can a visa be refused?

Yes. A visa can be refused if the documents are incomplete, if the reason for the visit is not clear, or if the officers are not satisfied with the application. A refusal does not always end the matter: the official letter usually explains what to do next, including whether you can apply again.
