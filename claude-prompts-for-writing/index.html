30 Claude Prompts for Writing

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

These thirty original prompts cover the whole writing process with Claude: drafting new text, editing what you have, changing the style, fixing the structure, and getting useful feedback. Each prompt has a one-line note on how to adapt it to your own project. Copy a prompt, fill in the bracketed parts, and paste it into a chat.

Good writing prompts share a pattern. They say what you want, give the context Claude needs, and name the shape of the answer. A prompt that says "write something about gardening" will get a generic paragraph. A prompt that says what the piece is for, who will read it, and how long it should be will get something you can actually use. Every prompt below follows that pattern, and the adaptation notes show you how to bend each one to fit your task.

The prompts are grouped by the stage of writing they help with. Start with drafting when you face a blank page, move to structure when the ideas are there but the order is wrong, use style when the words are right but the voice is off, use editing when the draft is nearly done, and use feedback whenever you want a second opinion. You can use them in any order, and you can use several in one chat.

Claude can make mistakes, even with a good prompt. It can invent facts, quotes, and statistics, and it can misunderstand your draft. Always check the result before you use it, especially for anything factual, and never paste personal details, names, or private information into a chat.

How to use these prompts

Each prompt below is written to be copied as it stands. The square brackets mark the parts you fill in: your topic, your audience, your draft, or your word count. Replace the brackets and everything inside them with your own words, then paste the whole prompt into Claude. If a prompt asks you to paste text, paste the text after the prompt, or attach it as a file if that is easier.

If the first answer is close but not right, do not start over. Follow up in the same chat and say what to change: shorter, warmer, simpler, more detail on one point. The follow-up is where most of the value is, because Claude can adjust an answer far faster than it can write a new one from scratch. Keep the conversation in one chat while you work on one piece of writing.

Drafting

  1. "Draft the opening paragraph of an essay about [topic] for [audience]. Start with a surprising fact, then state the main argument in one sentence." Adapt it: swap the topic and audience for your own, and change the opening hook to a question or a short story if a fact does not suit the subject.
  2. "Write a first draft of a short story opening set in [place]. Introduce one character, one problem, and one detail that makes the place feel real." Adapt it: name the character and the problem yourself if you already know them, and set the length to a paragraph or a page.
  3. "Turn these rough notes into a clean first draft of a [letter, email, or report]: [paste notes]. Keep the tone [formal or friendly] and the length under [number] words." Adapt it: say who will read it, since the reader decides the tone more than anything else.
  4. "Draft a product description for [product] that a [type of buyer] would find convincing. Mention the three most useful features and end with one clear reason to buy." Adapt it: list the features yourself if accuracy matters, and name the buyer as specifically as you can.
  5. "Write the introduction to a guide about [topic] for complete beginners. Explain what the guide covers, who it is for, and what the reader will be able to do afterwards." Adapt it: add the one thing beginners usually get wrong, so the introduction can promise to fix it.
  6. "Draft a speech of about [number] words for [occasion]. Open with a short personal story, then make two main points, then close with one line people will remember." Adapt it: give Claude the personal story in your own words, since it cannot invent a true one for you.

Editing

  1. "Read this draft and fix the grammar and spelling only. Do not change the wording or the order of ideas: [paste draft]." Adapt it: add "and list every change you made" if you want to learn from the corrections.
  2. "Tighten this paragraph by removing filler words and repeated ideas. Keep the meaning exactly the same: [paste paragraph]." Adapt it: set a target length, such as half the original, to control how hard it cuts.
  3. "Find every sentence longer than twenty-five words in this text and rewrite each one as two shorter sentences: [paste text]." Adapt it: change the number to suit your style, or ask it to flag long sentences without rewriting them.
  4. "Check this email for tone. Tell me any sentence that sounds rude, cold, or confusing, and suggest a warmer version of each: [paste email]." Adapt it: name the reader and your relationship to them, since tone depends on who is reading.
  5. "Proofread this text and list every error you find in a table with three columns: the mistake, the correction, and why it is wrong: [paste text]." Adapt it: ask for only the most serious errors first if the list would be long.
  6. "Rewrite this paragraph so a twelve-year-old can understand it. Keep all the facts, but use simpler words and shorter sentences: [paste paragraph]." Adapt it: change the age to match your real reader, or name the reader directly instead.

Style

  1. "Rewrite this paragraph in a warm, conversational style, as if I am explaining it to a friend over coffee: [paste paragraph]." Adapt it: name a different setting or a different kind of friend to shift the voice further.
  2. "Rewrite this text in a formal, professional style suitable for a business report. Keep the facts the same: [paste text]." Adapt it: say what the report is for, since a report for managers differs from one for clients.
  3. "Give me three versions of this sentence: one playful, one serious, and one plain and direct: [paste sentence]." Adapt it: ask for five versions if you are choosing a headline or a slogan.
  4. "This paragraph sounds flat. Suggest five more vivid verbs and five more precise adjectives I could use, and show me the paragraph with your best three swaps in place: [paste paragraph]." Adapt it: ask for nouns as well if the paragraph is full of vague ones like "thing" and "stuff".
  5. "Rewrite this opening in the style of a newspaper feature: a strong first line, a human detail, and a sense of place: [paste opening]." Adapt it: name a different style, such as a diary entry or a travel blog, to try another voice.
  6. "Make this text sound more confident. Remove hedging words like 'just', 'maybe', and 'I think', and rewrite any weak sentence: [paste text]." Adapt it: list your own hedging words if you have pet phrases you overuse.

Structure

  1. "Turn this jumble of ideas into an outline with three main sections and two sub-points under each: [paste ideas]." Adapt it: change the number of sections to match the length of the piece you are planning.
  2. "I have written the middle of this essay but not the beginning or the end. Suggest an opening paragraph and a closing paragraph that fit what I have written: [paste middle]." Adapt it: tell Claude the argument in one sentence so the opening and closing match it.
  3. "Reorder these paragraphs so the argument flows logically. Tell me the new order and why each paragraph belongs where you put it: [paste paragraphs]." Adapt it: number your paragraphs first so the new order is easy to follow.
  4. "Break this long article into sections with a heading for each. Give me the heading list and a one-line summary of what each section should cover: [paste article]." Adapt it: ask for headings in a particular style, such as questions or short phrases.
  5. "My argument has a hole in it. Read this draft and tell me where the reasoning jumps, what evidence is missing, and what a sceptical reader would ask: [paste draft]." Adapt it: name the sceptical reader, such as a teacher or a manager, to sharpen the critique.
  6. "Turn this list of points into a story structure: a beginning that sets up a problem, a middle that works through it, and an ending that resolves it: [paste list]." Adapt it: say how long the story should be, since that decides how much detail each part needs.

Feedback

  1. "Read this draft as a friendly editor. Tell me the three strongest parts, the three weakest parts, and the one change that would help the most: [paste draft]." Adapt it: ask for a stricter editor if you want blunt feedback instead of encouragement.
  2. "Pretend you are my target reader, a [type of reader]. Read this text and tell me where you get bored, where you get confused, and where you want more detail: [paste text]." Adapt it: describe the reader as specifically as you can, including what they already know.
  3. "Grade this essay against these three criteria: clear argument, good evidence, and readable style. Give a short verdict on each and one suggestion for improvement: [paste essay]." Adapt it: replace the criteria with your teacher's rubric or your own checklist.
  4. "Read this story opening and tell me whether it makes you want to keep reading. Be specific about what works and what does not: [paste opening]." Adapt it: say what genre the story is, since a thriller opening is judged differently from a comedy one.
  5. "Compare these two versions of the same paragraph and tell me which is stronger and why. Suggest a third version that keeps the best of both: [paste version A and version B]." Adapt it: say what the paragraph needs to do, such as persuade, explain, or entertain.
  6. "I am stuck on this draft. Read it and ask me five questions that would help me see what is missing or what to write next: [paste draft]." Adapt it: tell Claude where you feel stuck, so the questions aim at the real problem.

Adapting a prompt to your project

The adaptation notes are the most important part of this page. A prompt copied word for word will give a decent answer, but a prompt shaped to your project will give a much better one. The three things that matter most are the reader, the purpose, and the length. Tell Claude who will read the piece, what it needs to do, and how long it should be, and almost any prompt on this page improves.

It also helps to keep your best prompts. When a prompt works well, save it somewhere with a short note about what it did. Over time you will build a small library of prompts that fit the way you write, which is worth far more than a long list of prompts you have never tried. Start with two or three from this page, adapt them until they feel like yours, and add to the collection as you find what works.

Questions people ask

How do I use a prompt from this list?

Copy the prompt, fill in the bracketed parts with your own topic, audience, or text, and paste it into Claude. The adaptation note under each prompt tells you what to change first if the result is not quite right.

Should I paste my whole draft into Claude?

Yes, the editing and feedback prompts work best when Claude can read the full text. Before pasting, remove any personal details, names, or private information that the task does not need.

What if the answer is not what I wanted?

Follow up in the same chat. Say what was wrong in plain words, such as too formal, too long, or missing the main point, and ask for another version. Two or three short follow-ups usually get a much better result than the first answer.

Can these prompts be used with other chatbots?

Yes. The prompts are written in plain words and work with any conversational chatbot. The results will differ a little between chatbots, but the method is the same: give context, state the task, and ask for a specific shape of answer.

Will Claude write the whole piece for me?

It can draft a whole piece, but the best results come from your ideas. Use Claude to get started, to fix a stuck paragraph, or to polish a draft you wrote yourself, then edit the result in your own voice before you use it.
