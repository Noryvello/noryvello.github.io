When to watch in 2028

The Perseids peak on the night of Friday 11 to Saturday 12 August 2028. That is the night to aim for: the hours of darkness between Friday evening and Saturday morning, when the shower is at its strongest. Meteors can be seen for several nights around the peak, and the nights just before and after it are worth a look too, but the peak night is the one to put in your diary.

The best viewing comes after dark, and the late night hours are usually the most rewarding. The shower's meteors appear across a wide stretch of sky, so you do not need to stare at one spot: lie back, look up, and give the sky time. The countdown above tracks the start of the peak night, and the checklist helps you get ready for it.

What causes the Perseids

The Perseids happen every August, when the Earth passes through a trail of dust left behind by the comet 109P/Swift-Tuttle. The comet sheds dust and small rocky grains along its orbit, and each year the Earth runs into that trail. The grains strike the upper atmosphere at high speed and burn up, and the streak of light they leave is the meteor you see. A meteor shower is simply a night when the Earth is passing through a thick part of such a trail.

The shower is named for the constellation Perseus, because the meteors appear to stream outwards from that part of the sky. That point is only a matter of perspective: the meteors are burning up in the atmosphere above you, and they only seem to come from Perseus because of the direction the Earth is travelling. You do not need to find Perseus to enjoy the shower; the meteors cross the whole sky.

Most of the grains are tiny, no bigger than a grain of sand, and they burn up completely high above the ground. The brightest meteors can leave a glowing trail that lingers for a second or two after the meteor itself has gone, and the very brightest may break into fragments as they burn. Nothing reaches the ground: what you are watching is dust turning to light, tens of miles above your head.

The 2028 forecast

Forecasters have noted the possibility of enhanced rates for the Perseids in 2028. The American Meteor Society and meteor researcher Esko Lyytinen have both drawn attention to the 2028 return, and the prediction is that the peak could be stronger than an ordinary year. That is a prediction, not a promise: meteor showers are hard to forecast exactly, and the real test is the night itself.

Whatever the numbers turn out to be, the peak night of 11 to 12 August is the night to watch. Even in an ordinary year the Perseids are one of the most reliable showers of the summer, with bright, fast meteors and a warm night to watch them from. A forecast of enhanced rates is a good reason to make the effort, not a guarantee of a record night.

How to watch

You need no equipment at all to watch the Perseids. A telescope would only narrow your view, and the meteors cross too much sky for binoculars to help. The naked eye is the best tool, and the most important thing is a dark sky. Get away from street lights and lit windows, and give your eyes time to adjust to the dark before you expect to see the fainter meteors.

Comfort matters, because meteor watching is a waiting game. Lie back on a blanket or a reclining chair so you can see as much sky as possible without straining your neck. Dress warmly, even in August: you will be still for a long while, and the night air cools fast. Put your phone away or keep it face down, because a bright screen spoils your night vision for minutes at a time. Then settle in and watch. The meteors come in bursts, with quiet spells between them, so patience is part of the show.

Cloud is the one thing you cannot beat. If the peak night is cloudy where you live, try the night before or the night after: the shower is active for days around the peak, and a clear near-peak night beats a cloudy peak night every time.
