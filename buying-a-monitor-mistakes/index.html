7 Mistakes to Avoid When Buying a Monitor

A monitor that suits someone else's desk may not suit yours. This guide covers the 7 mistakes buyers most often make, from size and resolution to ports and ergonomics, and ends with a picker that suggests a size and resolution from your desk distance and use.

A monitor is the part of your computer you actually look at, all day, every day, so a wrong choice is one you feel in your eyes and neck within a week. The mistakes in this guide come from shopping by a single number, usually the screen size or the price, instead of by the desk the monitor will sit on and the work it will do. Measure your desk, think about your usual distance from the screen, and work through the seven mistakes before you buy. The picker at the end turns your distance and use into a size and resolution to start from.

This guide is general buying advice with no brand recommendations. Screen sizes and resolutions are suggestions to start from, not rules — your eyes, desk and room light decide what works.

Mistake 1: Buying a size that does not fit your desk

The most common monitor mistake is buying a screen that is too big for the desk it sits on. A large screen at close range fills your whole field of view, which means turning your head to read the corners and tiring your eyes by the end of the day. Before you look at any screen, measure the width of your desk and the distance from where you sit to where the screen will stand. A smaller screen at a comfortable distance is easier to work with than a large one you have to lean back from. If your desk is shallow, a monitor arm can buy you a few extra centimetres of distance, but it cannot fix a screen that is simply too large for the space.

Mistake 2: Picking resolution without thinking about text size

Resolution and size have to be chosen together, because resolution decides how small text and icons appear. A high resolution on a small screen makes everything tiny, and while you can scale text up, the result is not always as crisp as a screen that matches its resolution naturally. A lower resolution on a large screen goes the other way: text looks large and soft. The right balance depends on your distance and your eyesight, so if you can, look at a screen of the size and resolution you are considering before you buy. Mistake 3: Ignoring viewing angles and panel differences

Not all screens look the same from the side. On some panels the colours wash out and the brightness drops as soon as you move off centre, which matters if you share your screen with someone sitting beside you, or if you often lean back and to the side. Different panel types trade viewing angle against contrast and speed, and there is no type that wins at everything. If you mostly sit directly in front of your screen, viewing angle matters less. If you share the screen or move around a lot, check how the picture holds up from the side before you commit.

Mistake 4: Forgetting refresh rate

Refresh rate decides how smooth motion looks on screen. For documents, email and video calls, a standard refresh rate is perfectly comfortable, and paying for more buys you nothing you will notice. For games and fast video, a higher refresh rate makes motion visibly smoother, and once you have seen it, going back can feel sluggish. The mistake is buying on refresh rate alone in either direction: paying for a fast screen you will only use for spreadsheets, or buying a slow screen for games and wondering why motion smears. Match the refresh rate to what you actually watch and play.

Mistake 5: Not checking ports and cables

A monitor is only useful if your computer can talk to it. Before you buy, count what you need to plug in: the computer itself, and anything else such as a second computer, a console or a work laptop. Then check the monitor has the right ports for all of them, and that the cable in the box matches your computer's output. Adapters can fix most mismatches, but they cost extra and add clutter, and some adapters limit resolution or refresh rate. A monitor with the ports you need, and the right cable in the box, is worth more than a slightly larger screen with the wrong connections.

Mistake 6: Ignoring ergonomics

A fixed stand forces your neck to adapt to the screen instead of the other way round. Look for a stand that adjusts in height, tilts, and ideally swivels, so you can put the top of the screen near eye level and angle it to suit your seating. If the stand does not adjust enough, check whether the monitor can mount on a standard arm, which gives you far more freedom and frees desk space underneath. Ergonomics matter most if you sit at the screen for hours at a time: a screen at the wrong height causes neck and shoulder strain that no amount of resolution can fix.

Mistake 7: Buying on screen size alone

Screen size is the number on the box, so it is the number buyers compare, but it says nothing about how the screen actually looks. Brightness decides whether the screen is readable in a bright room, contrast decides how deep dark scenes look, and the finish of the screen decides how much it reflects your windows. Two screens of the same size can look completely different side by side. Judge a monitor by the picture it shows in your kind of light, not by the diagonal measurement, and remember that a smaller screen with a better picture often beats a larger screen with a poor one.

Before you buy

Once the picker has given you a starting point, measure your desk again and check the screen will fit with room for your keyboard, speakers and anything else that shares the space. Count your ports, check the stand adjusts to your height, and confirm the return policy in case the screen does not suit your eyes. If you can, look at the screen in person with a document open at your usual distance, because a shop display of bright video hides how text will look during a working day. The right monitor is the one you forget you are looking at.

Questions people ask

How far should I sit from my monitor?

A comfortable distance is usually about an arm's length, with the top of the screen near eye level. The picker uses your desk distance to suggest a size that fits that range.

Is a bigger monitor always better?

No. A screen that is too big for your distance means turning your head to see the corners, which is tiring. Size should match distance, not ambition.

What resolution do I need for office work?

For documents and browsing, a modest resolution is fine at smaller sizes. What matters most is that text looks sharp and comfortable at your usual distance.

Do I need a high refresh rate?

For games and fast video, yes, it makes motion smoother. For documents, email and video calls, a standard refresh rate is usually enough.

What should I check before I buy?

Measure your desk, count your ports, check the stand adjusts to your height, and confirm the return policy in case the screen does not suit your eyes.
