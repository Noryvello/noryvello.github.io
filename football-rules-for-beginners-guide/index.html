Football Rules for Beginners Guide

Football is simple at heart: two teams of eleven try to put the ball in the other team's goal, and the team with more goals wins. The offside rule and the set pieces are the parts that confuse newcomers, and both are explained here in plain words.

Football is the most watched sport in the world, and its basic idea could not be simpler: two teams, one ball, two goals, and the team that scores more wins. Yet newcomers quickly run into words like offside, set piece and stoppage time, and the game can feel confusing until those words make sense.

This guide explains the pitch, the positions, the clock, offside in plain words, fouls and cards, and the set pieces that restart play. Use the term picker to look up any word you hear during a match.

This guide covers the basic Laws of the Game. Local leagues and children's games sometimes adapt the rules, so check with your league for the details.

The pitch

A football pitch is a rectangle of grass, longer than it is wide, with a goal at each end. The lines matter: the long edges are the touchlines, and the short edges are the goal lines. If the ball crosses a touchline, play restarts with a throw-in; if it crosses a goal line, play restarts with a corner kick or a goal kick, depending on who touched it last.

In front of each goal is the penalty area, a large box where the goalkeeper is allowed to handle the ball and where fouls by the defending team are punished with a penalty kick. Inside it is the smaller goal area, where goal kicks are taken. The penalty spot sits twelve yards from the goal, and the centre circle marks the middle of the pitch, where play starts and restarts after each goal.

A goal counts when the whole ball crosses the whole goal line between the posts and under the crossbar. If any part of the ball is still on the line, it is not a goal. In major matches, goal-line technology helps the referee decide the closest calls.

Positions

Each team has eleven players, and each has a job. The goalkeeper guards the goal and is the only player allowed to use their hands, and only inside their own penalty area. Defenders play in front of the goalkeeper and try to stop the other team from scoring. Midfielders link defence and attack, winning the ball and passing it forward. Forwards, or strikers, play nearest the other team's goal and score most of the goals.

Teams arrange their players in formations, described by numbers — 4-4-2 means four defenders, four midfielders and two forwards, not counting the goalkeeper. Formations change during a match as managers react to the score, and modern players often move between roles, but the basic shape of goalkeeper, defenders, midfielders and forwards stays the same.

Substitutes sit on the bench and can be brought on during the match to replace tired or injured players, or to change tactics. In most professional matches each team may make up to five substitutions, and a player who has been substituted cannot come back on.

The clock

A football match lasts ninety minutes, split into two halves of forty-five minutes with a fifteen-minute break in between. The clock does not stop for injuries, substitutions or goal celebrations, so the referee adds extra time at the end of each half, called stoppage time or added time, to make up for the minutes lost.

If the scores are level at the end of a league match, it is a draw and each team takes one point. In knockout matches, where somebody must go through, the game goes to extra time — two more halves of fifteen minutes — and if it is still level, a penalty shoot-out decides the winner, with each team taking five penalties in turn.

Offside, in plain words

Offside is the rule that confuses newcomers most, but the idea is simple: it stops attackers from standing next to the other team's goal waiting for the ball. A player is in an offside position when they are in the other team's half, ahead of the ball, and ahead of the second-last opponent at the moment the ball is played to them. Usually the second-last opponent is the last outfield defender, since the goalkeeper is the last.

Being in an offside position is not an offence on its own. The offence happens when the player then plays the ball, interferes with an opponent, or gains an advantage from the position. A player who is offside but takes no part in the play is not penalised.

There are three important exceptions: there is no offside in your own half, and there is no offside from a throw-in, a corner kick or a goal kick. And offside is judged at the moment the ball is played, not the moment the player receives it — a player who runs back from an onside position to collect the ball is fine.

Fouls and cards

A foul is unfair play: tripping, pushing, holding, charging unfairly, jumping at an opponent, or handling the ball on purpose. The referee awards a free kick to the other team, or a penalty if the foul happens inside the penalty area. Not every contact is a foul — football is a contact sport, and the referee judges whether the challenge was fair.

For more serious offences the referee shows cards. A yellow card is a warning, given for unsporting behaviour, a reckless foul, wasting time or arguing with the referee. Two yellow cards in one match mean a red card. A red card sends a player off immediately for a serious foul, violent conduct, or denying an obvious goal-scoring chance, and the team plays the rest of the match with one fewer player.

Set pieces

Set pieces are the planned restarts that follow a stoppage, and they are responsible for a large share of goals. A corner kick is awarded when the ball crosses the goal line after last touching a defender, and it is taken from the nearest corner. A free kick follows a foul, and can be direct (scored from directly) or indirect (must touch another player first). A penalty kick follows a foul inside the area, taken from twelve yards with only the goalkeeper to beat.

A throw-in restarts play after the ball crosses a touchline, thrown with both hands from behind the head. A goal kick restarts play when the ball crosses the goal line after last touching an attacker. The term picker below explains each set piece in plain words.

Questions people ask

How long is a football match?

A match lasts ninety minutes, split into two halves of forty-five minutes with a fifteen-minute break. The referee adds stoppage time at the end of each half to make up for minutes lost to injuries and substitutions.

What is offside?

A player is offside when they are in the other team's half, ahead of the ball and ahead of the second-last opponent when the ball is played to them, and then take part in play. There is no offside in your own half or from a throw-in, corner or goal kick.

What is the difference between a free kick and a penalty?

A free kick is awarded for a foul anywhere on the pitch and is taken from where the foul happened. A penalty is awarded for a foul inside the penalty area and is taken from the penalty spot, twelve yards from goal.

When is a player sent off?

A player is sent off with a red card for a serious foul, violent conduct, or denying an obvious goal-scoring chance — or after receiving two yellow cards in the same match. Their team plays on with one fewer player.

What happens if the scores are level?

In a league match it is a draw and each team takes one point. In a knockout match the game goes to extra time, and if it is still level, a penalty shoot-out decides the winner.
