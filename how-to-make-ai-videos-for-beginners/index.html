How to Make AI Videos: A Beginner's Guide

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

Making an AI video means generating short clips from text or images, reviewing them, and joining the best ones in an editing tool. This guide walks you through the whole process, from picking a tool type and writing your first video prompt to assembling a finished video you can share.

If you have never made a video before, AI video tools are a friendly place to start. You do not need a camera, a studio, or actors. You need an idea, a few sentences describing it, and the patience to review what the generator produces and try again when it misses. The generator does the filming; you do the directing and the editing.

It helps to think of the process as three jobs: planning, generating, and assembling. Planning decides what the video shows and in what order. Generating produces the raw clips. Assembling turns those clips into a video with pacing, sound, and captions. Beginners often skip the planning and jump straight to generating, which is why so many first AI videos feel like a random collection of pretty clips rather than a video with a point.

Honest limit: AI video has real limits. Clips are short, characters and objects can change between clips, and text on screen usually comes out garbled. Interfaces and features change often, so check your tool's current help pages rather than relying on any button-by-button guide.

Picking a tool type

AI video tools fall into a few broad types, and the right one depends on what you want to make. Text-to-video tools generate clips from a written description alone. Image-to-video tools animate a picture you provide. Some tools do both, and some add extra features such as camera controls, longer clips, or built-in editing.

For a first project, a tool that does both text-to-video and image-to-video is the most flexible choice. You can start with text for scenes you can describe, and switch to image-to-video when you need a specific look or want to keep a character consistent across clips.

Check what a tool offers before you commit to it. Useful things to look for include the maximum clip length, whether you can choose the aspect ratio, whether the tool supports vertical video for short-form platforms, and what happens to your uploads and generations under its terms. Plans and prices change, so check the current pricing page, and start with a free tier if one is available.

Also consider where the tool runs. Some tools run in a web browser, which is convenient and works on any computer. Others are apps for a phone or a desktop program. A browser tool is the easiest starting point, because there is nothing to install and your clips are available wherever you sign in.

Writing your first video prompt

A good video prompt has four parts: the subject, the action, the setting, and the camera. Many beginners write only the subject, such as "a dog in a park," and then wonder why the result is dull. The action and the camera are what turn a still idea into a moving one.

Start with a simple formula: subject plus action plus setting plus camera movement plus mood. For example: "A golden retriever runs across a green park in the morning, leaves scattering behind it, camera following smoothly from the side, warm sunlight, cheerful mood." Every part of that sentence gives the model something to work with.

Keep the motion simple for your first clips. One clear action, one camera move, and a calm setting produce the cleanest results. "The camera slowly pushes in on a lighthouse as waves roll against the rocks below" is a good first prompt. A prompt with three actions, two camera moves, and a crowd of people is a recipe for a confused clip.

Write the prompt in plain, concrete words. The model responds to what it can see: colours, light, movement, and shapes. Abstract words like "beautiful" or "amazing" add little, because they do not describe anything specific. Replace them with concrete details: "golden evening light" instead of "beautiful light," or "slow, steady movement" instead of "amazing motion."

Generating and reviewing your clips

Once you have a prompt, generate the clip and watch it carefully. The first generation is rarely perfect, and that is normal. The review step is where you learn what works, and it is worth doing properly rather than rushing past it.

Watch the whole clip, not just the first second. Check the beginning, the middle, and the end, because artefacts often appear partway through. Look at faces and hands, which are the most likely to distort. Check that objects keep their shape and that the background stays sensible. Listen if the clip has sound, though most generated clips are silent.

When a clip is close but not right, change one thing in the prompt and regenerate. If the motion is too fast, add "slowly" or "gently." If the subject is wrong, describe it more precisely. If the composition is off, add a camera word such as "wide shot" or "close-up." Changing one thing at a time teaches you what each word does.

Generate more clips than you need. If your video needs five shots, generate eight or ten. Having spares means you can pick the best take for each shot instead of settling for the only one you have. Save the clips with clear names that say which shot they belong to, so you can find them when you assemble the video.

Extending a clip

Most generated clips are only a few seconds long, which is often shorter than a single shot in a finished video. There are two common ways to make a shot longer: extending a clip, and joining clips together.

Some tools offer an extend feature, which continues a clip beyond its original length. The tool takes the last frames of the existing clip and generates more, following the same prompt. This can work well for slow, simple scenes, but the longer the extension, the more the scene can drift. Check the extended section carefully for morphing objects and changing faces.

The other method is to use the last frame of one clip as the starting image for the next. Take a screenshot or export the final frame, upload it as an image, and generate a new clip from it with a similar prompt. This gives you more control than automatic extension, because you choose exactly where the new clip begins. The join between the two clips may still need a cut or a short transition to hide a small jump.

For most beginner projects, joining separate clips is simpler than extending them. Plan your video as a series of short shots, each one a single generation, and let the editing do the work of making them feel continuous. A well-timed cut between two good clips looks better than a long clip that slowly falls apart.

Assembling clips into a finished video

Assembling is where a collection of clips becomes a video. You will need an editing tool, and there are good free options for both computers and phones. The basic skills are trimming, ordering, and adding transitions, and you can learn them in an afternoon.

Start by arranging your clips in the order of your plan. Watch the sequence through and check the pacing: does each shot stay on screen long enough to be understood, but not so long that it drags? Short shots of two to four seconds keep a video moving. Longer shots suit calm scenes where the viewer needs time to take in the detail.

Trim the weak parts of each clip. The beginning and end of a generated clip are often the weakest moments, because the model is settling into the scene or drifting out of it. Cutting a second from each end can remove most artefacts. A short, clean clip always looks better than a longer clip with a visible flaw.

Use simple transitions. A straight cut between shots is the most reliable choice, and it suits most videos. Fades and dissolves work for calm, slow videos, but they can look amateurish if overused. Keep the editing invisible: the viewer should notice the video, not the transitions.

Adding sound, music, and captions

Sound changes how a video feels more than any visual effect. Music sets the mood, a voiceover explains what the viewer is seeing, and sound effects add realism. Most generated clips are silent, so sound is something you add in editing.

Music is the simplest addition. Choose a track that matches the mood of your video, and keep the volume low enough that it supports the visuals rather than overwhelming them. Use music you have the right to use, such as tracks from a royalty-free library or music you made yourself. Avoid using commercial songs without permission.

Captions help in two ways: they make the video accessible to people who watch without sound, and they let you add words that the generator could not render. Keep captions short, use a readable size, and check that they stay on screen long enough to read. Most editing tools can generate captions automatically from a voiceover, but always check the wording before publishing.

If your video needs a voiceover, record it yourself or use a text-to-speech voice, then place it over the clips in the editing tool. A short, clear voiceover can turn a wordless sequence of clips into a video that explains something. Keep the script simple and speak slowly enough that the words land.

The limits to plan around

AI video is improving quickly, but it still has limits that shape what you can make. Planning around them is the difference between a frustrating project and a satisfying one.

  • Length. Individual clips are short, so plan your video as a series of shots rather than one long take. A storyboard of short shots is the natural shape for AI video.
  • Consistency. Characters, objects, and settings can change between clips. Reuse the same style words, use image-to-video from a consistent starting image, and generate extra takes so you can pick matching clips.
  • Text on screen. Words in generated video usually come out garbled. Add all titles, labels, and captions in editing, where you control them exactly.
  • Complex action. Scenes with many people, fast movement, or precise sequences often fail. Keep the action simple: one subject, one movement, one camera move per shot.
  • Faces and hands. Close-ups of faces and hands are the most likely to distort. Use wider shots for people, and check close-ups frame by frame.

These limits are not reasons to avoid AI video. They are the shape of the medium right now, and working with them, rather than fighting them, is what produces good results. The creators who make the best AI videos are the ones who plan for short, simple, consistent shots and let the editing carry the story.

Questions people ask

Do I need editing skills to make AI videos?

Basic editing helps a lot, but you can start with simple skills: trimming clips, joining them in order, and adding music and captions. Free editing tools cover all of these. The generator makes the clips, but the editing is what turns them into a video worth watching.

How long can an AI video be?

A single generated clip is usually only a few seconds long, but a finished video can be any length because you join many clips together in an editing tool. Plan your video as a series of short shots, and generate each shot separately.

Why do my AI video clips look different from each other?

Each generation starts fresh, so the model may interpret the same prompt slightly differently every time. To keep clips consistent, reuse the same style words, keep the same starting image for image-to-video, and generate extra takes so you can pick the ones that match best.

Can AI video show readable text on screen?

Usually not reliably. Text in generated video often scrambles into gibberish, even when the rest of the clip looks good. Add titles, labels, and captions afterwards in an editing tool, where you control the wording exactly.

What is the best first AI video project for a beginner?

A short, simple video with three to five shots and one clear subject works best, such as a ten-second nature scene or a short product showcase. Keep the prompts simple, the motion slow, and the editing minimal, and treat the first project as practice.
