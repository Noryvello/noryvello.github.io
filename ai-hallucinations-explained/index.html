AI Hallucinations Explained: Why AI Makes Things Up

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

An AI hallucination is a confident answer that sounds true but is not: an invented fact, a fake citation, or a detail that never existed. Hallucinations happen because chatbots predict likely words rather than looking up facts. This guide explains why they happen, shows the common types with worked examples, and teaches you how to catch them before you rely on them.

Ask a chatbot a question and it will answer in smooth, confident sentences. The confidence is part of the problem. A chatbot sounds just as sure when it is wrong as when it is right, because it does not know the difference. It is not lying, and it is not guessing on purpose. It is producing the most likely words, and sometimes the most likely words are false.

The word for this is hallucination, and it is one of the most important things to understand about AI. Once you know why hallucinations happen, and what they look like, you can use chatbots safely: as a drafting tool and a brainstorming partner, with a habit of checking anything that matters.

A chatbot's confident tone is not evidence that it is right. Never rely on an AI answer alone for facts that matter, such as health, money, law, or safety. Verify important claims against independent sources, and treat any citation or quotation as unverified until you have checked it yourself.

What a hallucination is

In AI, a hallucination is an output that sounds plausible but is false or unsupported. It might be a made-up fact, a quotation that was never said, a book that does not exist, or a confident explanation of something that works differently. The key feature is the gap between how true it sounds and how true it is.

Hallucinations are different from ordinary mistakes. A search engine that returns the wrong page is wrong in a visible way: you can see the source and judge it. A hallucination comes with no source, or with a fake one, and it arrives in the same confident voice as a correct answer. That makes it harder to notice.

The term itself is a metaphor, and it can mislead. A chatbot does not see things that are not there, the way a person hallucinating does. It generates text by predicting likely words, and when the likely words describe something false, the result is a hallucination. Keeping that mechanism in mind helps you understand why the problem is so stubborn.

Why confident wrong answers happen

A chatbot is trained to produce text that looks like good writing. During training, it learns which words tend to follow which other words, across an enormous amount of text. When you ask a question, it generates the most likely continuation, word by word. It is not checking a database of facts; it is completing a pattern.

That is why fluency and accuracy come apart. A well-formed sentence can be completely wrong, and a chatbot has no way to feel the difference. It does not know what it knows. It has no sense of certainty, no memory of where it learned a fact, and no ability to say that it is unsure unless it has been trained to hedge.

Gaps in knowledge make hallucinations more likely. Ask about something rare, recent, or specific, and the model has fewer patterns to draw on. Instead of saying it does not know, it often fills the gap with its best guess, presented in the same confident voice. The more specific your question, the more you should check the answer.

Fake citations and invented facts

Citations are a common hallucination. Ask a chatbot for sources, and it may give you a list of books and articles with real-sounding titles, real-sounding authors, and plausible dates. Some of those sources are real. Some are invented, or stitched together from pieces of real sources. The mix is hard to spot without checking each one.

Invented facts follow the same pattern. A chatbot may tell you that a historical event happened in a particular year, that a public figure said a particular thing, or that a study found a particular result, and get the detail wrong while keeping the shape of a true statement. The answer feels solid because it has the texture of a fact: names, dates, and numbers.

Quotations are especially risky. A chatbot can produce a convincing quotation attributed to a famous person, complete with a plausible source, that the person never said. Quotations are easy to check: search for the exact words in quotation marks, and see whether any trusted source carries them. If no source does, the quotation is almost certainly invented.

Types of hallucinations, with worked examples

Invented facts are the simplest type. Imagine asking a chatbot about a small town's history, and it tells you the town was founded in a specific year by a named person, with a story about the founding. The story sounds detailed and specific. But if you check the town's own history page, the year is different, the person does not exist, and the founding story is invented. The detail was the giveaway: real history is usually messier than the chatbot's tidy version.

Fake quotations are a second type. Ask for a quotation about hard work from a famous inventor, and the chatbot may return a polished sentence attributed to that inventor, with a book title attached. A search for the exact words finds nothing, and the book does not contain the quotation. The chatbot produced a sentence that sounds like something the inventor might have said, and attached a real name to it.

Mixed-up details are a third type. A chatbot explaining a recipe might combine two similar dishes, giving you the ingredients of one and the method of the other. A chatbot describing a historical event might merge two events that happened years apart. Each piece is real, but the combination is wrong, and the error is easy to miss because every part sounds familiar.

Confident numbers are a fourth type. Ask how many of something exist, how long something takes, or what a figure is, and the chatbot may give you a precise number that sounds researched. Numbers feel authoritative, which is why this type is dangerous. A precise number from a chatbot is a claim to check, not a fact to quote, unless you have verified it against a trusted source.

How to catch them: verify, ask for sources, cross-check

Verification starts with a simple question: does this claim appear anywhere else? Search for the key fact, name, or number, and see whether an independent source confirms it. One confirming source is good. Two independent sources are better. If no source confirms it, treat the claim as unverified, however confident the chatbot sounded.

Asking for sources can help, but only if you check them. A chatbot may give you real sources, fake sources, or a mix. Click through to each one, and confirm that it says what the chatbot claims. If a source does not exist, or does not say what was claimed, the answer is unreliable, even if other parts of it check out.

Cross-checking is the strongest habit. Compare the chatbot's answer with a trusted source you already know: an official website, a reference book, or a specialist publication. The comparison catches errors that a single search might miss, because you are testing the answer against knowledge you trust, not just against the internet in general.

When hallucinations matter most

Hallucinations matter most when the stakes are high and the reader cannot check easily. Health advice, legal information, financial claims, and safety instructions are all areas where a confident wrong answer can cause real harm. In these areas, a chatbot should be a starting point for research, never the final authority.

They also matter in work and study. A hallucinated fact in a report, a fake citation in an essay, or an invented figure in a presentation can damage your credibility, even if you did not know it was wrong. Checking before you publish is part of the job, and it is your responsibility, not the chatbot's.

In low-stakes uses, hallucinations matter less. A made-up detail in a brainstorming list, or a wrong suggestion for a party theme, costs little. The skill is matching your checking effort to the stakes: light checking for fun, careful checking for work, and thorough checking for anything that affects health, money, or safety.

Using AI safely despite hallucinations

The safest way to use a chatbot is to treat it as a draft writer, not a source of truth. Let it help you organise ideas, draft text, and explore options. Then check the facts yourself, using sources you trust. The chatbot saves you time on the drafting, and you keep control of the truth.

You can also reduce hallucinations by asking better questions. Give the chatbot context, ask it to show its reasoning step by step, and ask it to say when it is unsure. These habits do not remove hallucinations, but they make them easier to spot, because a step-by-step answer shows you where the reasoning went wrong.

Finally, keep a healthy habit of doubt. If an answer surprises you, check it. If an answer feels too neat, check it. If an answer matters, check it. Doubt is not distrust of the tool; it is the correct way to use a tool that is fluent, helpful, and sometimes wrong.

Questions people ask

Why do chatbots hallucinate instead of saying they do not know?

Chatbots are trained to produce helpful-sounding answers, and they generate text by predicting likely words rather than checking facts. Saying I do not know is a behaviour that must be trained in, and even well-trained models will sometimes guess rather than admit a gap, especially on specific questions.

Are some chatbots less likely to hallucinate than others?

Yes, models differ, and they improve over time. But every current chatbot can hallucinate, and the difference between models is a matter of degree, not of kind. Whatever tool you use, verify important facts against independent sources.

Can I trust a chatbot's citations?

Not without checking them. Chatbots can invent citations, or attach real citations to claims they do not support. Click through to each source and confirm that it exists and says what was claimed. A citation you have not checked is not evidence.

Do hallucinations happen with images and video too?

Yes, in a similar way. Image and video tools can produce confident-looking results with wrong details: extra fingers, garbled text, impossible objects. The same principle applies: check the result carefully, and do not treat a polished output as proof of accuracy.

Will hallucinations ever be fixed completely?

Probably not completely. Hallucinations come from the way these models work: predicting likely text rather than looking up verified facts. Models are improving, and techniques like showing sources and checking answers help, but a habit of verification will remain necessary for the foreseeable future.
