Resin Art Project Guide

Resin art turns a two-part liquid into glossy coasters, trays and jewellery, but the craft starts with safety, not colour. This guide compares resin types, covers the safety rules in full, and walks you through a first project step by step.

Safety first, always. Resin is a chemical: wear nitrile gloves, eye protection and a respirator rated for organic vapours, work with good ventilation, keep resin off your skin, and keep it away from children and pets. Read the product label and follow it exactly.

What resin art is

Resin art is made by mixing a liquid resin with a hardener, pouring it into a mould or over a surface, and letting it cure into a hard, glossy solid. While it is liquid, you can add colour, glitter, dried flowers or small objects, and the resin holds them in place as it sets. The finished pieces look like glass: coasters, trays, jewellery, key rings and decorative bowls are all common first projects.

The craft is popular because the results look impressive and the basic method is simple: mix, pour, wait. But resin is a chemical, not a paint, and it deserves respect. The uncured liquid can irritate skin and eyes, and the fumes need ventilation. Every good resin artist starts with the safety rules in this guide and follows them every single time, even for a small pour.

This guide covers the two main resin types, the safety rules, a first project in full detail, and the most common problems and their fixes. It does not recommend any brand. Resin products differ, and the label on the product you buy is the final word on mixing ratios, working time and curing time.

Epoxy resin versus UV resin

The two resins beginners meet are epoxy resin and UV resin, and they work in very different ways. Epoxy resin comes as two parts, a resin and a hardener, which you mix together. The mixture then cures by a chemical reaction over hours or days. UV resin comes as one part and stays liquid until you shine ultraviolet light on it, when it cures in minutes.

For a first project, epoxy resin is the usual choice. It is cheaper for coasters and trays, it pours in thicker layers, and it gives you time to arrange colours and decorations before it sets. UV resin is better for small jewellery pieces and for fixing small details, because it cures fast and needs no mixing. Many artists end up using both, for different jobs.

Whichever resin you choose, buy a small amount first. Resin has a shelf life, and a large bottle that sits unused for a year may not cure properly when you finally open it. Start small, learn the craft, and buy more when you know you will use it.

Safety first: the rules you never skip

Resin safety is not optional, and it is not a one-time thing. The rules below apply to every pour, large or small, and skipping them because the project is quick is how accidents happen. Read them before you buy anything.

  • Wear nitrile gloves every time you handle uncured resin. Resin on skin can cause irritation and, with repeated contact, a lasting allergy. If resin touches your skin, wash it off at once with soap and water. Do not use solvents on your skin.

Cured resin is generally safe to handle, but sanding it creates fine dust that you should not breathe. Sand wet, outdoors or with extraction, and wear your respirator and eye protection. And never pour leftover liquid resin down a drain: let it cure in the mixing cup, then dispose of the solid waste as your local rules require.

What you need: materials and tools

A first project needs a short list of supplies. Buy the safety gear first, then the resin and the mould, then the colours and decorations. You do not need the most expensive version of anything to start.

  • Epoxy resin and hardener, in a small kit, with the label read before you start.

Do not substitute household items for safety gear. Kitchen gloves tear, sunglasses are not safety glasses, and a paper mask does not stop fumes. The right gear costs little and protects you every time you pour.

Your first project: resin coasters, step by step

Coasters are the classic first resin project: they are small, useful, and forgiving of small mistakes. Follow the steps in order, and read your resin label first, because the mixing ratio and working time on the label override anything written here.

  1. Set up your workspace. Cover the surface, open the windows, put on your gloves, glasses and respirator, and lay out everything you need.

Your first coaster may have a bubble or two, or a slightly uneven edge. That is normal. The second one will be better, and by the fourth you will have the feel of the material.

Colour and effects

Colour is where resin art becomes personal. Pigment paste gives strong, opaque colour. Mica powder gives a shimmering, metallic look that shifts in the light. Alcohol ink made for resin creates flowing, petal-like patterns when dropped into a fresh pour. Each one behaves differently, so try them one at a time on small test pieces before you commit to a large pour.

Less is more with colour. A small amount of pigment goes a long way, and too much can stop the resin curing properly. The same goes for glitter and dried flowers: a few well-placed pieces look better than a crowded surface, and organic material must be fully dry before it goes into resin, or it can trap moisture and cloud the finish.

For a marble effect, pour two or three colours into the mould in separate streams and drag a wooden stick through them once, slowly. Do not stir: one slow pass makes the pattern, and more passes turn it to mud. For cells and lacing, a drop of silicone oil made for resin art can help, but use it sparingly and expect to practise.

Curing: time, temperature and patience

Curing is the chemical reaction that turns liquid resin into a solid, and it needs time and warmth. Most epoxy resins reach a firm, demouldable state in about a day and full hardness over two to three days, but the label on your product gives the real times, and they vary. Cold rooms slow the cure, and a room below the temperature on the label can leave the resin soft for days.

Keep the curing piece level, covered and undisturbed. Dust in the air will settle on a wet surface and stay there, so a loose cover, such as a cardboard box over the mould, is worth the trouble. Do not move the piece to a warmer spot halfway through, and do not test it with a fingernail: every poke leaves a mark.

If the resin is still soft after the full curing time, the most likely causes are a wrong mixing ratio, a cold room, or not enough stirring. A soft piece cannot always be saved, but a thin fresh layer of correctly mixed resin poured over a cleaned, fully cured surface can sometimes rescue a piece that is only slightly tacky.

Troubleshooting

Almost every resin problem has a known cause and a fix. The table below covers the ones beginners meet most often.

ProblemLikely causeWhat to do next time
Bubbles in the cured pieceAir stirred in, or air from the mouldStir slowly, pour low, warm the resin slightly first
Sticky or soft surfaceWrong ratio, cold room, or short mixingMeasure by weight, mix fully, keep the room warm
Cloudy finishMoisture, or a cold and damp roomWork in a dry room; dry decorations fully
Uneven or wavy edgeMould not level while curingLevel the work surface before you pour
Dust in the surfaceUncovered cure in a dusty roomCover the mould loosely while it cures
Resin stuck in the mouldWrong mould, or released too earlyUse silicone moulds; wait for a full cure

When a piece goes wrong, work out the cause before you pour again. Changing two things at once teaches you nothing, because you will not know which change fixed it. Keep a note of the resin, the ratio, the room temperature and the result for each pour, and the pattern will show itself quickly.

Cleaning up and storing resin safely

Cleaning up is part of the craft. Wipe mixing cups and sticks with paper towels while the resin is still liquid, then throw the towels away. Let any leftover resin cure in the cup, then peel it out and dispose of it as solid waste. Never wash liquid resin down a sink or drain.

Store resin and hardener in their original bottles, tightly closed, upright, in a cool, dry place away from sunlight and out of reach of children and pets. Keep the two parts apart, and label everything. Resin has a shelf life, so check the date on the bottle and do not use resin that has gone cloudy or thick in storage.

Keep your safety gear with your resin supplies, not in another room. The gloves, glasses and respirator should be the first things you pick up and the last things you put away. That habit is what keeps a fun craft safe.

Questions people ask

Is resin art safe?

Resin art is safe when the safety rules are followed every time: nitrile gloves, eye protection, a respirator rated for organic vapours, and good ventilation. The uncured liquid can irritate skin and eyes, and repeated skin contact can cause a lasting allergy. Never skip the safety gear, even for a small pour, and keep resin away from children and pets.

How long does epoxy resin take to cure?

Most epoxy resins are firm enough to remove from the mould in about a day and reach full hardness over two to three days, but the label on your product gives the real times and they vary. A cold room slows the cure, so keep the workspace at the temperature the label recommends and leave the piece undisturbed until it is fully hard.

Why is my resin still sticky after a day?

The most common causes are a wrong mixing ratio, a cold room, or not enough stirring. Epoxy resin must be measured by weight exactly as the label says, mixed slowly and thoroughly for the full time, and cured at the right temperature. A slightly tacky surface can sometimes be rescued with a thin fresh layer of correctly mixed resin over the cleaned surface.

How do I get rid of bubbles in resin?

Stir slowly to avoid whipping air in, pour in a thin stream from a low height, and pop surface bubbles with a quick pass of a heat gun held well away and moved constantly. Warming the resin bottles slightly in warm water before mixing also helps the bubbles rise. Deep bubbles in a thick pour may need a second thin layer to cover them.

Can I use resin indoors?

Yes, with good ventilation: open windows on two sides of the room so the air moves, and wear a respirator rated for organic vapours. Never work in a closed room, and keep children and pets out of the workspace. If you cannot ventilate the room well, work outdoors instead.

What is the difference between epoxy resin and UV resin?

Epoxy resin is two parts mixed together, and it cures by a chemical reaction over hours or days, which suits coasters, trays and larger pours. UV resin is one part that stays liquid until ultraviolet light shines on it, when it cures in minutes, which suits small jewellery and thin layers. Many artists use both for different jobs.
