Legal Terminology Encyclopedia

This encyclopedia explains common legal terms in plain language, with a short example for each one. It is written for anyone who has to read a contract, a letter from a lawyer, or a court document and wants to know what the words mean.

This page explains legal words in plain language. It is information only, not legal advice, and it never promises a legal outcome. For advice about your own situation, speak to a qualified lawyer.

How to use this encyclopedia

Legal language can feel like a foreign language, but most legal terms describe ordinary ideas: a promise, a debt, a disagreement, a decision. This encyclopedia takes the terms you are most likely to meet in everyday life — in contracts, in letters, in the news, in court papers — and explains each one in plain words, with a short example that shows how the term is used.

The terms are grouped by topic so you can find what you need: contracts and agreements, responsibility and harm, courts and lawsuits, property and money, wills and estates, and laws and rules. You do not need to read the page from start to finish. Look up the word you need, read the definition and the example, and move on. If a definition uses another legal word, that word has its own entry too.

A note on meaning: legal words can have slightly different meanings in different countries and in different kinds of document. The definitions here give the common, everyday meaning of each term. If a word matters for a decision you have to make — signing a contract, answering a court letter, making a will — ask a qualified lawyer to explain what it means in your own case. This page teaches the language; it does not give legal advice.

Terms about contracts and agreements

Contract. A contract is an agreement between two or more people or businesses that the law will enforce. Example: when you sign a phone agreement, you promise to pay each month and the company promises to provide the service.

Consideration. Consideration is what each side gives in a contract — money, work, goods, or a promise to do something. Example: in a sale, the buyer's money is the consideration and the seller's goods are the consideration on the other side.

Breach. A breach is a failure to do what a contract requires. Example: if a builder promises to finish work by June and does not, that is a breach of the contract.

Clause. A clause is a single part of a contract that deals with one point, such as payment, delivery, or what happens if something goes wrong. Example: a cancellation clause says how either side can end the agreement.

Covenant. A covenant is a formal promise in a written agreement, often about land or property. Example: a covenant in a house sale might promise that the buyer will not use the property as a shop.

Force majeure. Force majeure means an event nobody could prevent or predict — a natural disaster, a war, a pandemic — that stops someone from keeping a contract. Example: a concert cancelled because of a flood may be excused under a force majeure clause.

Terms about responsibility and harm

Liability. Liability means being legally responsible for something, usually for a loss, a debt, or harm done to someone. Example: if a shop's wet floor causes a fall, the shop may have liability for the injury.

Negligence. Negligence is a failure to take reasonable care, which causes harm to someone else. Example: a driver who looks at a phone instead of the road and causes a crash may have acted negligently.

Indemnity. Indemnity is a promise to pay for someone else's loss or costs if a stated thing happens. Example: a contractor's indemnity clause may promise to pay the client's legal costs if the contractor's work causes a claim.

Damages. Damages are money a court orders one person to pay another to make up for a loss or injury. Example: after a breach of contract, a court may award damages equal to the money the other side lost.

Tort. A tort is a civil wrong — harm done to a person or their property that is not a crime and not a breach of contract. Example: defamation and negligence are both kinds of tort.

Defamation. Defamation is a false statement about a person that harms their reputation, made in writing or speech. Example: publishing a false claim that a shopkeeper sells stolen goods could be defamation.

Terms about courts and lawsuits

Plaintiff. The plaintiff is the person or business that starts a civil court case, claiming that someone else has harmed them or owes them something. Example: in a case about unpaid work, the worker who brings the claim is the plaintiff.

Defendant. The defendant is the person or business that a court case is brought against. Example: in a case about a faulty product, the company that sold it is the defendant.

Arbitration. Arbitration is a way of settling a dispute outside court, where both sides agree to accept the decision of an independent person called an arbitrator. Example: many business contracts say that disputes will go to arbitration rather than to court.

Mediation. Mediation is a way of settling a dispute with the help of an independent person who helps both sides reach an agreement, but who does not decide the case. Example: neighbours in a boundary dispute might try mediation before going to court.

Subpoena. A subpoena is a court order telling a person to come to court to give evidence, or to bring documents. Example: a witness may receive a subpoena ordering them to appear on a set date.

Deposition. A deposition is a formal statement given under oath outside court, usually in a lawyer's office, where a witness answers questions that are written down. Example: before a trial, lawyers may take the deposition of a witness who lives far away.

Verdict. A verdict is the decision reached at the end of a trial, usually by a jury or a judge, about whether the claim or charge is proved. Example: the jury returned a verdict in favour of the plaintiff.

Appeal. An appeal is a request to a higher court to review the decision of a lower court, usually because one side believes the decision was wrong. Example: after losing a case, the defendant may appeal to a higher court.

Terms about property and money

Easement. An easement is a right to use someone else's land for a specific purpose, such as a path or a drain. Example: a house may have an easement allowing its owner to cross a neighbour's land to reach the road.

Lien. A lien is a legal claim over property that secures payment of a debt. Example: a builder who is not paid may place a lien on the property they worked on.

Mortgage. A mortgage is a loan used to buy property, where the property itself is the security for the loan. Example: most home buyers take out a mortgage and repay it over many years.

Escrow. Escrow is an arrangement where money or documents are held by a neutral third party until agreed conditions are met. Example: in a house sale, the deposit may be held in escrow until the sale completes.

Lease. A lease is a written agreement that gives someone the right to use property, such as a flat or a shop, for a set time in return for rent. Example: a two-year lease gives a tenant the right to live in the flat for two years.

Deed. A deed is a formal legal document, often used to transfer ownership of property. Example: when a house is sold, a deed records the transfer of ownership to the buyer.

Terms about wills and estates

Will. A will is a legal document in which a person states what should happen to their property after they die. Example: a will may leave a house to a spouse and savings to children.

Testament. A testament is another word for a will, used in the formal phrase "last will and testament". Example: the two words together mean the same document — a person's final written wishes.

Executor. An executor is the person named in a will to carry out its instructions, such as paying debts and passing property to the people named. Example: a woman may name her brother as executor of her will.

Probate. Probate is the legal process of confirming that a will is valid and giving the executor authority to act. Example: after a death, the executor applies for probate before selling the house.

Trust. A trust is an arrangement where one person holds property for the benefit of another person. Example: a parent may set up a trust to hold money for a child until the child is grown.

Fiduciary. A fiduciary is a person who has a legal duty to act in someone else's best interests, such as a trustee or a lawyer acting for a client. Example: a trustee is a fiduciary for the people who benefit from the trust.

Terms about laws and rules

Statute. A statute is a law passed by a legislature, such as a parliament or congress. Example: a statute may set the rules for how landlords must treat tenants.

Precedent. A precedent is a decision in an earlier court case that judges use as a guide when deciding a later case with similar facts. Example: a judge may follow a precedent from a higher court in the same country.

Jurisdiction. Jurisdiction is the authority of a court, or of a country's laws, over a place, a person, or a type of case. Example: a court in one country usually has no jurisdiction over events in another country.

Injunction. An injunction is a court order telling a person to do something, or to stop doing something. Example: a court may grant an injunction stopping a company from using a disputed name.

Statute of limitations. A statute of limitations is a law that sets a time limit for bringing a legal claim. Example: a claim for a debt may have to be started within a set number of years, or it may be too late.

How to read a legal document

When you meet a legal document, read it slowly and in order. Start with the names of the people or businesses involved, then the dates, then the main promises. Mark any word you do not understand, look it up, and read the sentence again with the plain meaning in mind. Long sentences are common in legal writing: break them into parts, find the subject and the verb, and ask what each part is doing.

Pay special attention to words like "shall" and "must", which state a duty, and "may", which states a permission. Watch for the small print about money, dates, and what happens if something goes wrong — those parts matter most in practice. If a clause says that one side gives up a right, or agrees to pay the other side's costs, make sure you understand it before you sign.

Never sign a document you do not understand, and never rely on a verbal promise that is not written in the document. If the document matters — a contract, a lease, a court paper, a will — have a qualified lawyer read it with you. A short conversation with a lawyer can save a long dispute later, and it is the only way to get advice that fits your own situation.

Questions people ask

What does liability mean?

Liability means being legally responsible for something, usually for a loss, a debt, or harm done to someone. For example, if a shop's wet floor causes a fall, the shop may have liability for the injury.

What is the difference between arbitration and mediation?

In arbitration, an independent arbitrator hears both sides and makes a decision that the sides have agreed to accept. In mediation, an independent mediator helps both sides reach their own agreement but does not decide the case.

What is a breach of contract?

A breach is a failure to do what a contract requires. For example, if a builder promises to finish work by June and does not, that is a breach of the contract, and the other side may have a claim.

What does indemnity mean?

Indemnity is a promise to pay for someone else's loss or costs if a stated thing happens. For example, a contractor's indemnity clause may promise to pay the client's legal costs if the contractor's work causes a claim.

What is a statute of limitations?

A statute of limitations is a law that sets a time limit for bringing a legal claim. The time limit depends on the kind of claim and the country, so check the rule that applies to your case.

Do I need a lawyer to understand a contract?
