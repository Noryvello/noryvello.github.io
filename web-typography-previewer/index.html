What typography is, and why it matters

Typography is the art of arranging type so that words are easy to read and pleasant to look at. On a website it means the fonts you choose, the sizes you set, the space between lines, and the way headings relate to body text. Visitors rarely think about typography directly, but they feel it at once: a page with clear, comfortable type feels trustworthy, while a page with tiny grey text on a white background feels hard work, and most readers leave before they finish the first paragraph.

Good typography does three jobs at the same time. First, it makes reading effortless, so visitors stay longer and understand more. Second, it builds a visual hierarchy, so readers can scan the page and find what matters: the headline first, then the subheadings, then the body text. Third, it sets a mood. A formal serif font feels serious and traditional; a clean sans-serif font feels modern and friendly. The words stay the same, but the feeling changes completely.

The 4 typography rules

Four rules cover most of what a small site needs. Learn them once and almost every page you make will look better.

  1. Readable body size. Body text should be at least 16 pixels on the web. Smaller text forces readers to zoom or squint, and on a phone that means they leave. Larger text, up to 18 or 20 pixels, is comfortable for long articles.
  2. Clear hierarchy. Headings should be clearly bigger and bolder than body text, and each level should be visibly different from the next. If a reader cannot tell a heading from a paragraph at a glance, the hierarchy is too weak.
  3. Enough line height. Lines that sit too close together blur into a grey block. A line height of 1.5 to 1.8 times the font size gives each line room to breathe and makes it easier for the eye to find the start of the next line.
  4. Two fonts maximum. One font for headings and one for body text is enough for almost every site. A third font adds clutter, and a fourth makes the page look like a ransom note. If you want variety, use weight and size within the same family instead.

These rules are starting points, not laws. A children's site can be bolder, a legal site can be more formal, and a fashion site can break a rule on purpose. But a site that breaks all four rules at once is almost always hard to read.

Worked examples: a blog, a shop and a formal site

A blog. A food blogger writes long recipes and stories. Her readers stay for minutes at a time, mostly on phones. She picks Georgia for headings and Arial for body text, sets the body size to 17 pixels and the line height to 1.7, and keeps letter spacing at zero. A shop. A small online shop sells handmade soap. Its pages are short: a product name, a price, a few lines of description, and a button. The owner picks Arial for both headings and body, sets the body size to 16 pixels, the heading size to 28 pixels, and adds half a pixel of letter spacing to the headings. One font family keeps the page fast and consistent, and the spaced headings give the shop a clean, modern feel without any decoration.

A formal site. A community association publishes meeting notes and reports. Its readers expect a serious, trustworthy look. The editor picks Times New Roman for both headings and body, sets the body size to 17 pixels and the line height to 1.8. The classic serif feels official, and the airy lines make long documents less tiring. Nothing on the page shouts; everything is calm and readable.

Notice that all three examples use at most two fonts, a body size of 16 pixels or more, and a line height of at least 1.6. The rules stay the same; only the mood changes.

Common mistakes

  • Tiny body text. Twelve or thirteen pixels may look elegant on a large desktop monitor, but on a phone it is unreadable. If your readers have to pinch to zoom, your body text is too small.
  • All-caps paragraphs. Capital letters are fine for a short heading, but a whole paragraph in capitals is slow to read because every word has the same shape. Use capitals sparingly, and never for body text.
  • Four fonts at once. A different font for the logo, the headings, the body and the buttons makes a page look patched together. Two fonts, used consistently, look far more professional.
  • Grey text on a white background. Light grey text looks subtle in a design tool and invisible in sunlight. Body text should be dark — near-black is safest — with strong contrast against the background.
  • Lines that are too long. A line of text that stretches across a wide screen is hard to read because the eye loses its place on the way back. Aim for lines of about 50 to 75 characters, and keep your content column narrow.
  • Ignoring the phone. Most visitors read on a phone, where the screen is narrow and the light is often poor. Always check your typography at phone width before you publish.

How to pick a pairing that works

Start with the body font, because readers spend most of their time with body text. Pick something plain and highly readable: a clean sans-serif such as Arial or Verdana, or a classic serif such as Georgia. Then pick a heading font that contrasts with it. Contrast is the key: a serif heading over a sans-serif body, or a bold sans-serif heading over a serif body, both work well. Two fonts that are too similar look like a mistake rather than a choice.

Test your pairing with real words, not with placeholder text. Headlines should be short and punchy; body text should be a real paragraph from your site. Read the sample out loud, scroll it on your phone, and show it to someone else. If they say the page looks clear and easy, the pairing works. If they say it looks fancy or unusual, it may be working against you.

Keep a note of the settings you choose. Typography is a system: once you pick a body size, a line height and a pair of fonts, use them the same way on every page. Consistency is what makes a site feel designed, even when the design itself is simple.

Questions people ask

What font size should body text be?

Body text should be at least 16 pixels on the web, and 17 or 18 pixels is comfortable for long reading on a phone. Smaller text forces readers to zoom or squint, and most of them leave instead.

How many fonts should a page use?

Two fonts at most: one for headings and one for body text. A third font adds clutter, and a fourth makes the page look patched together. Use weight and size for variety instead of extra fonts.

What is line height?

Line height is the vertical space between lines of text, measured as a multiple of the font size. A line height of 1.6 means each line gets 60 percent extra space, which makes long text much easier to read.

Why does text look different on phone?

Phones have narrower screens, different default fonts and different screen sizes, so the same page reflows and can look quite different. Always check your typography at phone width, not only on a desktop monitor.

Serif or sans-serif for body text?

Both can work. Sans-serif fonts such as Arial are usually easier on screens, especially small ones, while serif fonts such as Georgia suit long formal reading. Pick the one that fits your site and test it on a phone.

How do I pick a heading font?

Pick a heading font that contrasts with your body font: a serif heading over a sans-serif body, or a bold sans-serif heading over a serif body. Keep it clearly larger and bolder than the body text.
