Cricket World Cup 2027 Tickets Guide

By the Noryvello team · Published 19 November 2026 · Updated 19 November 2026

Tickets for the Cricket World Cup 2027 are sold through the ICC's official ballot and sale, and demand will be high for the biggest matches. This guide explains how the system works in general terms and how to plan a trip around the tournament.

The Cricket World Cup 2027 runs from 2 October to 21 November 2027 across South Africa, Zimbabwe and Namibia, with 14 teams playing 57 matches at 12 venues. It is the first World Cup in Africa since 2003, and the first hosted by three nations, so demand for tickets will be strong, especially for the host nations' matches, the semi-finals and the final.

This guide explains how ICC ticketing works, how to plan around the venues, and how to avoid scams. It never states ticket prices, because prices change: check the ICC's official ticketing pages for current prices and sale windows.

How ICC ticketing works

Tickets for the Cricket World Cup are sold through the ICC's official ballot and sale. The system generally works in stages. First, fans register their interest through the official ticketing channels. For high-demand matches, a ballot is held: fans apply for tickets, and if demand exceeds supply, tickets are allocated by ballot. After the ballot, a general sale usually follows for the remaining matches and seats.

Hospitality packages are also usually available through official channels, offering premium seats with food and drink included. The details, including which matches go to ballot and which go to general sale, are confirmed by the ICC for each tournament, so check the official ticketing pages rather than relying on how it worked at a previous World Cup.

The sale windows: what to check

Sale windows are announced by the ICC and can change, so the only reliable source is the ICC's official ticketing pages. Sign up for official alerts and check the pages regularly, because ballot deadlines are easy to miss and they do not move. If you are planning to attend, register your interest early, even before you have decided which matches to target.

Be ready when a sale opens. Have your payment details ready, know which matches and how many tickets you want, and be flexible about seating areas. High-demand matches can sell out quickly, and the fans who get tickets are usually the ones who were prepared. Keep your plans flexible until your tickets are confirmed.

Which matches to target

The most sought-after matches are the final on 21 November 2027, the two semi-finals in Cape Town on 17 November and Centurion on 18 November, the opening matches, and the host nations' games. These are the matches most likely to go to ballot and the hardest to get. If you want to attend one of them, apply through the ballot and have a backup plan.

Group-stage matches offer much better availability and are often the best experience for travelling fans: the atmosphere is lively, the tickets are easier to get, and you can see several teams in a short trip. The Super 7 matches, 21 matches between the seven surviving teams, offer high stakes with better availability than the knockouts. A mix of group matches and one big match is often the best plan.

Planning around the 12 venues

The tournament is played at 12 venues: eight in South Africa (Johannesburg, Tshwane and Centurion, Cape Town, Durban, Gqeberha, Bloemfontein, Paarl, and KuGompo City, formerly East London), three in Zimbabwe (Harare, Bulawayo and Victoria Falls), and one in Namibia (Windhoek). Distances between venues can be large, so plan your travel around clusters of matches rather than trying to cover the whole tournament.

Book accommodation early, especially in the smaller host cities, where the number of rooms is limited. Victoria Falls, with its new international cricket stadium close to one of the great natural sights of the world, is likely to be especially popular. Arrive a day before your match if you can, so that travel delays do not cost you the game.

Travelling between three countries

Attending matches in all three host countries means crossing borders, and that needs planning. Check the entry requirements for South Africa, Zimbabwe and Namibia for your nationality well in advance, including whether you need a visa and what documents to carry. Requirements differ by nationality, and they can change, so check official government sources rather than relying on what worked for someone else.

Check travel advice for each country, and make sure your travel insurance covers the whole trip. Keep copies of your tickets, your passport and your bookings, and leave enough time between matches in different countries for border crossings and flights. A little planning here saves a great deal of stress later.

Avoiding scams and fake tickets

Major tournaments attract ticket scams, and the best defence is simple: buy only from the ICC's official ticketing channels. Be wary of anyone offering tickets on social media, on unofficial resale sites, or at prices that seem too good to be true. Fake tickets are a real problem at World Cups, and a fake ticket will not get you into the ground.

If a match you want is sold out, check the ICC's official channels for any official resale or ticket exchange options. Avoid unofficial resellers, however convincing they look, and never pay by bank transfer to someone you do not know. If an offer feels wrong, it almost certainly is.

Tips for getting seats

Be ready when sales open, be flexible about which matches and seats you accept, and consider the less popular matches, which are often the most enjoyable. Weekday group matches, matches between lower-profile teams, and matches in smaller venues are all easier to get tickets for, and the cricket is just as good.

If you miss out on a big match, do not give up: further sale windows sometimes open, and official channels are the place to hear about them first. And remember that the World Cup is seven weeks long, with 57 matches. There are many ways to be part of it, and the fans who plan early and stay flexible are the ones who get the best of it.

Getting to the match on the day

Match day starts long before the first ball. Arrive early, because the roads and public transport around a stadium fill up quickly on World Cup days, and security checks take time. Check the venue's entry rules before you travel: stadiums have their own rules about what you can bring in, including bags, food, drink and cameras, and those rules differ from venue to venue. Bring your ticket and your identification, and keep them somewhere safe.

Plan for the weather and the length of the day. A 50-over match lasts most of the day, so bring sun protection, a hat and water if the venue allows it, and dress for the conditions. Plan your way home before the match starts, because transport after a big match is always busier than before it. If you are staying overnight, book accommodation near the venue or near reliable transport. A little match-day planning turns a long day into an enjoyable one.

If you are attending with children or elderly relatives, agree a meeting point inside the ground in case anyone gets separated, and keep a phone charged. Large stadiums are easy to get lost in, and mobile networks can be slow when tens of thousands of people are using them at once. A simple plan agreed in advance saves worry on the day.
