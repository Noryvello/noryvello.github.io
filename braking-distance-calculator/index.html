Braking Distance Calculator

Enter your speed and the road condition, and this calculator gives you the thinking distance, the braking distance and the total stopping distance in metres, with the working shown. It is an estimate for general education, built on typical reaction times and grip values.

All distances are in metres. The calculator uses a typical reaction time of 0.7 seconds and typical grip values — 0.7 for dry, 0.4 for wet, 0.1 for icy roads.

This is an estimate for general education, not an official figure. Real stopping distances vary by vehicle, tires, brakes, driver and road surface, and the reaction time and grip values used here are typical values, not guarantees. Never drive faster than the conditions allow.

How the maths works

Stopping distance has two parts, and both come from your speed. First the speed is changed from km/h to metres per second: speed in m/s = km/h ÷ 3.6.

  • Thinking distance: the distance travelled while you react, before the brakes even start working. Thinking distance = speed in m/s × 0.7, using a typical reaction time of 0.7 seconds.
  • Braking distance: the distance travelled while the brakes bring the vehicle to a stop. Braking distance = speed in m/s² ÷ (2 × μ × 9.81), where μ is the grip between tire and road: 0.7 for dry, 0.4 for wet, 0.1 for icy. These are typical values, not official figures.
  • Total stopping distance: thinking distance + braking distance.

Because the braking formula squares the speed, braking distance grows with the square of speed — double the speed and the braking distance becomes four times as long.

Worked examples

Example 1 — 50 km/h, dry road. Speed: 50 ÷ 3.6 = 13.89 m/s. Thinking distance: (50 ÷ 3.6) × 0.7 = 9.72 m. Braking distance: (50 ÷ 3.6)² ÷ (2 × 0.7 × 9.81) = 14.05 m. Total: thinking + braking = 23.77 m.

Example 2 — 50 km/h, wet road. Speed: 50 ÷ 3.6 = 13.89 m/s. Thinking distance: (50 ÷ 3.6) × 0.7 = 9.72 m. Braking distance: (50 ÷ 3.6)² ÷ (2 × 0.4 × 9.81) = 24.58 m. Total: thinking + braking = 34.30 m.

Example 3 — 100 km/h, dry road. Speed: 100 ÷ 3.6 = 27.78 m/s. Thinking distance: (100 ÷ 3.6) × 0.7 = 19.44 m. Braking distance: (100 ÷ 3.6)² ÷ (2 × 0.7 × 9.81) = 56.18 m. Total: thinking + braking = 75.63 m.

Why speed matters so much

Compare Example 1 and Example 3: doubling the speed from 50 km/h to 100 km/h doubles the thinking distance (9.72 m to 19.44 m) but makes the braking distance about four times as long (14.05 m to 56.18 m). The total stopping distance jumps from 23.77 m to 75.63 m — more than three times as far.

The road matters just as much as the speed. At the same 50 km/h, a wet road stretches the braking distance from 14.05 m to 24.58 m, and an icy road stretches it to 98.32 m — about 7 times the dry-road figure. That is why slowing down early, and leaving a much bigger gap in rain or ice, makes such a difference.

Questions people ask

What is thinking distance?

Thinking distance is how far your vehicle travels between spotting a hazard and actually pressing the brake. This calculator uses a typical reaction time of 0.7 seconds, so at 50 km/h the thinking distance is 9.72 metres. Real reaction times vary from person to person and moment to moment.

Why does braking distance grow with the square of speed?

A moving vehicle carries energy that grows with the square of its speed. Stopping means using the brakes to spend all that energy, so the distance needed grows with speed squared too. Double the speed and the braking distance becomes four times as long: 14.05 metres at 50 km/h on a dry road becomes 56.18 metres at 100 km/h.

How much difference does a wet road make?

A lot. On a wet road the grip between tire and road is weaker, so the braking distance at 50 km/h is 24.58 metres instead of 14.05 metres on a dry road — about 1.75 times as long. On ice it is far worse: about 7 times the dry-road braking distance. Slow down well before you need to stop.

Are these official figures?

No. The reaction time of 0.7 seconds and the grip values of 0.7 for dry, 0.4 for wet and 0.1 for icy roads are typical values used for a general estimate, not official figures. Real stopping distances vary by vehicle, tires, brakes, driver and road, so treat the results as a guide for learning, not as a guarantee.
