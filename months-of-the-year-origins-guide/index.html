Month Name Origins Guide

The names of the months come from the Roman calendar, and they are a mix of gods, festivals, numbers and emperors. January honours the god Janus, March honours Mars, July and August are named after Julius Caesar and Augustus, and September to December still carry the numbers seven to ten from an older year that began in March.

Say the months out loud and you are speaking Latin, most of it two thousand years old. January, March, May and June are named after gods. February is named after a festival. September, October, November and December are simply the numbers seven, eight, nine and ten. July and August are named after two Roman rulers who were powerful enough to put their names on the calendar.

The names have survived every calendar reform since Rome. They survived the Julian reform, the Gregorian reform, and the spread of the calendar across the world. Pick any month below to see where its name comes from, or read the full story of how the Roman year gave us the names we still use.

The old Roman year began in March

The key to the month names is that the Roman year did not always begin in January. According to Roman tradition, the earliest Roman calendar had ten months, running from March to December, and the year began in March. The winter stretch after December had no months at all. Later, two new months were added to cover the winter gap: January at the start and February at the end.

That change is why four of our month names are numbered wrong. September means seven, October means eight, November means nine and December means ten, because in the old ten-month year they were the seventh, eighth, ninth and tenth months. When January and February were added at the front of the year, all the numbered months shifted two places, but their names never changed.

The year now begins in January because January is named after Janus, the god of beginnings, which made it the natural month to open the year. That choice was fixed by Julius Caesar's reform in 45 BC, and it has stayed ever since.

The gods and the festivals

Six of the months are named after gods or festivals, and they tell you what mattered to the Romans. March honours Mars, the god of war, and it is no accident that the war god's month opened the year: spring was when armies marched out after winter. May honours Maia, a goddess of growth, and June honours Juno, the queen of the gods. June's link to Juno, the protector of marriage, is one reason June became a traditional month for weddings.

January honours Janus, the two-faced god of doors, gates and beginnings, who looks both forward and backward. It is a perfect name for the first month, which looks back on the old year and forward to the new one. February is named after Februa, a festival of purification, a month for cleansing and for rites for the dead before the year began again in March.

April is the one name scholars still argue about. The most widely accepted account links it to the Latin word aperire, meaning to open, for the buds and flowers opening in spring. Some Roman writers instead linked April to Aphrodite, the goddess of love. The opening explanation is the one most often given today, but the argument is a reminder that even the Romans were not always sure where their own words came from.

The two emperors

July and August are the only months named after people, and both of them were renamed for rulers. July was originally Quintilis, the fifth month, and it was renamed for Julius Caesar after his death, because he was born in that month and because his calendar reform had reshaped the year. August was originally Sextilis, the sixth month, and it was renamed for Augustus, the first Roman emperor.

A popular story says that Augustus took a day from February to make August as long as July, so that his month would not be shorter than Caesar's. The surviving calendar evidence does not support that story: August already had 31 days in the calendar Caesar had set out. The tale is a good one, but the days were already there.

Other emperors tried to get months named after them too, and their names did not stick. July and August survived because Caesar and Augustus were the two men who remade Rome, and their names were already woven into the calendar's story.

The numbered months

September, October, November and December are the plainest names on the calendar, and they are also the most confusing, because their numbers are wrong by two. September is the ninth month but its name means seven. October is the tenth but means eight. November is the eleventh but means nine. December is the twelfth but means ten.

The numbers are not wrong, though. They are a record of the old Roman year, in which March was first and December was tenth. The names kept their original numbers when the year was rearranged, and nobody ever bothered to rename them. It is a small piece of history that millions of people repeat every day without noticing.

English keeps the Latin numbers in other words too. An octopus has eight arms, a nonagon has nine sides, and a decade has ten years. The same Latin roots sit inside the month names, hiding in plain sight.

Why the names never changed

The calendar has been reformed more than once, and each reform could have renamed the months. Nobody did, because names that everyone already knows are worth keeping. A new name for September would have confused every farmer, priest and tax collector in the empire, and it would have fixed nothing.

So the names stayed, and they travelled. As the Roman calendar spread across Europe and then the world, the Latin names went with it, changing shape in each language but keeping their roots. The months we use today are the same names Romans used, and the story of the old year that began in March is still written into them.

Some month origins are debated by scholars, especially April, where the link to the Latin word for opening is the most widely accepted account but not the only one. The origins given here follow the accounts most often taught, and the early history of the Roman calendar is partly tradition rather than certain record.

Questions people ask

Why is September called the seventh month when it is the ninth?

September comes from the Latin septem, meaning seven, because it was the seventh month of the old Roman year, which began in March. When January and February were added, the month moved to ninth place but kept its name.

Which months are named after people?

July is named after Julius Caesar and August after Augustus, the first Roman emperor. They were originally called Quintilis and Sextilis, meaning fifth and sixth.

What does February mean?

February is named after Februa, a Roman festival of purification held in that month, a time for cleansing and rites for the dead before the new year began in March.

Why does the year begin in January?

January is named after Janus, the god of beginnings, who looks both forward and backward. Julius Caesar's calendar reform in 45 BC fixed the start of the year on the 1st of January.

Is it true Augustus stole a day from February?

Probably not. The popular story says Augustus took a day from February to make August as long as July, but the surviving calendar evidence shows August already had 31 days in the calendar Caesar had set out.
