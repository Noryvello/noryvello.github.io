Music Scales Explained

A scale is a set of notes in order, and it is the raw material of melody. Every scale has a pattern of whole steps and half steps, and once you know the pattern you can build the scale from any starting note.

Every tune you have ever heard is built from a scale — a small set of notes in a fixed order. Scales are the reason some notes sound right together and others sound wrong, and they are the first thing any musician learns, whether they play piano, guitar, violin or sing.

This guide explains what a scale is, how whole steps and half steps build major and minor scales, and why pentatonic scales are so popular. Use the picker below to see any scale's notes, its interval pattern, where it sits on a keyboard, and a note on playing it on piano and guitar.

Note names in this guide use the standard letter system with sharps and flats — if you read sheet music, the key signature tells you which notes are sharp or flat.

What a scale is

A scale is a ladder of notes that runs from one note up to the same note an octave higher. The octave is the point where a note repeats at twice the pitch — a high C sounds like a low C, only higher. Between the two, the scale picks out a set of notes in a fixed order, and that set is what gives a piece of music its character.

Western music names notes with the letters A to G. After G the letters start again at A, so the full run of note names is A, B, C, D, E, F, G, A, B, C and so on. The black keys on a piano are the notes in between, called sharps (one step up) and flats (one step down). F sharp and G flat are the same black key, described from different directions.

A scale is defined by its pattern of steps, not by the note it starts on. The same pattern starting on C gives C major, and starting on G gives G major. Learn the pattern once and you can build the scale from any note.

Whole steps and half steps

The smallest step in Western music is a half step, also called a semitone: the distance from one piano key to the very next key, white or black. A whole step, or tone, is two half steps — the distance from C to D, for example, skipping the black key in between.

On a piano, most white keys are a whole step apart, but E to F and B to C are only half steps apart, because there is no black key between them. That quirk is why the pattern of a scale matters: the pattern tells you exactly where the half steps fall, and those half steps are what make a scale sound major or minor.

On a guitar, a half step is one fret and a whole step is two frets. The same pattern works on both instruments, which is why musicians can talk about scales without naming an instrument at all.

Major scales

The major scale is the brightest and most familiar scale, and its pattern is: whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half. Start on C and follow the pattern and you get C major — C, D, E, F, G, A, B, C — which uses only white keys on a piano and has no sharps or flats. That is why C major is usually the first scale beginners learn.

Start the same pattern on G and you get G major, which needs one sharp (F sharp) to keep the pattern correct. Start on D and you need two sharps (F sharp and C sharp). Start on F and you need one flat (B flat). The pattern never changes; only the starting note and the sharps and flats change.

Major scales sound happy or bright to most listeners, which is why they are common in pop songs, anthems and children's tunes. The feeling comes from the pattern itself — particularly the half steps falling between the third and fourth notes and between the seventh and eighth.

Minor scales

The natural minor scale uses a different pattern: whole, half, whole, whole, half, whole, whole. Start on A and you get A natural minor — A, B, C, D, E, F, G, A — which, like C major, uses only white keys. A minor and C major share the same notes; what changes is which note the music treats as home.

Minor scales sound sadder or more serious to most listeners, and they are common in ballads, film music and folk songs. The difference between major and minor comes down to the third note: in a major scale the third note is two whole steps above the starting note, while in a minor scale it is only one and a half steps above.

There are other forms of minor scale that musicians use for particular effects, but the natural minor is the one to learn first. If you know C major and A natural minor, you already know the two most important scales in Western music.

Pentatonic scales

Pentatonic means five notes, and pentatonic scales use only five of the seven notes of a major or minor scale. The major pentatonic leaves out the fourth and seventh notes, and the minor pentatonic leaves out the second and sixth. What is left is a scale with no awkward clashes: almost any combination of its notes sounds good together.

That is why pentatonic scales are everywhere — in folk songs around the world, in rock and blues solos, and in the first tunes children learn. If you want to try making up a melody, a pentatonic scale is the safest place to start, because there are no wrong notes to hit.

The keyboard layout

A piano keyboard looks complicated, but it is built from a repeating pattern. The black keys come in groups of two and three, and that pattern repeats all the way along the keyboard. C is the white key just to the left of each group of two black keys, so you can find any C by looking for the twos.

Each white key is one letter name, and the black keys take their names from their neighbours: the black key between C and D is C sharp (up from C) or D flat (down from D). Once you can find C, you can find every other note by counting along the alphabet.

Playing along on piano and guitar

On the piano, play scales slowly with one hand first, one note at a time, listening to each note as you go. Speed comes later; clean, even notes matter far more than fast ones. When the right hand is comfortable, try the left hand, then both hands together.

On the guitar, a scale is a path across the strings. Play one note per fret where you can, and use one finger per fret to keep your hand steady. Start on the lowest string and work your way up, then come back down the same way. A slow, steady scale played cleanly is worth more than a fast, messy one.

Whichever instrument you play, the best practice is short and regular: a few minutes every day beats one long session a week. Play the scale up and down, then try making up a short tune using only the notes of that scale.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between a major and a minor scale?

The two scales use different patterns of whole and half steps. A major scale follows whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half, while a natural minor scale follows whole, half, whole, whole, half, whole, whole, which gives it a sadder sound.

How many notes are in a pentatonic scale?

A pentatonic scale has five notes. The major pentatonic leaves out the fourth and seventh notes of the major scale, and the minor pentatonic leaves out the second and sixth notes of the minor scale.

What does WWHWWWH mean?

It is shorthand for the major scale pattern: whole, whole, half, whole, whole, whole, half. Each letter is the step from one note to the next, and the pattern works from any starting note.

Which scale should a beginner learn first?

C major is the usual first scale, because it uses only the white keys on a piano and has no sharps or flats. A natural minor is the natural second step, since it uses the same notes.

Do I need to read music to use scales?

No. You can learn scales by note names and by ear. Reading music helps later, but the picker on this page gives you every scale's notes in plain letters.
