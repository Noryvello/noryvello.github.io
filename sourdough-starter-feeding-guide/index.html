Sourdough Starter Feeding Guide

This guide builds a 7-day feeding plan for your sourdough starter. Pick a start date and a feeding rhythm, and it shows each day's discard and feed amounts by weight. Signs of readiness and troubleshooting notes are included below.

Build your 7-day plan

This guide covers feeding a starter with flour and water only. It is general baking guidance, not food-safety advice. If your starter smells strongly unpleasant or shows mould, throw it away and start fresh.

How the feeding ratio works

This plan uses a 1:1:1 feeding ratio, which means equal weights of starter, flour and water. At each feeding you keep a set weight of starter, then add the same weight of flour and the same weight of water. If you keep 50 grams of starter, you feed it 50 grams of flour and 50 grams of water. Weighing keeps the ratio exact, which measuring by volume cannot do, because flour and water weigh different amounts per cup.

Before each feeding, the starter in the jar weighs three times the amount you keep, because it holds the kept starter plus the last feed of flour and water. With a 50 gram keep, the jar holds 150 grams before feeding. You discard 100 grams, keep 50 grams, and feed it 50 grams of flour and 50 grams of water. Discarding first is what keeps the starter from growing endlessly and keeps the ratio steady.

Feeding twice a day suits a new starter or a warm kitchen, where the starter eats through its food faster. Feeding once a day suits a mature starter in a cooler kitchen. Pick the rhythm that fits your starter and your day, and stick with it for the week so you can see how the starter responds.

Signs your starter is ready

A ready starter rises well after feeding. Mark the level of the starter on the jar just after feeding, and check it a few hours later. A healthy starter climbs well above the mark, often close to doubling, then slowly falls back as it runs out of food. The rise is the clearest sign that the starter is active enough to raise bread.

Look for bubbles through the jar and on the surface. A ready starter is full of small and large bubbles, which show that gas is being made inside. The texture should be light and airy rather than flat and dense. Smell it too: a ready starter smells pleasantly tangy, like yoghurt or green apples, not sharply sour or unpleasant.

The float test is a useful extra check. Drop a small spoonful of starter into a glass of water. If it floats, it is full of gas and ready to use. If it sinks, give it more time or another feeding and test again. Use the float test alongside the rise and the bubbles rather than on its own.

Troubleshooting notes

If your starter is not rising, check the warmth of the room first. A cold kitchen slows everything down, and a starter in a cool spot may need longer between feeds or a warmer place to sit. Move it somewhere a little warmer, keep feeding on schedule, and give it a few more days before you change anything else.

A layer of liquid on top, sometimes called hooch, means the starter is hungry. It is common and not a sign of failure. Stir the liquid back in and feed the starter, or pour it off if you prefer a milder flavour. If hooch appears often, feed more often or keep less starter at each feeding.

A strong unpleasant smell, such as nail polish remover, usually means the starter has gone too long without food. Feed it on schedule for a few days and the smell should soften as the starter recovers. If you see mould, which looks like fuzzy coloured spots, throw the starter away and start fresh rather than trying to save it.

Looking after your starter between bakes

If you bake often, keep the starter at room temperature and feed it on schedule. If you bake once a week or less, keep it in the fridge, where the cold slows it down and it needs feeding less often. A fridge starter still needs regular feeding, just less often than a room-temperature one.

Before baking with a fridge starter, bring it back to room temperature and feed it. Let it rise and check the signs of readiness before you mix your dough. A starter straight from the fridge is sleepy and will not raise bread well. One or two feeds at room temperature usually wake it up.

Keep notes on your starter. Write down the feeding ratio, the flour you use and how the starter behaves. Notes turn guesswork into knowledge, and they help you spot patterns, such as a starter that always peaks a few hours after feeding. Over time you will learn your starter's rhythm, and feeding it will become a simple habit.

Questions people ask

What does 1:1:1 feeding mean?

It means equal weights of starter, flour and water. If you keep 50 grams of starter, you feed it 50 grams of flour and 50 grams of water.

What should I do with the discard?

Discard is the starter you remove before feeding. You can throw it away or use it in discard recipes such as pancakes or crackers.

How do I know my starter is ready to bake with?

A ready starter rises well after feeding, is full of bubbles and smells pleasantly tangy. The signs of readiness section below lists what to look for.

What is the liquid on top of my starter?

A layer of liquid on top is common when a starter is hungry. Stir it back in and feed the starter, or pour it off if you prefer a milder flavour.

Can I keep my starter in the fridge?

Yes. A fridge slows the starter down, so it needs feeding less often. Bring it back to room temperature and feed it before you bake with it.
