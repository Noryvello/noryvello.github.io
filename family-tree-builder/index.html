What a family tree is (and what it is not)

A family tree is a map of the people you come from. At its simplest it is a list: you, your parents, your grandparents, and the people around them. It grows one name at a time, and it does not need to be complete to be useful. A tree with twelve names that you know well is worth more than a tree with two hundred names copied from somewhere else.

A family tree is not a legal document and not a test. There is no rule that says you must go back five generations, and no shame in a tree that stops at your grandparents. The point is to keep the stories and the connections that would otherwise fade. Every family has someone who remembers things nobody else does, and a tree is where those memories get written down before they are lost.

This builder keeps things simple on purpose. You add one person at a time — a name, a relation, and any dates you know — and the builder draws a simple diagram grouped by generation. There are no accounts, no uploads, and no requests for private details. Your tree lives in your browser, and you decide who sees it.

How to use this builder

Start with yourself. Add your name, pick Me as the relation, and add your year of birth if you like. Then add your parents, then your grandparents. Work outwards one ring at a time: siblings, spouse or partner, children, and other relatives as you remember them. Dates are optional — a name with no dates is still a branch on the tree.

Each person appears in a group for their generation: grandparents' generation at the top, then parents, then your generation, then children. You can remove anyone with the Remove button. When you are happy with the tree, copy it as text to share with a relative, or export it as a text file to keep on your device.

The tree saves in this browser automatically, so you can add a few names today and a few more next week. But a browser is not a safe place for the only copy of anything. Export the tree after each session, and keep the file somewhere you back up. A family tree is the kind of thing you will be glad you kept.

Start with the oldest relative

The best first step in any family tree is a conversation, not a website. Find the oldest relative you can reach — a grandparent, a great-aunt, an old family friend — and ask them about the family. Ask about names, places, jobs, and the stories behind them. Record the conversation if they are happy for you to, or take notes while you talk.

Ask open questions, not yes-or-no ones. What was your grandmother like? is a better question than Was your grandmother nice? Ask about places: where did the family live, where did they move from, what was the old house like? Ask about work: what did people do for a living, and how did that change over time? The answers are often surprising, and they are the material your tree is made of.

Do not wait for a perfect moment. The oldest relatives are the ones whose memories reach furthest back, and they will not always be there to ask. A half-hour conversation this week is worth more than a perfect research plan next year. Bring a notebook, bring patience, and let them talk.

What to record — and what to leave out

For each person, the useful facts are few: full name, relation to you, year of birth, year of death if they have died, and a short note about their life. A note like Born in Dhaka, loved gardening is worth more than a list of dates, because it is the kind of thing a grandchild will want to read one day.

Leave out anything private about living people. This page never asks for addresses, phone numbers, identity numbers or any private detail, and you should not add them in the notes either. A family tree is meant to be shared, and anything you write in it might one day be read by someone you did not expect. Names and years are enough. If a detail feels private, leave it out.

Be careful with guesses. If you are not sure of a year, leave it blank rather than guessing. A blank space is honest and can be filled in later. A guessed date looks like a fact and can mislead the next person who reads the tree. When you do know something for certain, a short note about where you learned it — a birth certificate, a family story, an old letter — helps the next researcher.

How to handle missing information

Every family tree has gaps. A grandparent whose birth year nobody knows. A great-uncle who moved away and was never heard from again. A name that everyone remembers differently. Gaps are normal, and they are not a failure. They are simply the places where the trail goes cold.

When you hit a gap, try three things. First, ask another relative — different people remember different things, and a cousin may know what your parents do not. Second, look at the documents the family already has: old letters, wedding invitations, school certificates, and the backs of photographs often carry names and dates. Third, check public records if they exist in your country, such as birth and marriage registers.

If the gap stays, leave it blank and move on. A tree with honest gaps is better than a tree with invented answers. You can always come back to a gap later, and sometimes a gap fills itself when a new relative appears or an old document turns up. Patience is part of the work.

Keeping old photos safe

Old family photos are often the most precious thing a family owns, and the most fragile. Paper photographs fade in sunlight, stick together in damp air, and tear easily. The first rule is to handle them by the edges and keep them away from direct light. The second rule is to make a digital copy of every important photo before anything else.

To scan a photo, use a flatbed scanner if you have one, or a phone camera in bright, even light. Lay the photo flat, hold the phone steady and level, and take the picture straight down. Save the scan with a clear name — the person's name and the year, if you know it — and keep a copy in two places, such as your phone and a cloud backup or a second device.

Store the originals flat, in acid-free sleeves or envelopes if you can get them, in a cool dry place. Attics and basements are bad choices: too hot, too cold, too damp. A drawer in a lived-in room is better. And write on the back of each photo, lightly in pencil, who is in it and when it was taken. A photo with no names is a mystery that gets harder every year.

A worked example: a three-generation tree

Imagine a woman called Ayesha who wants to map her family. She starts with herself, then adds her parents: her mother Rahima, born 1968, and her father Karim, born 1965. Then she adds her grandparents: on her mother's side, her grandmother Fatima, born 1940, and her grandfather Anwar, born 1938 and died 2015. On her father's side, her grandmother Nasreen, born 1942, and her grandfather Jamal, born 1940.

She adds her younger brother Imran, born 1998, and her husband Daniel, born 1993. She adds her daughter Sara, born 2021. In the notes she writes one line about each person: Fatima ran a small shop; Anwar was a schoolteacher; Nasreen loved to sing. The tree now has nine people across three generations, and it took her less than an hour, most of it spent on the phone with her mother checking dates.

Notice what Ayesha did not do. She did not guess at dates she did not know. She did not add private details about anyone living. She did not try to go back further than her grandparents in the first session. Her tree is small, honest and complete as far as it goes — and it is ready to grow whenever she learns something new.

Questions people ask

How do I start a family tree?

Start with yourself, then add your parents, then your grandparents, working outwards one ring at a time. Talk to the oldest relative you can reach first, because their memories go back furthest. A small honest tree is better than a big guessed one.

Who should I ask first?

The oldest relative you can reach — a grandparent, a great-aunt, or an old family friend. Ask open questions about names, places, jobs and stories, and take notes or record the conversation if they are happy for you to.

What if I do not know some dates?

Leave them blank. A blank space is honest and can be filled in later, while a guessed date looks like a fact and can mislead the next person who reads the tree. Ask another relative or check old documents, and fill the gap when you know for certain.

How far back should I go?

Three or four generations is a good goal for a first tree: you, your parents, your grandparents, and great-grandparents if you can reach them. There is no rule that says you must go further, and a tree that stops at your grandparents is still a real family tree.

Where should I keep old family photos?

Scan every important photo first, and keep the digital copy in two places. Store the originals flat in a cool, dry room, away from sunlight, in sleeves or envelopes if you can. Write lightly in pencil on the back who is in the photo and when it was taken.

Can I share my tree with relatives?

Yes. Use the copy button to send the tree as text, or export it as a text file and share the file. Only share names and years, never private details about living people, and check with relatives before sharing anything about them.
