7 Mistakes to Avoid When Buying a Fridge

A fridge runs all day, every day, so a wrong choice is one you live with for years. This guide covers the 7 mistakes buyers most often make, from size and layout to ventilation and noise, and ends with a measurement and needs checklist.

A fridge is the hardest-working appliance in your home. It runs all day and all night, it holds the food your household depends on, and replacing it is a large, awkward job. The mistakes in this guide come from buying by the outside measurements or the price alone, instead of by how the fridge will live in your kitchen: how its doors open, how much air it gets, how loud it is at night and how its shelves suit what you actually store. Measure your space, think about your shopping and cooking habits, and work through the seven mistakes before you buy. The measurement and needs checklist at the end is worth completing before you spend anything.

This guide is general buying advice with no brand recommendations and no prices. It does not size a fridge for you — measure your space and check the energy label before you decide.

Mistake 1: Buying the wrong size for the household

Size is the first decision and the one you live with every time you open the door. A fridge that is too small means crowded shelves, food pushed to the back and forgotten, and a freezer that fills up before the week is out. A fridge that is too big wastes energy cooling empty space and takes up kitchen room you could use better. Think about your household size, how often you shop and how much fresh food you keep at once. A household that shops every day needs less space than one that does a weekly shop, and a household that cooks from scratch needs more fresh storage than one that eats out often.

Mistake 2: Not measuring the space and the delivery route

A fridge must fit two spaces: the gap it will stand in and the route it travels to get there. Measure the width, depth and height of the gap, and remember that the fridge's depth includes its handles and the space its doors need. Then measure every doorway, hallway and stair turn between the front door and the kitchen, because a fridge that fits the gap but cannot get through a narrow hallway is no use to anyone. Write the measurements down and take them with you. Guessing from memory in a shop is how fridges end up going back on the delivery van.

Mistake 3: Ignoring door swing and clearance

Fridge doors need room to open fully, and a door that only opens halfway makes the shelves behind it hard to reach. Before you buy, check what sits beside the fridge's final position: a wall, a cupboard, another appliance. Check which way the doors open and whether the hinges can be swapped to the other side, because that one detail can make the difference between a fridge that is easy to use and one that fights you. Also check the handles: they add to the depth, and they can catch on anything nearby. A fridge that opens freely is a fridge you will enjoy using.

Mistake 4: Choosing the wrong layout for your habits

Top freezer, bottom freezer and side-by-side layouts each suit different routines, and the right one depends on what you reach for most. If fresh food is what you use every day, a bottom freezer keeps it at eye level and saves you bending down for the milk. If you use the freezer often, a top freezer or a side-by-side puts it within easy reach. Think about the tallest and widest things you store, too: a wide shelf fits a large dish, while narrow shelves suit bottles and jars. Visit a shop and open the doors of each layout before you decide, because a layout that looks fine on paper can feel wrong in use.

Mistake 5: Forgetting ventilation space

A fridge gives off heat as it cools, and it needs air around it to do its job well. Pushed hard against a wall or squeezed into a tight gap, a fridge works harder, uses more energy and wears out sooner. Leave space around the fridge as the maker advises, especially at the back and on top, and never block the vents. If your fridge will sit in a fitted kitchen, check the ventilation requirements before you order, because a beautiful fitted look is no comfort if the fridge struggles. Good airflow is free, and it pays for itself in energy and lifespan.

Mistake 6: Overlooking noise

A fridge hums, clicks and occasionally gurgles, all day and all night. In a separate kitchen with the door shut, that hardly matters, but in an open-plan home or a small flat, a loud fridge is hard to ignore, especially at night when the house is quiet. Before you buy, find out how loud the fridge is: the energy label usually gives a noise rating, and a shop floor is a poor place to judge it because of the background noise. If your kitchen opens onto your living space, a quieter fridge is worth paying attention to, even if it costs a little more.

Mistake 7: Not checking the energy label

A fridge runs all day, every day, for years, so its running cost matters far more than for almost any other appliance. The energy label lets you compare fridges on how much electricity they use in a year, and the difference between the best and the worst adds up to a large sum over the fridge's life. A cheaper fridge with poor efficiency can cost more in the end than a dearer one that sips electricity. Look at the label before you look at the price, and remember that the running cost is a cost you pay every month for as long as the fridge lasts.

Before you buy

Once the checklist is done, compare two or three fridges on the energy label and the layout that suits your habits. Open the doors in the shop and picture your own food on the shelves, and check the drawers slide smoothly and the seals sit flat. Confirm the delivery, installation and removal terms in writing, and check the warranty and return policy. A fridge is a long-term buy, and an hour spent measuring and comparing is a small price for years of quiet, reliable service.

Questions people ask

What size fridge do I need?

It depends on household size, how often you shop and how much fresh food you keep. Measure your space first, then choose the largest size that fits comfortably without crowding the kitchen.

How much space should I leave around a fridge?

Fridges need air to circulate around them, especially at the back and top. Check the maker's guidance for your model and never push a fridge hard against a wall.

Which layout is best?

It depends on your habits. If you reach for fresh food most, a bottom freezer keeps it at eye level. If you use the freezer often, a top freezer or side-by-side may suit you better.

Does a fridge need to stand before switching on?

Many makers advise letting a new fridge stand upright for a while after delivery before switching it on. Follow the instructions that come with your fridge.

What should I check on delivery day?

Check for dents and scratches before the delivery team leaves, confirm the doors open fully in place, and keep the receipt and warranty details together.
