Negative Prompts Explained: What to Exclude for Better AI Images

By the Noryvello team · Published 20 November 2026 · Updated 20 November 2026

Most prompt guides tell you what to ask for; this one covers the other half — telling the generator what to leave out. A negative prompt is a short list of things you do not want in the picture, and used well it fixes the flaws that keep coming back.

Image tools change their menus and buttons often, so if a step below does not match what you see on screen, the tool's own help pages are the authority. Not every tool has a separate negative-prompt box — some need the exclusion written into the main prompt.

What a negative prompt is, in one paragraph

A negative prompt is a short list of the things you do not want in a picture. Where the main prompt says what to draw — a portrait, a landscape, a product shot — the negative prompt says what to leave out: blurry, distorted, extra fingers, watermark, text, low quality. It is the other half of the instruction, and for many pictures it matters as much as the main prompt itself.

Think of it as two hands on the same steering wheel. The main prompt pulls the picture toward what you want; the negative prompt pushes it away from the failures you have seen before. Without it, you are steering with one hand, and the model is free to drift into the same mistakes — soft focus, warped hands, garbled text — that it makes by habit.

A negative prompt is not a complaint and it is not a filter applied afterwards. It is part of the generation itself: the model takes the exclusions into account while it builds the picture, which is why it can prevent flaws rather than just hiding them. The rest of this guide shows you what to exclude, how to phrase it, and when it does not help.

Why telling AI what to avoid works

An image model works from patterns it learned by looking at millions of pictures. The main prompt tells it which patterns to pull toward — a face, a sunset, a coffee cup — and the negative prompt tells it which patterns to push away from. That push matters because the model's most common failures are also patterns: blurry images, distorted hands, and garbled text all appear in its training, and without an instruction to avoid them it can drift into them.

The negative prompt works best against flaws the model has seen often. Blurry, low quality, and distorted are strong exclusions because the model knows exactly what they look like. Extra fingers works because the model has seen many hands and many wrong hands, and the exclusion nudges it toward the right ones. The more common the flaw, the better the negative prompt handles it.

There is a second reason it works, and it is about how the model reads the main prompt. Writing do not draw text in the main prompt often fails, because the model still has to think about text to understand the sentence, and thinking about text can put text in the picture. A separate negative prompt gives the model a different place for the word avoid — a channel where text means keep it out, not draw it. That separation is why a negative prompt succeeds where a do not in the main prompt often does not.

It is a nudge, not a guarantee. A negative prompt makes a flaw less likely; it does not make it impossible. The sections below show you how to write one that nudges hard, and when to stop expecting it to do more than it can.

The words to exclude most often

Most negative prompts draw from a small shared list, because the flaws worth excluding are the ones that turn up again and again. Learning the list — and what each word actually excludes — is the fastest way to write a negative prompt that works.

  • Blurry, low quality. The two most common exclusions. They push the model toward sharp, clean results and away from soft focus and muddy detail.
  • Distorted, deformed. Covers warped shapes in general: faces that melt, bodies that bend wrong, objects that lose their form. Distorted is the broader word; deformed is stronger and suits people and animals.
  • Extra fingers, extra limbs. The classic hand and body failures. Extra fingers is the single most useful exclusion for portraits, and extra limbs helps with full-body pictures and animals.
  • Watermark, text, logo. Models sometimes add fake watermarks, garbled words, or invented logos, because they have seen them in training pictures. Excluding all three keeps the picture clean.
  • Bad anatomy, duplicate. Bad anatomy covers wrong proportions in people and animals. Duplicate stops the model from drawing the subject twice, which happens more often than you would expect.
  • Cropped, out of frame. Useful when the subject keeps getting cut off at the edge. These words ask the model to keep the whole subject inside the picture.

Pick from this list according to the flaw you actually see. A portrait that keeps producing bad hands needs extra fingers and bad anatomy; a landscape that keeps sprouting fake signs needs text and watermark. A negative prompt aimed at your real problem beats a long generic list every time.

How to write a negative prompt

A good negative prompt is short, specific, and plain. Write it as a comma-separated list of short phrases, one idea per phrase: blurry, distorted, extra fingers, watermark, text, low quality. Six items is a good starting length — enough to cover the common flaws, short enough that each one still carries weight.

Use plain words the model knows well. Blurry beats out of focus, and extra fingers beats an incorrect number of digits on the hands. The model learned from ordinary language, so ordinary words work better than clever ones. Avoid sentences: do not include any text in the image is weaker than the single word text, because the sentence gives the model more to misunderstand.

Add to the list only when you see the flaw. Start with four to six items, generate, and look at what went wrong. If the hands are the problem, add extra fingers. If a fake watermark appeared, add watermark. Each addition should answer a flaw you can point to in a result, not a flaw you imagine might happen.

Order does not matter much, and neither does capitalisation. What matters is that every item is something you genuinely want excluded, phrased as plainly as you can. A short list you understand beats a long list you copied, every time — and the section on common mistakes explains why the copied lists cause more trouble than they solve.

Tools with a negative-prompt box vs tools without one

Some image tools give you a separate box for the negative prompt, usually labelled negative prompt and sitting near the main prompt box or in the advanced settings. When that box exists, use it: it is the cleanest way to exclude things, because the model treats the two boxes differently, and the separation is what makes the negative prompt work as well as it does.

Other tools have no separate box at all. In those, the exclusion has to go into the main prompt, written as a without phrase at the end: a portrait of a woman in soft light, without text, without watermark. This works less cleanly than a separate box, because the model has to hold the exclusion and the subject in the same instruction — but it still helps, especially for text and watermark.

A third pattern is tools that accept negative words only through settings or style options rather than text at all. In those, look for quality or style controls that do the same job in a different form — a sharpness setting, a quality setting — and use the main prompt for the subject alone.

How to tell which kind your tool is: open the tool's help pages and search for negative prompt. The help is the authority here, because tools change their screens and features often, and a box that existed last month may have moved or vanished. If you cannot find one, the without phrase in the main prompt is your fallback, and it is better than nothing.

Before-and-after examples

Three examples, each showing the same main prompt with and without a negative prompt, and what changed. Results vary between generations, so treat these as typical outcomes, not guarantees — but the pattern is consistent enough to trust.

Example one: a portrait. The main prompt is:

Portrait of a woman in her forties, soft window light, 85mm lens, head-and-shoulders framing, gentle smile

Without a negative prompt, the hands at the edge of the frame came out warped, and the skin looked waxy. With the negative prompt blurry, distorted hands, extra fingers, plastic skin, the hands stayed out of trouble and the skin kept its texture. The change was in the flaws, not the subject — the face, the light, and the framing stayed the same.

Example two: a landscape. The main prompt is:

A quiet lake at dawn, mist over the water, pine trees on the far shore

Without a negative prompt, a fake wooden sign with garbled letters appeared on the shore — the model had seen signs in lakeside pictures and added one. With the negative prompt text, watermark, sign, the sign disappeared and the shore stayed clean. Text and watermark are the two words that fix this class of problem most reliably.

Example three: a product shot. The main prompt is:

A ceramic coffee mug on a plain table, soft morning light

Without a negative prompt, the table gathered clutter — a second mug, a spoon, a blurred newspaper. With the negative prompt cluttered background, duplicate, extra objects, the table stayed bare and the mug stood alone. The lesson is the same in all three: the negative prompt removes what you did not ask for, and leaves what you did alone.

When negative prompts don't help

A negative prompt cannot fix a bad main prompt. If the subject is vague, the light is undescribed, and the framing is missing, excluding blurry and distorted will not save the picture — it will only produce a sharper version of a confused idea. Fix the main prompt first, then add the negative.

It also cannot remove a flaw that is part of the subject itself. If you ask for a close-up of hands and the hands come out wrong, extra fingers will help a little, but the real problem is that you asked for the hardest subject in close-up. The better fix is to change the framing — hands out of frame, or a wider shot — and let the negative prompt handle the rest.

Rare and specific flaws resist negative prompts too. A negative prompt works against patterns the model has seen often; a flaw that is unusual — a strange reflection, a one-off distortion — has no strong pattern to push against, and the exclusion may do nothing at all. In those cases, regenerating with a slightly different main prompt usually helps more than a longer negative list.

Finally, a negative prompt can fight the main prompt when the two disagree. Excluding dark in a night scene, or excluding grain in a vintage film look, asks the model to do two opposite things at once, and the result is usually a compromise that satisfies neither. Check that every exclusion agrees with the picture you actually want.

Common mistakes

The most damaging mistake is negating the main subject. A negative prompt of no cat on a picture of a cat, or no trees on a forest scene, asks the model to draw something and not draw it at the same time. The result is confusion: the subject may vanish, distort, or appear half-formed. Never exclude the thing the picture is about.

The second mistake is excluding too much. A negative prompt of twenty items dilutes every one of them, and it can flatten the picture: excluding grain, shadow, texture, and contrast all at once leaves the model with nothing to build depth from. Start with four to six items and add only what a real flaw demands.

The third mistake is copying a giant negative prompt you do not understand. Long lists passed around online often contain words that fight your picture — excluding film grain when you want a vintage look, or excluding dark when you want a night scene. Every item in your negative prompt should be there because you know what it excludes and you want it gone. If you cannot explain an item, remove it.

The last mistake is using the negative prompt as a crutch. When every picture needs a long list of exclusions to come out right, the main prompt is usually the problem: too vague, too crowded, or asking for the wrong thing. A good main prompt needs only a short negative list beside it. Fix the main prompt first, and let the negative prompt do the smaller job it is good at.

Questions people ask

What is a negative prompt?

A negative prompt is a short list of things you do not want in a picture — blurry, distorted, extra fingers, watermark, text, low quality. It sits beside the main prompt and pushes the model away from the flaws you have seen before.

Do all image generators support negative prompts?

No. Some tools have a separate negative-prompt box, and some do not. If yours has no box, write the exclusion into the main prompt as a without phrase, such as without text, without watermark. Check your tool's help pages to see which kind it is.

What should I put in a negative prompt?

The flaws you actually see in your results. Start with blurry, distorted, extra fingers, watermark, text, and low quality, then add items only when a real flaw appears. A short list aimed at your problem beats a long generic one.

Can a negative prompt remove text from an image?

It helps a lot: excluding text and watermark stops the model adding fake signs, garbled words, and invented logos in most cases. It is not a guarantee, and if the main prompt itself asks for a sign or a label, the two instructions will fight.

Why does my image still have extra fingers?

Because a negative prompt is a nudge, not a guarantee — and hands are the hardest shape for the model. Try extra fingers and bad anatomy together, generate several versions, or keep the hands out of frame with arms crossed or hands in pockets.

How long should a negative prompt be?

Four to six items is a good starting length. Add items only when you see a real flaw that needs excluding. A list of twenty items dilutes every one of them and can flatten the picture.
