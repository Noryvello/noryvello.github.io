How to Fix a Zipper

Most zipper problems — a stuck slider, teeth that separate behind the slider, or a broken pull — can be fixed at home with a few simple steps and no special tools. Pick your problem below to get the right fix, and use the checklist if the slider itself needs replacing.

Before you start

Work on a flat surface in good light, and take a close look at the zipper before you touch it. Check whether the teeth are metal or plastic, whether the tape on either side is torn, and whether anything is caught in the slider. Most fixes need nothing more than a pair of pliers, a pencil, and a needle and thread, so gather those before you begin. If the item is a jacket or a bag, empty the pockets and lay it flat so the zipper lies straight. A zipper that is twisted or pulled tight while you work on it is much harder to judge, and you may think it is broken when it is only under strain.

Never force a stuck zipper. Pulling hard can bend the slider, snap teeth off, or tear the tape, and each of those turns a small fix into a bigger one. If the slider will not move, stop and look for the cause instead: a fold of fabric caught in the teeth, a grain of grit, or a bent tooth near the bottom. Work gently, move the slider a little at a time, and test it after each try. Patience matters more than strength with zippers.

Why zippers fail

A zipper is a simple machine, and most failures come down to wear. The slider is the part that moves, so it wears first: its jaws open up little by little until they no longer press the teeth together firmly, and the zipper starts to separate behind the slider as you close it. Dirt and grit speed this up, because every pass of the slider grinds the dirt into the metal or plastic. Salt, sand, and damp speed it up further, which is why zippers on beach bags and outdoor jackets often fail first.

Teeth fail differently. Metal teeth can bend or snap if the zipper is forced, and plastic coil teeth can stretch out of shape if the item is overfilled. A missing tooth near the top or bottom of the zipper is often no more than an annoyance, but a missing tooth in the middle usually stops the slider in its tracks. The tape can also tear away from the fabric, especially on bags that are carried by one handle with a heavy load.

When a zipper cannot be saved

Some damage is beyond a home fix. If the tape is torn along its length, the teeth have nothing firm to sit in, and no new slider will help. If several teeth are missing from the middle of the zipper, the slider will jam every time it reaches the gap. A broken bottom stop on a jacket zipper means the two sides cannot be joined at all. In these cases the whole zipper needs replacing, which means unpicking the old one and sewing in a new one.

Replacing a full zipper is possible at home if you sew, but it takes time and care to get the new zipper straight. For a valuable coat, a good bag, or anything with a waterproof zipper, a repair shop is usually the better choice. Ask the shop to show you the replacement zipper before the work starts, so you can check that the colour and size match.

Keeping zippers working

A little care keeps zippers working for years. Brush grit and sand out of the teeth now and then, especially on outdoor gear. Close zippers before washing, so the slider does not catch on other clothes and the teeth are not pulled sideways. Do not overfill bags and cases, because a zipper under constant strain wears out far faster than one that closes easily. Store jackets and bags with their zippers closed, so the tape stays flat and the teeth stay lined up.

If a zipper starts to feel stiff, rub a graphite pencil along the teeth or run a dry bar of soap over them. This light lubrication is enough for most zippers and does not attract dirt the way oil does. Do this once in a while, and the slider will keep running smoothly.

These steps cover common household zippers on jackets, bags, and trousers. If the tape is torn, many teeth are missing, or the item is valuable, a repair shop is the safer choice. Never force a stuck zipper — forcing it usually makes the damage worse.

Questions people ask

Why does my zipper keep coming apart behind the slider?

The slider has usually worn loose and no longer squeezes the teeth together firmly. Gently crimping the slider with pliers often fixes it, and if it does not, fitting a replacement slider usually does.

Can I fix a zipper without replacing it?

Yes, in many cases. A stuck slider often frees with cleaning and a little lubrication, and a loose slider can be crimped or replaced without touching the zipper tape.

What can I use to lubricate a stiff zipper?

A graphite pencil rubbed along the teeth works well, and so does a dry bar of soap. Avoid oils and greasy products, which attract dirt and can stain fabric.

How do I know which replacement slider to buy?

Match the size number stamped on the back of the old slider and the zipper type, such as metal or plastic coil. Take the old slider with you when you shop so you can compare it.

Is it worth repairing a zipper on a cheap item?

A simple fix costs little and takes a few minutes, so it is usually worth trying. If the tape is torn or many teeth are missing, replacing the whole zipper takes more skill and a repair shop may be the better choice.
