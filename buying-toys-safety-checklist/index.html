Toy Buying Safety Checklist

This checklist walks you through the safety checks worth doing before you buy a toy, from age labels and small parts to materials and recalls. Tick each item off as you check it, and the counter keeps track of your progress.

Work through the list before you buy, and tick each check off as you do it.

This page is general safety information only, not a safety certification. If a toy looks damaged, has loose parts or a strong chemical smell, do not buy it or give it to a child. Always follow the maker's warnings and your local safety guidance.

How to use this checklist

Start at the top of the list and work down while you still have the toy in the shop or the listing open in front of you. Most of the checks take a few seconds each: a look at the age label, a tug on the small parts, a sniff for a chemical smell. The point is not to turn toy shopping into a chore, but to catch the few things that genuinely matter before the toy comes home.

If you are buying online, use the product photos and description to work through the same checks, and read the listing carefully for the age range and materials. When the toy arrives, run through the list once more in person before you hand it over. Packaging can hide damage, and a second look after delivery is worth the minute it takes.

Why age labels matter

Age labels are not about how clever a child is. They are about safety: the size of the parts, the strength needed to use the toy, and the kind of play it invites. A toy labelled for older children may have small parts, long cords or small magnets that are fine for an older child but risky for a toddler. Buying above the age label is the most common mistake, and it is also the easiest to avoid.

The label is a starting point, not a promise. A child who still puts things in their mouth needs the small-parts check whatever the label says, and a younger sibling in the house changes the picture too. If in doubt, choose the toy made for the younger age group.

The checks that matter most

Small parts come first. Anything that can come off a toy can end up in a small mouth, so tug on eyes, buttons, wheels and decorations, and check that seams on soft toys hold when pulled. The toilet roll tube test is a useful rule of thumb: if a piece fits fully inside the tube, it is small enough to be a choking risk for a young child.

Materials come next. Look for non-toxic labelling and be wary of toys with a strong chemical smell, flaking paint or rough finishes. If a toy smells strongly when you open the box, leave it to air out and check again later; if the smell stays, it is better not to give it to a child.

Recalls are the check people skip most often. Makers do recall toys, and a quick search of the toy's name and maker before you buy takes less than a minute. It is especially worth doing for second-hand toys and for toys bought from a seller you have not used before.

After you buy

Safety does not stop at the till. Check toys again every few weeks, because play is hard on them: seams split, wheels loosen and batteries leak. Take broken toys away straight away, and throw out anything with sharp edges or exposed stuffing rather than trying to patch it up.

Keep small pieces in their box or a labelled container, and make putting them away part of the play routine. It keeps the pieces together, keeps them out of reach of younger children, and makes the next play session easier to start.

Questions people ask

What age label should I follow?

Follow the label on the box, and buy for the youngest child who will be able to reach the toy. Age labels are about safety, not ability, so a toy labelled for older children may have small parts or cords that are risky for a younger child.

How do I check for recalls?

Search the toy's name and maker together with the word recall, and check your country's product safety listings. Do this before you buy, and again for second-hand toys, where an older recall may be easy to miss.

Are second-hand toys safe?

They can be, if you inspect them properly. Check for damage, missing parts, loose seams and older safety labels, and run the recalls check. Avoid second-hand toys with peeling paint, a strong smell or parts you cannot identify.

What should I do if a toy breaks?

Take it away from the child straight away and check for sharp edges, loose parts or exposed stuffing. Small repairs are fine for a torn seam, but throw the toy out if it has sharp edges, a broken battery cover or parts that cannot be fixed securely.

Do I need to supervise every toy?

Not every toy, but some kinds of play always need an adult nearby: craft sets with small pieces, water toys, toys with strings, and anything a child is using for the first time. The checklist notes which toys need supervision so you can plan for it.
