Video Interview Preparation

A video interview goes well when the interviewer can see you clearly, hear you without strain, and talk to you without the call breaking up. This guide walks through your camera, lighting, sound, background, and connection, then gives you a short practice routine, a backup plan for tech failures, and a checklist for the day itself.

Most video interviews fail on small, fixable things: a camera pointing up at the ceiling, a bright window turning you into a silhouette, or a laptop that runs out of charge halfway through. None of these say anything about how well you can do the job, but they make the conversation harder than it needs to be. The good news is that every one of them can be fixed in advance, in your own home, with what you already have.

This page is general guidance. It cannot check your connection or your equipment for you, so test your own setup before the day and confirm the details of your interview — the time, the link, and who is calling — with the interviewer.

Camera, lighting and sound setup

Start with the camera. It should sit at eye level, pointing straight at you, not up at you from a laptop on a low table. If your laptop camera sits too low, raise the laptop on a stack of books or a sturdy box until the lens is level with your eyes. The picture should show your head and shoulders, with a little space above your head, and you should sit about an arm's length from the screen.

Light matters more than the camera itself. The best light is soft and even, coming from in front of you: sit facing a window in daylight, or place a lamp just behind your camera, pointing at your face. Never sit with a bright window or a lamp behind you, or the camera will turn you into a dark silhouette. Overhead light on its own casts shadows under your eyes and chin, so if the room feels dim, add one light in front of you and check your own picture on screen.

For sound, a quiet room beats expensive equipment. Close the doors and windows, turn off anything that hums or beeps nearby, and sit close enough to your device that its built-in microphone picks you up clearly. If the room is noisy, headphones with a built-in microphone can help, and they also keep the interviewer's voice out of your speakers. Before the call, check the software's audio settings and confirm that the right microphone is selected. Silence your phone and turn off notifications on the device you are using, so nothing pops up mid-answer.

Background and framing

Your background should be plain and tidy. A blank wall, a neat shelf, or a closed door works well. Move anything distracting or private out of the frame: laundry, clutter, and anything you would not want a stranger to read. If the room behind you cannot be tidied, sit with your back to the plainest wall you have, or use the software's background blur if it offers one, and test it first so you know how it looks.

Framing is simple once the camera is at eye level. Sit in the middle of the picture, with your head and shoulders in frame and a small gap between the top of your head and the top of the picture. Keep the camera still: if you use a phone, prop it up against something solid instead of holding it, and put it on silent. Dress as you would for an in-person interview, at least from the waist up. Tell the people you live with the time of your interview, and put a note on the door so nobody walks in halfway through.

Connection and software test

Test everything the day before, at the same time of day as your interview if you can, because connections are often slower in the evening than in the morning. Open the interview link to check it works, and install or update the video-call software in advance, so the call itself is not the first time you have opened it. Restart your device the night before or the morning of the interview: a fresh start clears the small glitches that cause frozen pictures and silent microphones.

On the day, close every program and browser tab you do not need, especially anything that streams video or downloads files, because those compete with your call for the same connection. Sit as close to your router as you can, or use a wired connection if one is available. If other people share your connection, ask them to pause large downloads and video streaming while you are in the interview. Charge your device fully and keep the charger plugged in. Then make a short test call with a friend, using the exact setup you will use, and ask them to confirm they can see and hear you clearly.

A short practice routine

Set aside 20 to 30 minutes the day before to practise. Answer a few common questions out loud — why you want the role, what you did in your last job, and a time you solved a problem — because speaking an answer aloud is very different from thinking it through in your head. Record yourself answering one or two questions, then watch the recording back: check your pace, your volume, whether you look at the camera, and whether you repeat any filler words.

Practise with the exact setup you will use on the day: the same chair, the same light, and the same device, so nothing feels new when the call starts. Prepare a few questions to ask the interviewer, and keep a short list of points beside you, out of camera view — a few notes to glance at, not a script to read. Have your CV or your application notes within reach too, so you can check a date or a detail without searching for it during the call.

A backup plan for tech failures

Decide what you will do if something goes wrong, before it happens. If the picture freezes or the sound drops, say so calmly and wait a few seconds: most glitches clear on their own. If they do not, try switching your camera off and back on, or leave the call and rejoin using the same link. If the call drops completely, rejoin the same way, and give the interviewer a moment to do the same.

Keep a second device ready, such as a phone, that can join the call if your laptop fails. Before the interview, write down the interviewer's phone number or another way to reach them, and keep it on paper beside you, not only inside the device you are using. If the call keeps failing, contact the interviewer and suggest continuing by phone, or ask to reschedule. If the power or the connection fails completely, get in touch as soon as you can and explain what happened. Technology fails for everyone, and handling it calmly is part of the impression you make.

Day-of checklist

Run through this list about half an hour before the call:

  • Charge your device fully and keep the charger plugged in.
  • Open the interview link and check your camera and microphone.
  • Close other programs and silence notifications.
  • Check your lighting and framing on screen.
  • Keep water, your notes, a pen, and the interviewer's contact details within reach.
  • Join the call five minutes early, sit down, and take a breath before the interviewer arrives.

Questions people ask

What is the best lighting for a video interview?

Soft, even light on your face, coming from in front of you. Sit facing a window in daylight, or place a lamp behind your camera, and never sit with a bright window or lamp behind you, or your face will fall into shadow.

How do I test my setup before the call?

Make a test call with a friend or family member the day before, using the same device, chair, and lighting you will use for the interview. Ask them to check that they can see your face clearly, hear you without strain, and read your name on screen.

What should I do if my connection drops mid-interview?

Stay calm and rejoin the call using the same link. If the call keeps failing, contact the interviewer on the phone number you saved before the interview and suggest continuing by phone or rescheduling.

Should I look at the camera or the screen?

Look at the camera lens when you are speaking, so the interviewer feels you are looking at them. When the interviewer is speaking, it is natural to look at the screen to see their face.
