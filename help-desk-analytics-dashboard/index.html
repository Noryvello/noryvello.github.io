Help Desk Analytics Dashboard

A help desk dashboard is only useful if it shows the numbers that actually describe good service: how fast you first reply, how fast you resolve problems, and how satisfied customers are. This guide explains the three metrics that matter most, shows you how to compute each one, and includes a calculator that works them out from your own numbers.

Metrics describe your own tickets only — they cannot be compared with another team's numbers, because every team counts differently. Use them to track your own trend over time, not to judge yourself against a number you read elsewhere.

Why help desk metrics matter

A help desk without numbers runs on feelings: the team feels busy, customers feel unhappy, and nobody can say whether things are getting better or worse. Metrics replace feelings with facts. They show how fast the team answers, how fast it solves problems, and how customers feel about the answers they got. With those three facts, a team can see its own trend and fix what is actually broken, instead of guessing.

Three metrics cover most of what matters. First response time measures how quickly a customer hears back after writing in. Resolution time measures how long it takes to solve the problem completely. CSAT — customer satisfaction — measures how the customer felt about the experience. Each one answers a different question, and a dashboard that shows all three gives a fair picture of the service.

First response time

First response time is the time between a customer writing in and the team sending its first reply. It is measured per ticket, in minutes or hours, and the dashboard usually shows the average across all tickets in a period. It matters because the first reply is when a customer stops wondering whether anyone heard them: a quick first reply, even one that only says "we are looking into this", changes the whole experience of waiting.

To compute it, add up the first-response times of every ticket in the period, then divide by the number of tickets. If 120 tickets took 1,800 minutes of first-response time in total, the average first response is 1,800 divided by 120, which is 15.0 minutes. The calculator above does this sum for you — enter the total minutes and the ticket count, and it shows the average with the working.

Watch the average together with the slowest cases. An average of 15 minutes can hide a few tickets that waited a whole day, and those are the tickets that lose customers. If your system shows the longest waits, look at them every week: they usually share a cause, such as tickets arriving outside working hours, or one type of problem that nobody picks up quickly.

Resolution time

Resolution time is the time between a ticket arriving and the problem being fully solved. It is usually measured in hours, and like first response time, the dashboard shows the average across the tickets resolved in a period. It matters because it is the number the customer actually experiences: the time from "I have a problem" to "my problem is fixed".

The maths is the same shape as first response time. Add up the resolution times of every ticket resolved in the period, then divide by the number of tickets. If 120 tickets took 360 hours of resolution time in total, the average resolution is 360 divided by 120, which is 3.0 hours. The calculator shows this sum with the working, so you can verify it against your own records.

Resolution time and first response time answer different questions, and a team can be good at one and poor at the other. A team that replies in minutes but takes days to solve problems feels responsive but unhelpful; a team that solves problems quickly but replies slowly feels capable but distant. Track both, and fix whichever is worse first — customers notice the slow one, not the fast one.

Be careful about what counts as resolved. A ticket closed because the customer stopped replying is not a resolution, and counting it as one flatters the numbers while hiding the problem. Count only tickets where the problem was actually solved, and keep a separate eye on tickets that go quiet — they are often customers who gave up, not customers who were helped.

CSAT: customer satisfaction

CSAT, short for customer satisfaction, is the share of customers who say they were satisfied with the help they received. It is usually measured by a short survey sent after a ticket is resolved, asking the customer to rate the experience. The score is the number of satisfied responses divided by the total number of responses, multiplied by 100, giving a percentage.

To compute it, count the satisfied responses and the total responses in a period, then divide and multiply. If 96 of 120 survey responses were satisfied, the CSAT is 96 divided by 120, times 100, which is 80 percent. The calculator above works this out from your two numbers and shows the working.

CSAT has a weakness worth knowing: the people who answer surveys are not a random sample. Very happy and very unhappy customers reply more often than everyone in the middle, so the score can overstate both extremes. Treat CSAT as a trend — is it rising or falling over months — rather than as a precise grade, and read the comments customers leave, because a single detailed comment often explains more than the score alone.

Also, never compare your CSAT with a number you read elsewhere. Every team asks the question differently, counts "satisfied" differently, and serves different customers. The only comparison that means anything is with your own past scores.

Other metrics worth watching

Three more numbers are worth a look once the main three are in place. The first is the backlog: how many tickets are open right now, and how old the oldest ones are. A growing backlog means the team is receiving more than it can solve, and the fix is either more help or fewer incoming tickets — often achieved by fixing the problem that causes the most tickets.

The second is the reopen rate: how many resolved tickets come back because the problem was not really fixed. A high reopen rate means the team is closing tickets too quickly, and it is one of the most honest measures of quality. If one in five tickets reopens, the team is not solving problems; it is postponing them.

The third is first-contact resolution: the share of tickets solved in a single exchange, without the customer having to write back. It is a good measure of how well the team understands the problems it sees every day. A high rate means common problems are solved on the first try; a low rate means the team needs better answers for the questions it sees most.

Reading the numbers together

No single metric tells the truth on its own. A fast first response with a slow resolution means the team is polite but stuck. A fast resolution with a low CSAT means the team solves problems in a way customers dislike — perhaps with rude replies, or by closing tickets before the customer agrees the problem is gone. A high CSAT with a growing backlog means the customers who get helped are happy, while more and more customers wait.

The useful habit is a short weekly review: look at the three main numbers, compare them with last week, and ask what changed. If first response time rose, was it a holiday, a surge of tickets, or a gap in cover? If CSAT fell, read the comments from the unhappy customers — they usually name the cause. A weekly review of ten minutes keeps the numbers honest and the team focused on the right fix.

Share the numbers with the team, not just with managers. People who can see their own metrics improve them; people who only hear about metrics in a review meeting fear them. A dashboard on the wall — or on a shared page — that the team itself updates is worth more than a report nobody reads.

Worked example

Here is a full worked example, using the numbers the calculator starts with. A small team resolved 120 tickets in one month. The first-response times of those tickets added up to 1,800 minutes. The resolution times added up to 360 hours. Of the 120 customers who answered the satisfaction survey, 96 said they were satisfied.

Average first response: 1,800 minutes divided by 120 tickets equals 15.0 minutes. Average resolution: 360 hours divided by 120 tickets equals 3.0 hours. CSAT: 96 satisfied divided by 120 responses, times 100, equals 80 percent. You can check each of these by hand, and the calculator above shows the same three results with the same working when you press the button.

What would this team do next? The numbers themselves do not say — they only describe. The team would compare them with the previous month: if first response time was 20 minutes last month and is 15 now, the team is improving; if CSAT was 85 percent and is 80 now, something changed for the worse, and the survey comments are the place to look. The metrics start the conversation; they do not finish it.

Common mistakes with help desk metrics

The first mistake is measuring speed alone. A team judged only on response time will reply quickly and solve slowly, because the metric rewards the reply. Measure resolution and satisfaction alongside speed, and the team will balance all three.

The second mistake is comparing with other teams. Every team counts differently: one counts a first reply from an automated message, another counts only a human reply. The numbers look comparable and are not. Compare only with your own past numbers, counted the same way.

The third mistake is hiding bad numbers. A team that is afraid of its metrics will find ways to make them look better — closing tickets early, skipping the survey for unhappy customers, or redefining what counts as resolved. The numbers then describe nothing, and the problems they were meant to reveal stay hidden. The fix is cultural: treat bad numbers as information, not as blame, and the team will report them honestly.

The fourth mistake is collecting numbers nobody reads. A dashboard that nobody opens is decoration. If the weekly review does not happen, the metrics do not matter — so protect the review, keep it short, and let the numbers drive one real change at a time.

Questions people ask

What is first response time in a help desk?

It is the time between a customer writing in and the team sending its first reply, averaged across the tickets in a period. To compute it, add up the first-response times of every ticket and divide by the number of tickets. For example, 1,800 minutes across 120 tickets gives an average first response of 15.0 minutes.

How is average resolution time calculated?

Add up the resolution times of every ticket resolved in the period, then divide by the number of tickets. For example, 360 hours across 120 tickets gives an average resolution of 3.0 hours. Count only tickets where the problem was actually solved — a ticket closed because the customer stopped replying is not a resolution.

What is CSAT and how do you work it out?

CSAT, or customer satisfaction, is the share of customers who say they were satisfied with the help they received. Divide the number of satisfied survey responses by the total number of responses, then multiply by 100. For example, 96 satisfied out of 120 responses gives a CSAT of 80 percent.

Which help desk metric matters most?

No single metric tells the truth alone. First response time shows how quickly customers are heard, resolution time shows how quickly problems are solved, and CSAT shows how customers felt about the experience. A team should track all three and read them together, because each one can look good while another is failing.

Can I compare my help desk metrics with other teams?

No, not fairly. Every team counts differently — one may count an automated reply as a first response while another counts only a human reply — so the numbers look comparable and are not. The only useful comparison is with your own past numbers, counted the same way, to see your own trend.

What else should a help desk dashboard show?

Beyond the three main metrics, watch the backlog (how many tickets are open and how old the oldest are), the reopen rate (how many resolved tickets come back), and first-contact resolution (the share solved in a single exchange). A short weekly review of these numbers keeps the team focused on the right fix.
