Legal Document Checklist

Why a document checklist matters

Every freelancer and small business runs on documents. Contracts record what was agreed. Invoices record what was charged. Licences prove you are allowed to trade. Tax records prove what you earned and what you paid. When those documents are missing, small problems become large ones: a client disputes a payment and there is no contract to check, a tax office asks for a record and it cannot be found, or a licence expires unnoticed and work has to stop.

A checklist turns that worry into a task you can finish. Instead of wondering whether your paperwork is in order, you work through a list, gather what is missing, and tick each item off. The list on this page covers the documents that freelancers and small businesses most often need, grouped by type. Tick a box when the document is in place and stored safely; your ticks are saved in this browser, on this device only, so you can close the page and come back later without losing your progress.

Two things this checklist cannot do. It cannot tell you exactly which documents your country requires, because those rules differ from place to place and change over time. And it cannot replace professional advice for your particular situation. What it can do is give you a clear, honest picture of where your paperwork stands, so that nothing important is forgotten.

Contracts and agreements

Contracts are the foundation of your paperwork. Every piece of work you do for someone else, and every service someone else provides to you, should be recorded in a written agreement that both sides have signed. A handshake and a friendly email are not enough when memories differ later — a signed contract is the record that settles the question.

Tick each item when the document exists, is signed, and is stored where you can find it.

Invoices and payment records

Invoices are the documents that ask for payment, and payment records prove that payment arrived. Together they are the paper trail of your income, and they are the first thing a tax office, an accountant, or a client in a dispute will ask to see. Good invoicing habits — clear numbers, clear dates, and a copy kept for every invoice sent — prevent most payment arguments.

An invoice should normally show your business name and contact details, the client's name, a unique invoice number, the date, a description of the work or goods, the amount due, and the date by which it should be paid. Your records should show which invoices are paid, which are overdue, and which are still waiting.

Licences and registrations

Many businesses need a licence, a registration, or a permit before they can trade legally, and the rules are set locally — by your country, your region, or your city. A licence you needed when you started may have an expiry date, and a business that has grown or changed what it sells may need a new one. This section is about knowing what you hold and when it runs out, not about guessing the rules.

Keep a note of every licence and registration you hold, the authority that issued it, the date it expires, and the date you must renew it. If you are not sure whether you need a particular licence, check with the official source for your area rather than guessing.

Tax and financial records

Tax records are the documents that prove what your business earned, what it spent, and what tax you paid. Tax offices in most countries can ask to see records going back several years, so keeping them in order is not optional housekeeping — it is part of running the business. The exact list of records and how long to keep them is set by your country's tax rules, so check those rules for your own situation.

A simple system works best: one place for income records, one place for expense records, and one place for tax filings and letters from the tax office. If your records are digital, keep a backup copy somewhere separate, so that a lost phone or a broken computer does not take your business history with it.

Employment and people records

If you employ people — even one person, even part time — you take on record-keeping duties that go beyond your own paperwork. Employment contracts, working hours, pay, holidays, and any training or safety records all need to be kept, and the rules about what to keep and for how long are set by your country's employment law. The same care applies to the people who work with you as subcontractors: keep their agreements and their invoices, and be clear in writing about who is an employee and who is not.

Keeping your documents safe and organised

A document you cannot find is almost as useless as a document you never had. The best filing system is the simplest one you will actually use: a small number of folders — contracts, invoices, licences, tax, people — each with documents named by date and subject, so that the newest version is easy to spot. Paper documents can live in labelled folders; digital documents can live in folders with the same names.

Keep originals of anything signed, and keep a copy somewhere else. A scan or a clear photograph of a signed contract is a good backup, but it is not always a replacement for the original, so do not throw the paper away until you know your country's rules. For digital records, a backup on a separate drive or a trusted cloud service protects you against a lost or broken device. Check your backup at least once a year by opening a few files — a backup you have never tested is a hope, not a plan.

Be careful with other people's private information. Contracts and invoices contain names, addresses, and payment details. Keep them where only the people who need them can reach them, lock your devices, and destroy old documents that hold private information when you no longer need them, rather than throwing them away whole.

Reviewing your documents once a year

Set aside one day each year to review your documents. Work through this checklist again: are the contracts still current, are the licences still in date, are the invoices all accounted for, and is the backup working? A yearly review catches the quiet problems — a licence that expires next month, a contract that ended without anyone noticing, a folder that has not been backed up — while they are still easy to fix.

A yearly review is also the right time to clear out what you no longer need, following your country's rules about how long records must be kept. Do not destroy tax or employment records on a guess: check the required keeping period first, and when in doubt, keep the document. Storage is cheap; a missing record is expensive.

If your business has changed during the year — new services, new staff, a new location, a new country of operation — the review is the moment to ask whether your documents have kept up. New work often needs new contracts, new licences, or new insurance, and the cheapest time to arrange them is before they are needed, not after.

Questions people ask

What documents does a freelancer need?

Most freelancers need signed contracts for their work, numbered invoices with copies kept, records of payments received, receipts for business expenses, and any licence their trade requires. If you employ anyone, you also need employment contracts and pay records. The exact list depends on your country's rules, so check the official requirements for your area.

How long should I keep invoices and tax records?

The required keeping period is set by your country's tax rules and differs from place to place, often several years. Check the rule that applies to you before destroying any record. When in doubt, keep the document: storage is cheap, and a missing record can be expensive if a tax office or a client asks for it later.

Do I need a written contract for small jobs?

Yes, even for small jobs. A short written agreement that names the work, the price, and the deadline prevents most misunderstandings, and it costs little to prepare. A friendly email that both sides confirm can work for very small jobs, but a signed contract is clearer and easier to rely on if something goes wrong.

What is the difference between an invoice and a receipt?

An invoice is a document that asks for payment: it lists what was sold or done, the amount due, and the date payment is expected. A receipt is proof that payment was made: it records the amount received, the date, and what it was for. You send invoices to clients, and you keep receipts for money you pay and money you receive.

Should I keep paper or digital copies?

Either can work, and many businesses keep both. Digital copies are easy to search and back up, while paper originals matter for signed contracts in some countries. Whatever you choose, keep your files organised by type and date, and keep a backup copy stored separately from the originals.

When should I get legal advice about my documents?

Get advice when the stakes are high: a large contract, a dispute, an employment problem, a new licence requirement, or anything you do not understand. A short review by a qualified lawyer in your own country is far cheaper than fixing a problem after it has grown. This checklist is information only and does not replace that advice.
