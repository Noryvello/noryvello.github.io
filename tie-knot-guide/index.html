How to Tie a Tie

A tie knot is just a short series of folds, and the four knots in this guide cover every collar and every occasion. Pick a knot below to see its steps in plain words, with notes on matching the knot to your collar.

Learning to tie a tie looks harder than it is, because the steps are usually shown in pictures that are hard to follow. In words, every knot is the same idea: the wide end crosses the narrow end, wraps around it, and passes through the loop at your neck. The knots differ only in how many times the wide end wraps before it goes through.

The four knots here go from smallest to largest. The four-in-hand is the slim everyday knot, the half-Windsor is a neat triangle, the full Windsor is the large formal knot, and the Pratt is a tidy medium knot that uses less length. Learn the four-in-hand first, and the rest will make sense quickly.

Knot sizes are only a guide. The width of the tie, the size of the collar and the length of your neck all change how a knot looks, so practise in front of a mirror and undo a knot and start again rather than forcing it tight.

Pick a knot

Choose a knot to see its numbered steps and what it suits best. All four start the same way: the wide end on your right, hanging lower than the narrow end.

Before you start

Put the collar of your shirt up and button the top button. The collar stays up while you tie the knot and comes down over the finished knot at the end. Trying to tie a knot with the collar down leaves you fighting the fabric the whole way.

Drape the tie around your neck with the wide end on your right and the narrow end on your left. The wide end should hang noticeably lower than the narrow end, because the wide end does all the wrapping and uses up length with each fold. How much lower depends on the knot: the full Windsor uses the most length, so start the wide end lower for that knot than for the four-in-hand.

Stand in front of a mirror and work slowly. Speed comes with practice, and a slow knot that is even beats a fast knot that has to be undone. If a step goes wrong, undo the knot fully and start again rather than trying to fix it in place.

Choose the tie before you choose the knot. A wide tie suits a larger knot, while a narrow tie suits a slim knot, and a thick silk tie will make a bigger knot than a thin cotton one. Hold the tie against the shirt and check the pattern and colour sit well together before you start tying, since a knot that is tied well but looks wrong with the shirt is still a poor choice.

Matching the knot to the collar

The collar decides the knot more than the occasion does. A knot that is too small for a wide collar looks lost, and a knot that is too large for a narrow collar crowds the neck and pushes the collar points outward.

A standard collar, with points that sit close together, suits the four-in-hand or the half-Windsor. The four-in-hand is the slimmer choice and works well with narrow ties, while the half-Windsor fills the space a little more and looks neater with a wider tie.

A spread collar, with points that angle outward, needs a larger knot to fill the gap. The full Windsor was made for this collar, and the half-Windsor works too. A narrow collar suits the four-in-hand or the Pratt, both of which stay slim and sit neatly between close collar points.

The tie itself matters as well. A thick tie makes a larger knot than a thin one, so a thick tie in a full Windsor can end up very large. If your tie is thick, drop down a knot size. If it is thin, a larger knot can give it the presence it needs.

Finishing and fixing

When the knot is formed, hold the narrow end in one hand and slide the knot gently up to the collar with the other. Keep the knot centred and straight as it rises, and pinch the centre of the wide end just below the knot to form the dimple, the small fold that gives a tied knot its finished look.

Check the length. The tip of the wide end should reach the middle of your belt, and the narrow end should be hidden behind it. If the wide end is too short, the knot used more length than you allowed for, so start again with the wide end hanging lower. If it is too long, start with it a little higher.

At the end of the day, undo the tie by reversing the steps: loosen the knot, slide the narrow end back through, and undo each fold. Never pull the narrow end through a tight knot, and never leave a tie knotted overnight. A tie that is undone properly and hung up keeps its shape, while one that is yanked off and left in a ball creases in ways that are hard to press out.

Store ties by hanging them on a tie rack or draping them over a hanger, rather than rolling them up in a drawer. Hanging lets the creases from the day fall out on their own. If a tie does pick up a crease, hang it in a steamy bathroom for a while, which relaxes the fabric without the risk of pressing it with an iron.

Questions people ask

Which knot should I learn first?

The four-in-hand. It has the fewest steps, it suits most collars and ties, and once you can tie it without thinking, the other knots are easier to learn.

How long should the tie hang?

The tip of the wide end should reach the middle of your belt when the knot is done. Start with the wide end hanging lower than the narrow end, and adjust the starting length until the finished tie lands in the right place.

Why does my knot look uneven?

An uneven knot usually comes from pulling one side tighter than the other. Undo the knot, keep the folds even as you build it, and tighten it slowly while you hold the narrow end.

What is the dimple under the knot?

The dimple is the small fold pinched into the tie just below the knot. Pinch the centre of the wide end as you slide the knot up, and the dimple forms on its own.

How do I undo a tie at the end of the day?

Reverse the steps: loosen the knot, slide the narrow end back through, and undo each fold. Never pull the narrow end through a tight knot, because it creases the tie and strains the fabric.
