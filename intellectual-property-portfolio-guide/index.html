Intellectual Property Portfolio Guide

This guide explains patents, trademarks, copyright and trade secrets in plain words, and how a small business can list and look after what it owns. It is general information only, not legal advice, and IP law differs by place.

What this guide covers and who needs it

Intellectual property is the name for the creations of the mind that the law protects: inventions, brand names, logos, written works, designs, music, and the secret know-how that makes a business work. Most small businesses own more intellectual property than they realise. A shop owns its name and its sign. A studio owns its designs and its client list. A maker owns a recipe, a method, or a pattern that competitors would love to copy. A portfolio is simply a list of all of it, kept in one place, so nothing is forgotten, lost or accidentally given away.

There are four main kinds, and they protect different things. A patent protects an invention — a new product or a new way of doing something. A trademark protects a brand — a name, a logo or a sign that tells customers who made a product. Copyright protects original creative work — writing, images, music, software — from being copied. A trade secret protects confidential business information, such as a recipe or a client list, by keeping it secret. Each kind has its own rules, its own length of protection, and its own way of being registered or kept.

This guide explains each kind in plain words, shows how to list what a business owns, and explains when registration is worth considering. It is written for creators and small businesses who want to look after what they have made. It is general information only, not legal advice, and IP law differs by place — ask a qualified IP professional before you register anything or rely on any right. The search tool named in this guide, tmsearch.uspto.gov, is given as plain text for you to type into your browser.

How intellectual property works, step by step

Start by understanding the four kinds. A patent gives the owner the right to stop others from making, using or selling an invention for a limited time, in exchange for describing it in public. Getting a patent takes time and money, and it is worth it only when the invention is genuinely new and commercially valuable. A trademark protects the signs that identify a business: its name, its logo, its slogan. A trademark can last indefinitely as long as it is used and renewed, and it is often the most valuable asset a small business owns.

Copyright is different from the other three because in most countries it arises automatically. As soon as an original work is created and fixed in some form — written down, drawn, recorded, coded — copyright exists, and no registration is needed for the right to exist. Registration can still help in a dispute in some places, and the rules differ, so check what applies where you are. A trade secret is different again: it is protected only while it stays secret. The moment a secret is published or widely shared, the protection is gone, which is why secrecy itself is the whole strategy.

The second step is to list what you own. Walk through your business and write down every name, logo, design, document, piece of software, recipe, method and client list that has value. For each item, note what kind of IP it is, who created it, when it was created, whether it is registered, and when any renewal is due. The list is the portfolio, and it can live in a simple document or a spreadsheet. The point is not the format; it is that the list exists, is complete, and is reviewed at least once a year.

The four kinds of intellectual property, in short:

  • Patent: protects an invention, for a limited time, after registration
  • Trademark: protects a brand name, logo or sign, renewable while in use
  • Copyright: protects original creative work, usually automatic on creation
  • Trade secret: protects confidential information, only while it stays secret

Costs, time and what you need before you start

Listing a portfolio costs nothing but time: an afternoon to walk through the business and write the list, and an hour a year to review it. Registration is where money is spent, and the cost varies widely by place, by the kind of right, and by whether a professional helps. A trademark application is usually the most affordable registration for a small business; a patent application is usually the most expensive and the slowest. Ask a qualified IP professional for the costs where you are before you decide, and never pay anyone who promises a guaranteed registration, because no honest professional can promise that.

Before you start, gather three things. First, a list of the names, logos and products your business uses. Second, any existing registrations, certificates or agreements, including any contract that says who owns work created for the business. Third, a note of dates: when each brand or product was first used, when each work was created, and when any renewal falls due. Dates matter in IP more than in almost any other area of business, because rights often depend on who used something first and whether a deadline was met.

What to have ready before you start:

  • A list of names, logos, designs and products the business uses
  • Existing registrations, certificates and ownership agreements
  • Dates: first use of each brand, creation of each work, renewals due
  • A simple document or spreadsheet to hold the portfolio list
  • The name of a qualified IP professional to ask when it matters

Worked example: listing what a small design studio owns

Consider a small example. Nadia runs a design studio with four people. She designs packaging for food businesses, and her studio has a name, a logo, a set of packaging designs, a website full of her own photographs, and a list of client contacts built over six years. Until this year, none of it was listed anywhere. Nadia spent an afternoon making a portfolio list. She wrote down the studio name and logo as trademarks, the packaging designs and photographs as copyright works, and the client list as a trade secret. She noted who created each item and when, and she found that the studio name had never been registered as a trademark.

Nadia then checked whether her studio name was already in use by someone else, using the official trademark search for her country — in the United States, the search tool is tmsearch.uspto.gov, typed into the browser. The old search system, known as TESS, has been retired, and the new tool has taken its place. She found no identical name in her field, and she asked an IP professional whether registration was worth it. The professional explained the cost, the time it would take, and the limits of protection, and Nadia decided to apply for the name and the logo.

Nadia also made two changes to how the studio works. First, every new design now carries a note of who created it and when, filed with the design itself. Second, the client list is now kept in a folder that only Nadia can open, and anyone who leaves the studio is reminded in writing that the list is confidential. The portfolio list is reviewed every January. None of this took more than a few days, and Nadia now knows exactly what the studio owns and what is protected.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The first common mistake is assuming that owning a business name means owning it as a trademark. Registering a company or a business name is not the same as registering a trademark, and the two systems are separate. The second mistake is using a name or logo without checking whether someone else already uses it. A search before you launch is cheap; a forced rebrand after launch is expensive and painful. Always search first, and ask a professional if the results are unclear.

The third mistake is forgetting who owns work made for the business. If a freelancer designs your logo, the freelancer may still own the copyright unless the contract says otherwise. Put ownership in writing before the work starts, not after. The fourth mistake is letting a trade secret leak: a client list shared casually, a recipe discussed in public, a method shown to a visitor. Once a secret is out, it cannot be put back. The fifth mistake is missing a renewal date. A trademark that is not renewed can be lost, so every renewal date belongs on the portfolio list and on a calendar.

A practical portfolio checklist you can follow

Use this checklist to build your portfolio list, and work through it again at each yearly review. It covers the four kinds of IP and the records each one needs. Adapt it to your business, and ask a qualified IP professional about anything that carries real value.

  • Names and logos listed, with first-use dates and registration status
  • Designs, photographs, writing and software listed as copyright works
  • Inventions noted, with a decision on whether to seek advice on patents
  • Trade secrets listed: recipes, methods, client lists, and who may see them
  • Ownership confirmed in writing for work made by freelancers or staff
  • Renewal dates listed and put on a calendar with reminders
  • Search done before any new name or logo is launched
  • Yearly review done: list current, renewals checked, secrets still secret

Tips from real practice

The best tip from real practice is to date everything. A design with a creation date, a brand with a first-use date, and a document with a signature date are all far more useful than the same items without dates. Keep the dated originals, and keep copies somewhere separate. If a dispute ever arises, dates are often the evidence that decides it, and a portfolio list with dates is worth more than a drawer of undated papers.

The second tip is to protect the valuable few, not everything equally. A small business cannot register every idea, and it does not need to. Protect the name customers know, the product that sells, and the secret that competitors want, and let the rest be listed and watched. The third tip is to get advice before a big step: before launching a brand, before signing a licence, before sharing a secret with a partner. A short conversation with a qualified IP professional at the right moment is one of the best investments a small business can make.

Questions people ask

What is intellectual property in simple words?

Intellectual property is the legal protection for creations of the mind: inventions, brand names, logos, written works, designs, music and secret business know-how. It gives the owner rights over what they created, such as the right to stop others copying it. Most small businesses own more of it than they realise.

What is the difference between a patent and a trademark?

A patent protects an invention — a new product or a new way of doing something — for a limited time, after registration. A trademark protects a brand — a name, a logo or a sign that tells customers who made a product — and it can last as long as it is used and renewed. They protect different things and work in different ways.

Do I own copyright automatically?

In most countries, yes. Copyright usually exists automatically as soon as an original work is created and fixed in some form — written down, drawn, recorded or coded — and no registration is needed for the right to exist. Registration can still help in a dispute in some places, so check the rules where you are.

When should I register a trademark?

Consider registering when your brand name or logo is settled, when customers know it, and when you plan to keep using it for years. Search first to check that no one else uses a similar mark in your field, then ask a qualified IP professional about the cost and the process. Registering early is cheaper than rebranding later.

How do I keep track of what my business owns?

Make a portfolio list: every name, logo, design, document, piece of software, recipe and client list, with the kind of IP it is, who created it, when, and whether it is registered. Keep the list in a simple document, review it once a year, and put every renewal date on a calendar.

What is a trade secret?

A trade secret is confidential business information that has value because it is secret: a recipe, a method, a client list, a pricing formula. It is protected only while it stays secret, so limit who can see it, keep it in a controlled place, and put confidentiality in writing with staff and partners.
