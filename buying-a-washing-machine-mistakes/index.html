7 Mistakes to Avoid When Buying a Washing Machine

A washing machine has to fit your home, your household and your laundry habits. This guide covers the 7 mistakes buyers most often make, from capacity and measurements to noise and plumbing, and ends with a capacity picker and a measurement checklist.

A washing machine is a machine you live with for years, several loads a week, so the wrong choice costs you time, water and patience every single wash day. The mistakes in this guide come from buying by the headline features instead of by the space it must fit, the household it must serve and the plumbing it must connect to. Start with your measurements and your household size, work through the seven mistakes, then use the capacity picker and the measurement checklist at the end before you spend anything.

This guide is general buying advice with no brand recommendations and no prices. Capacity suggestions are starting points — your laundry habits and the space you have decide what fits.

Mistake 1: Buying the wrong capacity

Capacity is the first decision, and getting it wrong costs you every week. A machine that is too small means extra loads, more time and more wear on the machine, while a machine that is too big wastes water and energy washing small loads in a large drum. Think about your household size and how often you wash, and remember the bulky items: bedding, towels and winter clothes take far more room than a normal load. The capacity picker below gives a starting point from your household size, but your own laundry habits are the final judge, and if you often wash bulky items, going one size up is usually worth it.

Mistake 2: Not measuring the space

A washing machine that does not fit is a costly return, and the space it needs is more than its width. Measure the width, depth and height of the gap, and leave room for hoses at the back and for the door to open fully at the front. Then measure the route the machine will travel: every doorway, hallway and stair turn between the front door and its final spot. A machine that fits the gap but cannot get through a narrow hallway is no use to anyone. Write the measurements down and take them with you, because guessing from memory in a shop is how mistakes happen.

Mistake 3: Ignoring noise and vibration

A washing machine shakes and hums, and where it sits decides how much that matters. In a flat, or near a bedroom or a room where you work, a loud spin cycle is a daily annoyance, and vibration can travel through floors to the rooms below. Check where your machine will sit and who will hear it, and remember that a machine on an uneven floor vibrates more than the same machine on a level one. If noise matters in your home, look for machines designed to run quietly and check the spin noise rating on the energy label before you decide.

Mistake 4: Choosing programmes you will never use

A long list of programmes looks impressive on the box, but most people use two or three programmes for almost every load. Before you compare machines, think about what you actually wash: everyday clothes, delicates, bedding, sportswear. Check that the programmes you need exist and are easy to find, and ignore the rest. A machine with a clear, simple set of programmes you understand is worth more than one with twenty you will never touch. If a programme you need is buried in a menu or needs an app to reach, you will not use it.

Mistake 5: Forgetting the plumbing and drainage

A washing machine needs three things within reach: a water supply, a drain and a power socket. Before you buy, check where each one sits in relation to the machine's final position, and whether the hoses supplied will reach. A drain that is too high or too far away causes problems, and a water supply on the wrong side may need a longer hose or a plumber. If you are replacing an old machine, note how it connects, because the same arrangement usually works for the new one. Sorting the plumbing out before delivery day is far easier than discovering a problem with a machine already in the hallway.

Mistake 6: Overlooking energy and water use

The purchase price is only the first cost of a washing machine. Over the years, the energy and water it uses can cost more than the machine itself, so the energy label deserves as much attention as the price. Compare machines on their rated energy and water use per cycle, and think about how many loads you run in a week, because a small difference per load grows quickly. A machine that costs more to buy but uses less per wash can be the cheaper choice over its lifetime. Look at the label, not just the ticket.

Mistake 7: Not checking delivery, installation and removal

A washing machine is heavy, awkward and connected to your water supply, so delivery is not a simple drop-off. Before you buy, find out who carries it in, who connects it, and whether installation is included or charged separately. Ask who takes the old machine away, and whether there is a charge for that too. On delivery day, check the new machine for damage before the team leaves, and run a short cycle to confirm there are no leaks at the connections. Agreeing all of this in advance turns a stressful day into a simple one.

Before you buy

Once the picker has given you a capacity and the checklist is done, compare two or three machines on the energy label and the programmes you will actually use. Check the delivery, installation and removal terms in writing, and confirm the warranty and return policy. If you are replacing a working machine, measure its space and note its connections, because the same ones usually apply. The right washing machine is the one that fits your home, your household and your week, and it is worth an hour of measuring to find it.

Questions people ask

What capacity do I need?

It depends on household size and how often you wash. The picker gives a starting point from your household size; if you often wash bulky items like bedding, consider going one size up.

What should I measure before buying?

The width, depth and height of the space, the door swing, and the route the machine will travel through your home. The measurement checklist below lists every step.

Does a bigger machine cost more to run?

A larger machine can use more water and energy per load, especially on small loads. Matching capacity to your household keeps running costs in check.

What if my old machine is still working?

Measure the space it sits in and note how it connects. The same measurements and connections usually apply to the new machine, which makes the swap simpler.

Should I check the energy label?

Yes. The label lets you compare machines on energy and water use before you buy, which matters more over the years than the purchase price alone.
