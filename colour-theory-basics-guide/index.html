Colour Theory Basics Guide

Colour theory is the set of rules that explains which colours sit well together and why. Once you know the colour wheel and the three classic harmonies, you can pick colours for a room or a design with confidence instead of guessing.

Some colour combinations feel right instantly, and others feel wrong even when you cannot say why. That feeling is not a mystery — it follows from where colours sit on the colour wheel and how our eyes read contrast. Colour theory turns that feeling into a small set of rules anyone can use.

This guide covers the colour wheel, warm and cool colours, and the three classic harmonies that designers have used for centuries. Use the picker below to see each harmony in action with a colour of your choice, then read the notes on using colour in a room and in a design.

Colours look different in different light and on different screens, so always test a colour in the place where it will be used before committing to it.

The colour wheel

The colour wheel arranges twelve colours in a circle in the order they appear in the rainbow, with smooth steps between them. Three of them are primary colours — red, yellow and blue — which cannot be made by mixing other colours. Mix two primaries and you get a secondary colour: red and yellow make orange, yellow and blue make green, and blue and red make purple.

Mix a primary with the secondary next to it and you get a tertiary colour, such as red-orange or blue-green. Together the primaries, secondaries and tertiaries make the twelve colours of the wheel. The wheel is useful because distance on the wheel tells you how colours will behave together: colours close together blend, and colours far apart contrast.

Artists have used the wheel since the 1700s, and the same wheel still sits behind modern design tools, paint charts and website palettes. You do not need to memorise it — you only need to know that opposites contrast, neighbours blend, and evenly spaced colours balance.

Warm and cool colours

One half of the wheel feels warm and the other half feels cool. Warm colours are the reds, oranges and yellows — the colours of fire and sunlight. Cool colours are the blues, greens and purples — the colours of water, shade and distance. Green sits on the border and can lean either way depending on whether it leans towards yellow or blue.

Warm colours tend to feel closer and more energetic, which is why they are popular in dining rooms and social spaces. Cool colours tend to feel further away and calmer, which is why they are common in bedrooms and bathrooms. Neither is better — the choice depends on the feeling you want the space or design to have.

A useful trick is to use mostly one temperature and add a small touch of the other. An all-cool room can feel cold, but a single warm accent — a cushion, a lamp, a picture frame — brings it to life. The same works in reverse for a warm room that feels too heavy.

The three classic harmonies

A colour harmony is a set of colours chosen by a rule on the wheel, and the three classic harmonies cover most situations. Complementary colours sit opposite each other — red and green, blue and orange, yellow and purple. They give the strongest contrast, so they are great for making one thing stand out, but they need a clear leader and a small accent, or they fight each other.

Analogous colours sit next to each other — blue, blue-green and green, for example. They share a common colour, so they blend smoothly and feel calm and natural. This is the easiest harmony to use well, and it suits rooms and designs where you want a gentle, restful feeling.

Triadic colours sit evenly spaced around the wheel, one third of the circle apart — red, yellow and blue are the classic example. The result is balanced and lively without the clash of a complementary pair. As with complementary colours, let one colour lead and keep the others in supporting roles.

Using colour in a room

A simple guide for rooms is to think in proportions: a main colour for most of the room, a second colour for about a third of it, and a small accent colour for the rest. The main colour usually goes on the walls, the second on larger furniture, and the accent on cushions, art and small objects. This keeps the room feeling planned rather than random.

Light changes everything. A colour that looks soft in a shop can look much stronger on a large wall, and a north-facing room makes colours look cooler than a south-facing one. Paint a large test patch and look at it morning and evening before you buy enough paint for the whole room.

If you are unsure, start with an analogous harmony for the walls and furniture and add one complementary accent. It is hard to go wrong with that combination, and you can always swap the accent later when you want a change.

Using colour in a design

The same rules work for posters, slides, websites and anything else you design. Pick one harmony, choose one colour to lead, and resist the urge to add more colours — three or four colours, used consistently, look far more professional than a rainbow. White, black and grey do not count against your total, and they give the eye somewhere to rest.

Contrast matters most where people need to read. Dark text on a light background is the easiest to read, and light text on a dark background works too, as long as the two are far apart in lightness. Two colours of similar lightness can look muddy together even if they are different hues, so check your design in greyscale: if the text is still easy to read, the contrast is good.

Finally, remember that colour carries meaning. In many cultures red suggests energy or urgency, blue suggests calm and trust, and green suggests nature and growth. Those associations are not rules, but they are worth keeping in mind when your design needs to send a message.

Questions people ask

What are the primary colours?

The primary colours are red, yellow and blue. They cannot be made by mixing other colours, and every other colour on the wheel is made by mixing them.

What is a complementary colour?

A complementary colour sits directly opposite another colour on the colour wheel. Red and green are complements, as are blue and orange, and yellow and purple.

What is the difference between analogous and triadic harmonies?

Analogous colours sit next to each other on the wheel and blend gently. Triadic colours sit evenly spaced around the wheel, one third of the circle apart, and give a balanced, lively mix.

Which colours make a room feel bigger?

Light, cool colours tend to make a room feel larger and airier, while dark, warm colours make it feel smaller and cosier. Pale blue, soft green and light grey are common choices for small rooms.

How many colours should a design use?

Three or four colours is a good limit for most designs, with one colour leading. White, black and grey do not count against the total and help the colours breathe.
