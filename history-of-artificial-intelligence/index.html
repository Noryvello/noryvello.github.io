A Short History of Artificial Intelligence

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

The history of artificial intelligence can be told in six milestones, from a question asked in 1950 to a chatbot launched in 2022. Each milestone changed what people believed machines could do. This guide walks through all six, in order, and explains why each one mattered.

Artificial intelligence feels very new, but its story stretches back to the middle of the last century. It is a story of big ideas, long quiet periods, and sudden breakthroughs. The pace was uneven: decades of slow progress, then a few moments that changed everything. Understanding those moments is the best way to understand where AI is today and why it arrived so suddenly in everyday life.

This guide tells the story through six milestones only: Turing's paper, the Dartmouth workshop, Deep Blue's chess win, the ImageNet breakthrough, the transformer paper, and the launch of ChatGPT. Six moments, each one a turning point, and together they explain how AI went from a philosopher's question to a tool in your pocket.

History is often told as a smooth line of progress, but AI's story had long stretches when little seemed to happen and funding and interest faded. This guide covers six milestones only, and it does not claim they are the whole story. They are the turning points that best explain how AI reached where it is today.

Why six moments tell the story

A full history of AI would fill a library, but a short history needs a different approach: pick the moments when the direction changed. Each of the six milestones in this guide did that. Turing's paper asked the founding question. The Dartmouth workshop gave the field its name. Deep Blue proved a machine could beat the best human at a thinking game. ImageNet proved deep learning worked. The transformer paper gave AI its most powerful architecture. ChatGPT put it all in front of everyone.

Between these moments there were long stretches of steady work: researchers building systems, testing ideas, and slowly improving results. Those stretches mattered, because without them the breakthroughs could not have happened. But the milestones are where the story turns, and they are the best landmarks for a beginner trying to understand how we got here.

It is also worth noting what the six milestones have in common. Each one took an idea that had been discussed in theory and showed it working in practice. A question became a paper, a paper became a field, a field became a chess champion, a method became a winning system, an architecture became the foundation of modern AI, and a research model became a product used by millions. Theory to practice, again and again, is the rhythm of this history.

1950: Turing asks whether machines can think

The story begins with a question. In 1950, the British mathematician Alan Turing published a paper called "Computing Machinery and Intelligence", in which he asked a bold question: can machines think? At the time, computers were enormous, slow machines that filled rooms, and the idea of a thinking machine sounded like science fiction. Turing took the question seriously and proposed a way to think about it.

His proposal is now known as the imitation game. Imagine a person chatting by text with two hidden partners, one human and one machine. If the person cannot tell which is which, then, Turing argued, it is reasonable to say the machine is thinking, at least in the sense that matters for the test. The game shifted the debate from philosophy to something practical: instead of arguing about what thinking means, test whether a machine can behave indistinguishably from a human in conversation.

Turing's paper did not build a thinking machine. What it did was frame the question in a way that researchers could work with. It gave the new field its founding puzzle and its first test. The paper is still discussed today, not because it answered the question, but because it asked it so clearly that the question has guided the field ever since.

1956: The Dartmouth workshop names the field

Six years later, a small group of researchers gathered for a summer workshop at Dartmouth College to discuss the idea of machines that could think, learn, and solve problems. The meeting was modest in size, but its effect was lasting: it gave the field its name. The term "artificial intelligence" was coined for the workshop, and it stuck.

The workshop mattered because it turned a scattered set of ideas into a field with a name and a community. Before Dartmouth, researchers interested in thinking machines worked in isolation, in different disciplines, with no shared label for what they were doing. After Dartmouth, they had a name, a set of shared questions, and a reason to meet and compare results. The field of AI, as an organised area of research, dates from this workshop.

The optimism at Dartmouth was high. The researchers believed that thinking machines might be only a short time away, and they set ambitious goals. That optimism turned out to be premature: the problems were far harder than anyone expected, and progress was slow. But the field they named survived the disappointments, and the name they chose is the one the whole world uses today.

1997: Deep Blue beats Kasparov

Chess had long been seen as a test of human intelligence. It needs planning, memory, pattern recognition, and judgment under pressure, and for decades the best human players seemed untouchable by machines. That changed in 1997, when Deep Blue, a chess-playing computer built by IBM, beat the reigning world champion, Garry Kasparov, in a match.

Deep Blue did not think the way Kasparov thought. It won by searching through possible moves at enormous speed, evaluating positions with programmed rules and a vast memory of past games. Kasparov played by intuition and experience; Deep Blue played by brute-force search. The contrast was part of the story: the machine won not by being human-like, but by being machine-like, and doing it better than anyone had expected.

The win made headlines around the world and changed how the public saw AI. A machine had beaten the best human mind at a game that symbolised intelligence itself. It did not mean machines were intelligent in a general sense, Deep Blue could play chess and nothing else, but it proved that a narrow AI system could outperform the best human in a complex thinking task. That proof reshaped expectations about what machines could do.

2012: Deep learning wins at ImageNet

For many years, the best way to teach a computer to recognise images was to hand-design the features: a programmer would decide which visual clues mattered, such as edges or textures, and the system would use those clues. Deep learning offered a different approach: let a neural network with many layers learn the features for itself from training data. For a long time, the two approaches were competitive, and it was not clear which would win.

That changed in 2012, at ImageNet, a contest in which systems competed to recognise objects in photographs. A deep neural network entered the contest and won by a clear margin, beating the older hand-designed methods decisively. The result was a shock to the field: the layered, learning approach was not just slightly better, it was much better, and the gap was obvious to everyone watching.

The ImageNet win is often called the moment deep learning arrived. After it, researchers and companies turned to deep neural networks for image recognition, speech, and language, and the results improved rapidly. The modern wave of AI, including the chatbots and image generators people use today, traces directly back to this breakthrough. It was the moment the method behind modern AI proved itself.

2017: The transformer paper

The next turning point was a paper published in 2017, titled "Attention Is All You Need". It introduced a new kind of neural network architecture called the transformer. The transformer was designed for language, and it solved a problem that had limited earlier systems: how to keep track of the relationships between words that are far apart in a sentence.

The key idea was attention: a mechanism that lets the system focus on the most relevant words when processing each new word, no matter how far apart they are in the text. Earlier systems processed words in order, one after another, and struggled to remember what came at the start of a long sentence. The transformer could look at the whole sentence at once and weigh which words mattered most for each decision. That made it far better at understanding and generating language.

The transformer became the foundation of modern AI. Large language models, the systems behind today's chatbots, are built on transformer architecture. So are many image and video systems. The paper was a research publication, not a product, but its influence was enormous: it gave the field the architecture that powers almost everything people now think of as AI.

2022: ChatGPT brings AI to everyone

The final milestone is the one most people remember personally. In 2022, OpenAI launched ChatGPT, a chatbot built on a large language model, and made it available to anyone with an internet connection. People could open a chat window, type a question, and get a fluent, detailed answer. It was the first time a powerful AI system had been put directly in front of the general public.

The response was immediate and enormous. Millions of people tried it in the first days, and it became a topic of conversation everywhere: in schools, in offices, in the news. People used it to write emails, explain concepts, draft stories, and answer questions. They also discovered its flaws: it invented facts, made confident mistakes, and sometimes produced strange answers. The public learned about AI's strengths and weaknesses at the same time, by using it.

ChatGPT's launch changed AI from a research topic into a daily reality. Before it, AI was mostly something engineers and researchers worked with. After it, AI was something everyone had an opinion about, because everyone had tried it. The launch did not create the technology, the transformer, the training methods, and the deep learning behind it had been building for years, but it made the technology visible, and that visibility changed everything.

What the six milestones show together

Look at the six milestones together and a pattern emerges. Each one took something that existed in theory and made it real: a question became a paper, a paper became a field, a field became a chess champion, a method became a winning system, an architecture became the foundation of modern AI, and a research model became a product for everyone. The history of AI is the history of ideas becoming things.

The pattern also shows how slow and then how fast progress can be. From Turing's question to Deep Blue's win, the story spans nearly five decades, with long stretches when little seemed to change. From ImageNet to ChatGPT, the story spans a single decade, and the pace of change was dizzying. The long quiet periods were not wasted: they were when the ideas, the data, and the computing power were being built, quietly, until they were ready to combine.

Finally, the six milestones show that AI's story is not finished. Each milestone was followed by new questions, new problems, and new debates. Turing asked whether machines can think, and the question is still open. ChatGPT showed what a language model can do, and it raised new questions about truth, work, and creativity. The history of AI is still being written, and the next milestone may already be on its way.

Questions people ask

When did AI begin?

The story usually starts in 1950, when Alan Turing published a paper asking whether machines can think, and in 1956, when a workshop at Dartmouth College gave the field its name. People had dreamed of thinking machines long before that, but those two moments mark the start of AI as a field of study.

What was the Dartmouth workshop?

It was a small summer workshop held in 1956 at Dartmouth College, where a group of researchers gathered to discuss the idea of machines that could think. The meeting is remembered as the birthplace of AI as a named field, and the term artificial intelligence was coined for it.

Why was Deep Blue's win important?

Because chess was long seen as a test of human intelligence, and a machine beating the reigning world champion in 1997 showed that computers could outperform the best human minds at a complex thinking task. It brought AI to the front pages and changed how the public saw what machines could do.

What changed in 2012?

In 2012, a deep neural network won the ImageNet image-recognition contest by a clear margin, beating the older methods that had dominated the field. The result convinced researchers that deep learning was the way forward, and it set off the wave of progress that led to modern chatbots and image tools.

Why was ChatGPT's launch a turning point?

Because it put a powerful language model in front of ordinary people for the first time. Before 2022, AI was mostly something researchers and engineers worked with. After ChatGPT launched, anyone could open a chat window and talk to an AI, and the whole world started using and debating the technology.
