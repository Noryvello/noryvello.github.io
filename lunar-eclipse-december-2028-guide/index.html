The eclipse at a glance

A total lunar eclipse takes place on Sunday 31 December 2028. During totality the Moon sits fully inside the Earth's shadow for 71 minutes 20 seconds, turning a deep red or coppery colour. The eclipse belongs to Saros series 125, a family of eclipses linked by a repeating cycle, and it is fully visible over eastern Europe, Asia and Australia. The same night is also a Blue Moon, the second full moon of December, and it is New Year's Eve, which makes this one of the most memorable nights of the year for skywatchers.

A lunar eclipse can only happen at a full moon, when the Sun, Earth and Moon line up with the Earth in the middle. Most full moons miss the alignment, because the Moon's orbit is tilted, and the shadow passes above or below the Moon. When the line-up is exact, the Earth's shadow falls on the Moon, and an eclipse follows.

What a total lunar eclipse is

During a lunar eclipse the Moon passes through the shadow the Earth casts in sunlight. The shadow has two parts: a faint outer shadow, called the penumbra, and a dark inner shadow, called the umbra. When the Moon is fully inside the umbra, the eclipse is total, and the Moon does not vanish: it glows red. The colour comes from sunlight bending through the Earth's atmosphere, which strips out the blue light and lets the red through. The same effect colours a sunset, and during totality the Moon is lit by the light of every sunrise and sunset happening on Earth at once.

Totality on 31 December 2028 lasts 71 minutes 20 seconds. That is the heart of the eclipse, the stretch when the whole Moon is inside the dark shadow and at its reddest. Before and after totality the Moon moves through the partial phases, when only part of it is in shadow and a dark bite seems to be taken out of it. The whole event, from first contact to last, takes several hours, with totality as its centrepiece.

The three kinds of lunar eclipse

Not every lunar eclipse is total. There are three kinds, and the difference is how deep the Moon goes into the Earth's shadow.

  • Penumbral eclipse: the Moon passes only through the faint outer shadow. The dimming is slight, and a casual watcher might not notice it at all.
  • Partial eclipse: part of the Moon enters the dark inner shadow, while the rest stays lit. A dark curve creeps across the Moon and then retreats.
  • Total eclipse: the whole Moon enters the dark inner shadow, and the Moon turns red for as long as totality lasts.

The eclipse of 31 December 2028 is a total eclipse, the most dramatic kind, with over an hour of totality to watch.

Why this eclipse is special

Three things come together on the night of 31 December 2028. The first is the eclipse itself, a total lunar eclipse with a long totality. The second is the Blue Moon: the full moon of 31 December is the second full moon of the month, after the full moon of 2 December, which makes it a Blue Moon by the usual definition. The third is the calendar: the eclipse falls on New Year's Eve, so the year ends with the Moon turning red in the sky.

The eclipse is fully visible over eastern Europe, Asia and Australia, where the whole event can be seen from start to finish. Other parts of the world may see part of the eclipse, or none of it, depending on whether the Moon is above the horizon during the event. How to watch

A lunar eclipse is one of the easiest sky events to watch, because it needs no special equipment and is safe to look at with the naked eye. Unlike a solar eclipse, where looking at the Sun can damage your eyes, a lunar eclipse is just the Moon in shadow, and you can watch the whole thing directly. Binoculars will sharpen the view and bring out the colour, and a small telescope will show the shadow's edge moving across the Moon's face, but none of it is necessary.

Find a spot with a clear view of the sky and as little glare as you can manage. The Moon is bright, so city lights are less of a problem than they are for meteor watching, but a dark spot still gives the better view. Dress warmly: the eclipse runs for hours, and the best way to watch is to step outside at intervals, letting the change sink in each time. The red colour is deepest near the middle of totality, so if you can only watch one part, watch that.
