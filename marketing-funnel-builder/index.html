What a marketing funnel is

A marketing funnel is a picture of the path a stranger takes to become a customer. At the top, many people discover you. Some of them get interested. Fewer still consider buying. Fewer again buy. And a small number come back and buy again. The shape is a funnel because each step is narrower than the last — and that is normal. No business converts every visitor into a buyer, and a funnel that loses people at every step is not broken, it is honest.

The value of the funnel is not the shape, it is the comparison between steps. If 1,000 people discover you and 100 buy, your funnel converts 10 percent overall. But that single number hides the useful question: where did the 900 go? If 800 of them left between discover and interest, your message is not reaching the right people. If 800 left between consider and buy, your offer or your price is the problem. The funnel tells you where to look.

Every business has a funnel, whether it is written down or not. Writing it down turns a vague feeling — sales are slow — into a specific question — which step loses the most people? And a specific question has a specific fix.

What belongs in each stage

Discover is the first contact: someone sees your post, finds you in search, hears about you from a friend, or walks past your shop. The channel matters more than the message here, because you are reaching people who do not know you yet. The action is simple: they notice you. Count first visits, first views, or first enquiries — whichever you can actually see.

Interest is the second look: they visit your page, read about the product, watch a video, or ask a question. They are not buying yet, but they are paying attention. Consider is the serious step: they compare your offer with others, read the details, check the price, or talk to someone who bought before. Buy is the purchase itself. Repeat is the return: they buy again, book again, or bring a friend.

Each stage needs one channel and one action, written in your own words. Vague stages — "people find us somehow" — cannot be measured and cannot be fixed. If you cannot name the channel for a stage, that stage is a guess, and the funnel will show it: the numbers will not add up, and the leakiest stage will be the one you understand least.

How to measure each stage

Measure each stage with a number you can actually count. Discover: visitors to your page, views of your posts, or people who walk in. Interest: people who read the product page, watch the video to the end, or send a message. Consider: people who ask for a price, add something to a basket, or book a call. Buy: orders. Repeat: second orders, or people who return within a few months.

Use the same period for every stage — a typical month is the easiest — and count each person once per stage. Do not mix periods: discover from last month and buy from this month will give you a funnel that means nothing. And do not count the same person twice in one stage: ten visits from one person is one interested person, not ten.

If you cannot count a stage, estimate it honestly and mark it as an estimate. A funnel with one honest estimate is still useful; a funnel with five invented numbers is a drawing, not a tool. Over time, replace estimates with real counts — the funnel gets more honest the more you measure.

How to fix the leakiest stage first

The leakiest stage is the step where you lose the largest share of people — not the largest number, but the largest share. If 1,000 people discover you and 100 show interest, you lost 90 percent at that step. If 100 consider and 50 buy, you lost 50 percent there. The first leak is the bigger problem, even though the numbers look smaller further down.

Fix one stage at a time, starting with the leakiest. A leak between discover and interest usually means the wrong audience or an unclear message: the people who find you are not the people who want what you sell. A leak between interest and consider usually means the product page does not answer the real questions — price, delivery, what happens next. A leak between consider and buy often means friction: a hard checkout, a slow reply, or a price that surprises people at the last step.

A leak between buy and repeat means the first experience did not earn a second one: the product, the delivery, or the follow-up fell short. And if there is no repeat stage at all — no follow-up message, no reason to return — that is the easiest fix of all: ask buyers how it went, and give them a reason to come back.

Worked example: an online course funnel

Leila sells an online course in basic bookkeeping. In a typical month, 2,000 people discover her through search and social posts. Of those, 400 visit the course page and read it — 20 percent. Of those, 120 start the free trial lesson — 30 percent. Of those, 36 buy the full course — 30 percent. And 12 of the 36 buy her second course later — 33 percent repeat.

The leakiest stage is discover to interest: she loses 80 percent there. The builder's checklist tells her to look at her message and her audience: are the right people finding her, and does the first thing they see explain the course clearly? She rewrites the first lines of her page to say exactly who the course is for and what it teaches, and she stops posting in groups where nobody wants bookkeeping.

Two months later, discover is down to 1,600 — fewer people, but the right ones — and 480 now visit the course page: 30 percent instead of 20. The funnel got narrower at the top and wider further down, and sales rose from 36 to 43 a month. The funnel did not bring more people; it showed her where the real problem was, and fixing that one stage changed everything below it.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is no follow-up. A buyer who hears nothing after the sale has no reason to return, and a person who asked a question and got no reply will not ask twice. The second mistake is asking for the sale too early: a stranger who has just discovered you is not ready to buy, and a hard sell at the first step drives people away before they have had a chance to get interested.

The third mistake is no repeat stage at all. Many funnels stop at the purchase, as if the customer stops existing after paying. But a returning buyer costs far less to reach than a new one, and a buyer who returns is worth more than a buyer who buys once. The repeat stage is not an extra — it is where the profit usually lives.

The fourth mistake is measuring the wrong numbers: counting followers instead of buyers, or counting visits instead of enquiries. The fifth mistake is fixing the wrong stage — polishing the checkout when the real leak is at the top, or rewriting the headline when the real problem is a price nobody can find. The funnel exists to stop exactly that: fix the leakiest stage first, and only then move to the next.

Funnel vs campaign: what is the difference?

A funnel is the permanent path people take through your business. A campaign is a temporary push with one goal and one end date. The funnel is the road; the campaign is a journey on it. You need both, but they do different jobs, and confusing them leads to the wrong decisions.

A campaign brings people into the top of the funnel: it finds new strangers and gets them to discover you. But if the funnel itself is broken — if the product page does not answer questions, or the checkout is hard — then the campaign pours people into a funnel that leaks them all away. That is why fixing the funnel comes before running a big campaign: there is no point paying to bring people to a path that does not work.

Think of it this way: the funnel is what you build once and improve slowly, month after month. The campaign is what you run for a few weeks to fill it. A good funnel makes every campaign cheaper, because more of the people the campaign brings actually buy. And a good campaign shows you where the funnel needs work, because it sends enough people through it to see the leaks clearly.

What this builder cannot do

This builder cannot count your visitors for you. It works from the numbers you enter, and if those numbers are guesses, the funnel is a guess too — an organised one, but still a guess. It cannot see your website, your shop, or your messages, and it cannot tell you which of your buyers came from which channel unless you track that yourself.

It also cannot fix the funnel for you. It names the leakiest stage and gives you a checklist, but the work — rewriting the page, answering messages faster, following up with buyers — is yours. And it cannot promise a result: a funnel that converts 10 percent today might convert 8 percent next month, because audiences and seasons change. What it can do is show you, clearly and honestly, where your people go — and that is the information every good marketing decision starts from.

Questions people ask

What is a marketing funnel?

A marketing funnel is a picture of the path a stranger takes to become a customer, in five stages: discover, interest, consider, buy and repeat. Each stage is narrower than the last, because not everyone who discovers you will buy. The funnel shows you where people drop off, so you know which step to fix first.

How many stages should a funnel have?

Five is a good number for most small businesses: discover, interest, consider, buy and repeat. Fewer stages hide the useful detail — you cannot tell whether people leave because they are not interested or because the price puts them off. More stages add detail you cannot measure, and the funnel becomes a guess.

Where do most funnels leak?

The biggest leak is usually at the top, between discover and interest: most people who find you are not the right audience, or the first thing they see does not explain clearly what you sell. But every business is different, and the only way to know your own leakiest stage is to measure your own numbers — which is what this builder does.

Do I need a funnel for a small shop?

Yes, and a small shop needs it more than a big one, because every lost buyer matters more. A small shop's funnel is simple: people who walk past, people who walk in, people who ask about something, people who buy, people who come back. Writing it down takes ten minutes and shows you which step to fix first.

What is the difference between a funnel and a campaign?

A funnel is the permanent path people take through your business; a campaign is a temporary push with one goal and one end date. The campaign brings people into the top of the funnel. If the funnel is broken, the campaign pours people in and they leak away — so fix the funnel before you run a big campaign.

How do I measure each stage?

Use a number you can actually count, for the same period — a typical month — at every stage. Discover: visitors or views. Interest: people who read the product page or send a message. Consider: people who ask for a price or start a trial. Buy: orders. Repeat: second orders. Count each person once per stage, and mark estimates as estimates.
