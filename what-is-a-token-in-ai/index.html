What Is a Token in AI? (And Why It Affects Cost)

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

A token is a piece of text, often a whole word but sometimes only part of one, and it is the unit that AI language models read and write in. Roughly speaking, one token is about four characters of English text, though that is only an approximation. Token counts matter because they set both the cost of using an AI service and how much text a model can consider at once.

If you have used a chatbot, you have used tokens, whether you knew it or not. Every message you send is broken into tokens before the model reads it, and every answer is built from tokens before it reaches you. Tokens are the currency of language AI: they are how the model measures text, how long conversations are limited, and, for many services, how use is charged.

This guide explains what a token is, how words split into tokens, the rough rule for estimating token counts, and why tokens affect cost and context limits. No prices are given here, because prices change; what matters is understanding the mechanism, which does not change.

Token counts are approximate and vary by model and language. The rule of about four characters per token is a rough guide for English text, not an exact measure. For anything where the count matters, such as a long document or a paid service, check the count with the tool's own counter rather than guessing.

What a token is, in plain words

A token is the smallest piece of text that a language model works with. When you type a message, the model does not read it letter by letter, and it does not read it word by word. It reads it token by token. A token might be a whole word, like "cat", or part of a word, like "un" in "unbelievable", or a punctuation mark, like a comma. The model treats each token as one unit.

The reason for tokens is practical. Language models need a fixed vocabulary of units they can work with, and words alone would not do: there are too many words, including names, typos, and new words that appear every day. By breaking text into tokens, the model can handle any text, even words it has never seen, by combining familiar pieces. A word it has never met can be built from tokens it knows.

Think of tokens as the bricks of language AI. Just as a wall is built from bricks of different sizes, a message is built from tokens of different sizes. Some bricks are whole words, some are word parts, and some are single characters. The model reads the bricks in order and predicts the next brick, one at a time, until it has written its answer.

How words split into tokens

Short, common words are usually one token each. "The", "cat", "run", and "happy" are each a single token in most systems. Longer words are often split into pieces. "Unbelievable" might become three tokens: "un", "believ", and "able". The split follows patterns the system learned during training: common word parts become tokens of their own, because they appear in many words.

Names and unusual words are split more aggressively. A rare surname might be broken into several small pieces, because the system has no single token for it. Numbers are often split digit by digit or in small groups. Punctuation usually gets a token each, and spaces are often attached to the token that follows them, so " hello" might be one token including the space.

The exact split depends on the model. Different models use different tokenisers, the tools that do the splitting, so the same sentence can produce a different number of tokens in different systems. That is why token counts are always approximate when you move between tools: the unit itself is defined by the tool, not by the text.

The rough rule: about four characters per token

For English text, a useful rough rule is that one token is about four characters. This is an approximation, not a measurement. It works well for ordinary English prose, where most words are short and common. A sentence of forty characters is roughly ten tokens. A paragraph of four hundred characters is roughly a hundred tokens. The rule gives you a quick sense of scale without needing a counter.

The rule is approximate because the split depends on the words. Text full of short, common words will have fewer tokens per character than the rule suggests, because each word is one token. Text full of long, technical, or unusual words will have more tokens, because each word splits into several pieces. Code, with its symbols and long names, often uses more tokens per character than plain prose.

Use the rule for estimating, not for accounting. If you want to know whether a document will fit in a conversation, or roughly how much of a limit you have used, dividing the character count by four gives a good enough answer. If the exact count matters, use the tool's own token counter, because only that counter knows how that particular model splits your particular text.

Why tokens matter for cost

Many AI services charge by the token. When you use an AI service through its programming interface, the service counts the tokens in your prompt and the tokens in the answer, and the charge is based on those counts. Longer prompts and longer answers use more tokens, and therefore cost more. The exact rates vary between services and change over time, so this guide gives no figures; the mechanism is what matters.

This is why prompt length has a real consequence for people who build on AI services. A prompt that includes a long document costs more than a short question, because the document adds many tokens. An answer that runs long costs more than a short one, because the model generates more tokens. People who use AI services heavily learn to write prompts that are as short as they can be while still getting good answers.

For casual chatbot use in a web browser or app, tokens are usually invisible: the service handles the counting, and you see only a usage limit or a plan tier. But the tokens are still there underneath, and understanding them explains why long conversations sometimes hit limits, and why pasting a very long document into a chat can fail. The limit is measured in tokens, not in words or pages.

Why tokens matter for context limits

Every language model has a context window: the maximum amount of text, measured in tokens, that it can consider at once. The window includes everything the model needs to see: your prompt, the conversation so far, and the answer it is writing. When the total grows beyond the window, the model cannot see it all, and the oldest parts are dropped.

This is why long conversations sometimes go strange. A chatbot that was following your discussion perfectly may suddenly seem to forget what you said at the start. It has not forgotten in the human sense; the early messages have simply fallen out of its context window, pushed out by newer tokens. Starting a fresh conversation restores the model's focus, because the window is empty again.

The context window also limits how much you can paste into a single prompt. A long document, a large spreadsheet, or a lengthy article may exceed the window on its own. When that happens, the model can only see part of it, and its answers will be based on that part. Breaking long material into smaller pieces, and asking about one piece at a time, works better than pasting everything at once.

How to use fewer tokens

Using fewer tokens is mostly a matter of writing shorter, clearer prompts. Cut the filler words, remove repeated instructions, and say what you need in as few words as will do the job. A prompt that says "Summarise this in three bullet points" uses fewer tokens than a paragraph explaining why you want a summary, and it usually gets a better answer too, because the instruction is clearer.

For long documents, summarise before you paste. Instead of pasting an entire article, paste the sections that matter for your question. If you need the model to work with the whole document, split it into parts and ask about one part at a time, then ask for a combined answer. This uses the context window efficiently and usually produces better results than a single overloaded prompt.

Finally, keep conversations focused. Each message in a long chat adds tokens to the window, and old messages eventually get pushed out. When you start a new topic, start a new conversation. You will use fewer tokens, the model will have a clearer context, and the answers will be better. Short, focused chats are the most token-efficient way to use a chatbot.

Tokens in different languages

Tokenisation is not the same for every language. English, with its large amount of training text, is split efficiently: common words become single tokens, and the four-characters rule works well. Languages with less training text are often split into many more tokens for the same meaning, because the system has fewer familiar word parts to work with.

This has real consequences. A message in a language that tokenises inefficiently uses more of the context window, and where use is charged by the token, it costs more for the same content. The difference can be large. It is one of the quieter inequalities in AI: the same service, the same question, but a different token count depending on the language you write in.

The situation is improving as models are trained on more languages, but it is worth knowing about. If you work in more than one language, you may notice that one language hits limits faster than another, or that answers in one language feel more constrained. Tokens are the reason, and understanding them helps you work around the limits, for example by writing the key parts of a prompt in the language the model handles most efficiently.

Questions people ask

Is a token the same as a word?

Not exactly. A token is a piece of text, and it is often a whole word, but it can also be part of a word or a punctuation mark. Short, common words are usually one token each. Long or unusual words are often split into several tokens.

How many tokens is a typical message?

It depends on the length. A short question uses only a few tokens, while a long document can use many thousands. As a rough guide, one token is about four characters of English text, but that is approximate and varies with the words used.

Why does the same text cost different amounts in different languages?

Because tokenisation depends on the language. Languages with large amounts of training text are split efficiently into few tokens. Languages with less training data are often split into many more tokens for the same meaning, which uses more of the context window and costs more where use is charged by the token.

What is a context window?

The context window is the maximum amount of text, measured in tokens, that a model can consider at once. It includes your prompt, the conversation so far, and the answer being written. When a conversation grows longer than the window, the oldest parts are dropped from the model's view.

Can I see how many tokens my text uses?

Some AI tools show token counts, and some providers offer a free token counter tool online. For a rough estimate, divide the number of characters in your English text by four. The result is approximate, but it is close enough for planning.
