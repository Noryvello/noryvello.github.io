Start a Podcast Checklist

A podcast needs a format, a quiet place to record and a plan for episodes, not a studio. This checklist covers format, recording setup, episode planning and publishing in general terms, with a progress counter.

A podcast is one of the simplest shows to start: it is a voice, a subject, and a recording. There is no camera to worry about and no set to build. What a podcast does need is a clear format, so listeners know what to expect, and a little planning, so the first episodes arrive on a steady rhythm instead of stalling after one. This checklist walks through the decisions in order, from the shape of the show to the first published episode.

This checklist is general guidance, not a recommendation of any product or service. Recording and publishing tools change often, so check the current guidance for the tools you choose.

How to use this checklist

Work through the list in order, ticking each step as you finish it. The counter at the top of the checklist shows your progress. If a step does not apply to your show, tick it and move on. The goal is a first episode that is recorded and published, not a first episode that is flawless. Your later episodes will be better, and that is how it should be.

Format: the shape of your show

Every podcast has a format, even if the host never names it. The simplest is one person talking about a subject, sometimes with notes. Another common shape is a conversation between two hosts, or a host interviewing a guest. A third is a short, focused episode that covers one idea or one question. Each shape suits a different kind of subject and a different kind of host, so pick the one that fits how you like to talk.

Decide on the length of your episodes before you record. Short episodes are easier to plan, record and edit, and they suit listeners with little time. Longer episodes suit deep subjects and relaxed conversations. Either way, pick a length you can fill without padding and keep roughly the same from episode to episode, so listeners know what to expect.

Choose a name that is easy to say and easy to remember, and check that a similar name is not already in use by another show in the same area. Write a short description of the show in one or two sentences: what it is about, and who it is for. A clear description helps a new listener decide in seconds whether to press play.

Recording setup

You do not need a studio. You need a quiet room, a microphone of some kind, and a way to record. A small room with soft furnishings — curtains, a rug, a sofa — sounds better than a large empty room, because hard surfaces bounce sound around and make a recording echo. Close windows and doors, turn off anything that hums or buzzes, and record at a quiet time of day if you can.

Record a short test before your first real session. Speak at your normal volume, from your normal distance, and listen back with headphones. The test reveals problems you would never notice while recording: a hum from a nearby appliance, a neighbour's television, the sound of the room itself. Fix what you can hear, then record again. A little time spent on the test saves a lot of frustration later.

Speak clearly and at a natural pace. Sit comfortably, keep water nearby, and keep your notes where you can see them without rustling paper near the microphone. If you stumble, pause for a moment and start the sentence again; the pause makes the mistake easy to find and remove later.

Episode plan

Plan the first episode in short notes: the subject, the main points in order, and how it ends. A plan keeps you on track and stops the episode wandering. It does not need to be a word-for-word script. Most podcasters work from a few bullet points and speak naturally around them, which sounds more relaxed than reading aloud.

Plan the next two episodes as well, even if only as rough ideas. Having a short list stops the stall that follows many first episodes, when the excitement fades and the second recording feels like a mountain. Decide how often you will publish, and pick a rhythm you can keep. A new episode every week or every two weeks, kept up steadily, is a strong start for most new shows.

Publishing steps

Publishing a podcast means making the audio available where listeners can find it, in general terms through a hosting service that distributes the show to listening apps. Before you publish, listen to the finished episode once from start to finish, checking the sound, the pace, and that nothing was cut off at the start or end. Write a short episode title and a brief description that says what the episode covers.

After the first episode is out, share it with a few people whose opinion you trust, and listen to their feedback with an open mind. Early listeners often notice things the host cannot hear: a part that dragged, a section that needed explaining, a moment that worked well. Use what you learn in the next episode.

Keeping a show going

The hardest part of podcasting is the second month, when the excitement of launching has faded and the episodes still need recording. A few habits help. Keep a running list of episode ideas and add to it whenever something catches your interest. Record when you have something to say rather than forcing an episode to fill a slot. Batch your work where you can: recording two episodes in one session, for example, gives you a buffer for a busy week. And listen to your own older episodes now and then. They show how far you have come, and they often spark ideas for new episodes. A show grows slowly, one episode at a time, and that is normal.

Questions people ask

Do I need expensive equipment to start a podcast?

No. A quiet room, a basic microphone and a way to record are enough to start. Listeners care far more about clear sound and an episode that keeps its promise than about expensive gear.

How long should podcast episodes be?

As long as the subject needs, and no longer. Short episodes suit focused ideas and busy listeners, while longer episodes suit deep subjects and relaxed conversations. Pick a length you can fill without padding.

Should I podcast alone or with a co-host?

Both work. Solo episodes are simpler to plan and record. A co-host adds conversation and energy, but needs scheduling and a shared sense of direction. Choose the shape that fits how you like to talk.

How do I choose episode topics?

Start with questions your listeners actually ask, subjects you know well, and ideas from your own experience. Keep a running list and add to it whenever something catches your interest.

Where do people find my podcast?

Listeners find shows through listening apps, search, and recommendations from people they trust. A clear show name, a plain description and a steady publishing rhythm all help new listeners discover your show.
