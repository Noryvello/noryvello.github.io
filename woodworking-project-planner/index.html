Woodworking Project Planner

A woodworking project goes smoothly when the planning happens before the first cut, not during it. This guide walks you through the whole plan, from measuring your space and choosing wood to the cut order, assembly and finish.

Woodworking tools can cause serious injury. Wear eye and ear protection and a dust mask, read each tool manual before use, keep your hands clear of blades, and never rush a cut.

Start with the finished piece in mind

Every good project starts with a clear picture of the finished piece. Before you buy wood or touch a saw, decide exactly what you are making, where it will live, and how big it must be. Measure the space it will go in, not once but twice, and write the numbers down. A shelf that is five millimetres too wide is a problem you discover at the worst moment.

Sketch the piece, even roughly. A sketch forces you to answer questions that are easy to skip: how thick is the top, how are the legs joined, where do the screws go, and what happens at the corners? You do not need drawing skill. A simple front view and a side view, with the main dimensions written on them, will do. If you are copying a piece you like, measure it and sketch it the same way.

Then ask what the piece has to do. A bookshelf carries weight, so its shelves need to be thick and well supported. A small box needs neat corners more than strength. A garden bench must survive rain. The job of the piece decides the wood, the joints and the finish, so settle the job first and the choices follow.

Choosing your wood

Wood falls into two broad groups. Softwood, such as pine and spruce, is light, cheap and easy to cut, and it is the sensible choice for a first project. Hardwood, such as oak, ash and walnut, is heavier, harder and more expensive, with a finer finish, and it suits pieces that will be handled every day. Plywood and MDF are sheet materials made from wood, and they are useful for panels, shelves and painted pieces.

Buy wood that is dry and straight. Sight along each board before you buy it: if it twists or bows, leave it on the pile. Check the ends for splits and the faces for deep knots where you plan to cut or join. A board that looks straight in the shop and bends at home is usually wood that was not dry, so buy from a supplier you trust and let the wood rest in your workshop for a few days before you cut it.

Think about the finish before you choose. If you plan to paint, a cheap softwood or MDF is fine, because the paint hides the grain. If you want to see the wood, choose a hardwood or a clear pine and a clear finish. Staining softwood to look like hardwood is possible, but the result is rarely as good as using the real thing.

The materials list: what to list and how to count it

A materials list is the heart of the plan. It lists every piece of wood, every screw, every length of dowel and every tin of finish, with sizes and quantities. Write it from your sketch, part by part, and count each piece as you go. A shelf unit, for example, is two sides, three shelves, a back panel, and the screws and brackets to hold it together.

Add a little extra to every quantity. Wood is sold in standard lengths, and a cut list that fits exactly on the boards you buy is rare. Buy one spare board, or a little extra length, so a mistake does not send you back to the shop. The same goes for screws and glue: running out halfway through an assembly is a small disaster that a spare box prevents.

  • List every wooden part with its finished size: length, width and thickness.

Tools you actually need

A first project needs fewer tools than the catalogues suggest. You need a way to measure, a way to mark, a way to cut, a way to smooth, a way to join and a way to finish. A tape measure, a pencil, a square, a hand saw or a circular saw, a block plane or sandpaper, a drill, a screwdriver and a brush will take you through most first projects.

Buy tools in the order you need them, and buy the best you can afford for the tools you use most. A good handsaw and a sharp plane will serve you for years. Power tools save time, but they also demand respect: a circular saw or a router can do serious damage in a second of inattention, so read the manual, practise on scrap wood, and never work when you are tired.

Borrow before you buy. A tool you use once a year is a good thing to borrow from a friend, a neighbour or a tool library. Hire is another option for big tools such as a thickness planer. And keep your tools sharp: a dull blade is more dangerous than a sharp one, because it needs force, and force slips.

Planning the cut order

The cut order is the sequence in which you cut your parts from the boards. Plan it on paper before you cut anything. The basic rule is simple: cut the longest pieces first, then the shorter ones from what is left. That way a mistake on a short piece costs you a small offcut, not a long board.

Mark every piece before you cut it. Write the part name and the finished size on the wood in pencil, and mark which face is the outside. Cut just outside the line and trim to it, rather than cutting on the line and hoping. After each cut, check the piece against your sketch before you move on. A small error early in the cut order grows into a big one by assembly.

  • Cut the longest parts first, then the shorter ones from the offcuts.

Joinery basics: how the pieces hold together

Joinery is how the parts of a piece hold together. The simplest joints are also the strongest for a beginner: screws and glue. A glued and screwed butt joint, where one board meets the end of another, is strong enough for most first projects if the screws are the right length and the parts are clamped while the glue dries.

Dowels add strength and alignment. A dowel joint uses short wooden pins set into matching holes in both parts, and it is a good next step after screws. A pocket-hole joint, made with a simple jig, hides the screws and is popular for frames and cabinets. Traditional joints such as mortise and tenon are beautiful and strong, but they take practice, so leave them until your second or third project.

Whatever joint you choose, dry fit it first. Put the parts together without glue and check that they meet cleanly and the piece is square. If a joint gaps or twists, fix it now, while the glue is still in the bottle. Glue hides small gaps badly and fixes nothing.

Assembly: dry fit first, then glue and clamp

Assembly day should be calm, because the planning is already done. Start by laying out every part in the order it goes together, with the fixings beside them. Do a full dry fit: assemble the whole piece without glue, check it is square by measuring the diagonals, and check that doors and drawers fit if there are any.

Then glue and clamp one joint at a time. Spread a thin, even layer of glue, bring the parts together, and tighten the clamps just enough to close the joint. Too much pressure squeezes out all the glue and starves the joint. Wipe away the squeeze-out with a damp cloth before it dries, because dried glue shows under a finish.

Let the glue dry fully before you move the piece or load it. Most wood glues need at least an hour in clamps and a full day to reach full strength, and the label gives the times for the glue you are using. Rushing this step is the most common cause of a wobbly finished piece.

Sanding and finishing

Sanding is the step that turns a built piece into a finished one. Work through the grits in order, from coarse to fine: a rough 80 or 100 grit to smooth the cuts, then 150, then 220 for the final pass. Sand with the grain, not across it, and wipe the dust away between grits so you can see what you are doing.

The finish protects the wood and shows it off. Oil brings out the grain and is easy to repair, but it needs recoating over time. Varnish gives a hard, protective coat and suits tables and pieces that get wet. Paint hides the wood entirely and suits MDF and cheaper softwood. Whatever you choose, try it on a test piece first, and follow the tin for drying times.

Sand lightly between coats with a fine grit, and wipe the dust away. Two or three thin coats always look better than one thick one, which runs and drips. Work in a clean, dust-free corner if you can, and give the final coat a full day to harden before you use the piece.

Safety habits for saws and dust

Safety is not a separate chapter in woodworking. It is part of every step, and the habits below belong in every project from the first cut to the last coat.

  • Wear safety glasses every time you cut, drill or sand. Your eyes do not heal the way a cut finger does.

If a cut feels wrong, stop. Step back, check the setup, and start again. The wood will wait. Your fingers will not grow back.

A first-project plan: a simple wall shelf

Here is the whole method applied to one small project: a simple wall shelf, about 60 centimetres wide, with two brackets. It is a good first piece because it uses one board, a few screws and a single finish, and it teaches measuring, cutting, sanding and fixing to a wall.

  1. Measure the wall and choose the shelf length. Mark the bracket positions on the wall with a level.

That is a complete project, planned end to end: sketch, materials, cut order, assembly, finish and safety. The next project will be bigger, but the method will be exactly the same.

Questions people ask

What is the best first woodworking project?

A simple wall shelf, a small box or a step stool are all good first projects. They use a few parts, simple joints and one finish, and they teach measuring, cutting, sanding and assembly without overwhelming you. Pick something you will actually use, because a useful finished piece is the best reward for the work.

How do I choose between hardwood and softwood?

Choose softwood, such as pine, for a first project: it is cheap, light and easy to cut, so mistakes cost little. Choose hardwood, such as oak, when the piece will be handled every day and you want a fine finish. Plywood suits flat panels and shelves, and MDF suits painted pieces.

What tools do I need to start woodworking?

You need a way to measure, mark, cut, smooth, join and finish: a tape measure, a pencil, a square, a saw, sandpaper, a drill, a screwdriver and a brush will cover most first projects. Buy tools in the order you need them, borrow the ones you will rarely use, and keep your blades sharp.

How do I avoid tear-out when cutting?

Tear-out is the splintering where a blade leaves the wood. Cut with the good face up on a table saw and the good face down with a hand-held circular saw, use a sharp blade with fine teeth, and support the wood close to the cut. Scoring the cut line lightly with a knife before you saw also helps.

How long should glue dry before I remove the clamps?

Most wood glues need at least an hour in clamps, and a full day to reach full strength. The label on your glue gives the exact times, and cold or damp conditions slow the cure. Do not move or load the piece until the glue has had its full time.

Do I need to sand between coats of finish?

Yes, a light sand with a fine grit between coats gives the next coat something to grip and removes dust and small bumps. Wipe the dust away before the next coat. Two or three thin coats always look better than one thick coat, which runs and drips.
