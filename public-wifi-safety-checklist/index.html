Public Wi-Fi Safety Checklist

This checklist walks you through staying safer on public Wi-Fi in three parts: before you connect, while you are connected, and after you disconnect. Tick each step as you go, and use the risky-action explainer to see why some everyday actions are best left for home.

Public Wi-Fi is convenient, and most of the time nothing goes wrong. The risk is not that every cafe network is dangerous; it is that an open network is shared with strangers, and you cannot see who else is on it or what the network itself is doing. A little preparation turns most of that risk into something you can manage.

The checklist below is built around three moments: what to do before you connect, what to avoid while you are connected, and what to tidy up after you disconnect. Work through it in order the first time, and it quickly becomes a habit you barely have to think about.

General safety information only. No checklist can make a public network fully safe, and this page is not security advice for any specific situation. If something about a network feels wrong, disconnect and ask the venue.

Why public Wi-Fi needs extra care

On your home network, you know who controls the router and who else uses it. On a public network, you know neither. Anyone in the room can be on the same network, and a network with a familiar name is not always the one it claims to be. That is why the strongest habit is simply to do less that matters on public Wi-Fi: browse, read and watch, and save the sensitive tasks for a network you trust.

The second habit is to check what you are connecting to. Ask staff for the exact network name rather than guessing from the list on your screen, and be wary of a network that asks for unusual details before it lets you in. A genuine venue network asks little and explains itself plainly.

Good habits that travel with you

The habits that protect you on public Wi-Fi are the same ones that protect you everywhere else. Keep your software updated, use a different password for each important account, and turn on two-factor authentication wherever it is offered. These three steps do more than any single checklist item.

Finally, trust your instincts. If a network behaves strangely, if a page you know looks wrong, or if you are asked for details that do not fit the situation, disconnect. You can always reconnect later or find another way to do what you need.

Questions people ask

Is public Wi-Fi ever safe to use?

For everyday browsing and reading, public Wi-Fi is usually fine, especially on sites that load over a secure connection. The risk grows when you sign in to accounts, enter payment details, or send personal documents, so save those tasks for a network you trust.

Should I use a VPN on public Wi-Fi?

A VPN adds a layer of protection by encrypting the traffic between your device and the VPN service. It helps, but it is not a magic shield: you still need to be careful about which sites you trust and what details you share.

What should I never do on public Wi-Fi?

Avoid banking, entering card details, signing in to accounts with sensitive personal information, and downloading files from unfamiliar sources. If a task involves money or identity documents, wait until you are on a trusted network.

Is a password-protected hotel network safer than an open one?

A password keeps casual passers-by off the network, which helps, but every guest shares the same password, so it is still a shared network. Treat it with the same care as any public Wi-Fi.

What should I do after using public Wi-Fi?

Forget the network so your device does not rejoin it automatically, turn Wi-Fi off if you no longer need it, and glance at your important accounts for any sign-in you do not recognise. If you typed a password on the network, change it.
