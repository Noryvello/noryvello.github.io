How to Spot Phishing Messages

A phishing message is a fake email, text, or chat message that tries to trick you into clicking a link, sharing a code, or handing over personal details. This guide shows you the red flags to look for, how to check a sender and a link before you act, and what to do if you already clicked. The checks take less than a minute, and they work for email, SMS, and chat messages alike.

A phishing message pretends to be from a bank, a delivery company, a government office, or someone you know. Its goal is simple: get you to click a link, open a file, or share something you would not normally share. The message may look polished, use a real company logo, and even address you by name — none of that proves it is genuine.

Phishing arrives through email, SMS, and chat apps, and the tricks are the same in every channel. Most phishing messages share the same handful of red flags, and once you know them, they are easy to spot.

This guide gives general advice about spotting phishing messages. It is not incident-specific security advice. If you think your accounts or money are at risk, contact your bank or the service involved directly through their official channels.

The urgency and fear tricks

Almost every phishing message tries to rush you. The sender wants you to act before you think, so the message creates pressure: your account will be closed, a payment will be taken, a parcel will be returned, or you will miss a deadline. The clock is always ticking, and the message often says so in capital letters or with several exclamation marks.

Typical pressure lines include:

  • Your account will be locked within 24 hours unless you verify it now.
  • We tried to deliver your parcel twice. Pay the redelivery fee today or it will be returned.
  • Unusual activity was found on your account. Confirm your identity immediately.

Real organisations do send urgent messages sometimes, but they rarely demand that you act through a link in the message itself. A genuine bank or delivery company will let you check the same information by opening their app or typing their web address into your browser yourself. If a message makes your heart beat faster, treat that feeling as a warning sign, not as a reason to click.

How to check a sender

Start with who the message claims to be from, then check whether the details match. In an email, look at the sender name and the full sender address shown by your mail app — the name on display can say anything, so the address behind it is what matters. A message claiming to be from your bank but sent from a free mailbox or a misspelled domain is a strong red flag.

In a text message, look at the sender number or name shown at the top. Delivery companies and banks usually send from a short code or a named sender, not from a random mobile number. If the message says it is from a company but arrives from an ordinary-looking number, be suspicious. The same applies to chat apps: check the profile, the number, and whether you have spoken to this contact before. A new account with no photo and no history that suddenly asks for a favour is a classic sign of a fake.

Also ask yourself whether the message fits. Did you order a parcel? Do you have an account with that bank? If the answer is no, the message is almost certainly not for you. Scammers send the same message to thousands of people and rely on a few of them having a parcel on the way.

How to inspect a link without clicking it

Never click a link to find out where it goes. On a computer, hover your mouse over the link and read the address that appears at the bottom of the browser window. On a phone, press and hold the link until a preview of the address appears. Then read the address carefully, from right to left, because the part just before the first single slash is the real domain.

Look for these tricks:

  • A misspelled brand name, such as exampel.com instead of example.com — one letter swapped or missing.
  • Extra words added to a real name, such as secure-example.com or example-support.net, where the brand is only part of a longer name.
  • A real brand name buried in the middle, such as example.com.login-page.net — the real domain here is login-page.net, not example.com.
  • A link shortener hiding the destination entirely, so you cannot see where the link leads at all.

When in doubt, do not use the link. Open your browser, type the address you know, or open the official app, and check there instead. If the message is genuine, the same information will be waiting for you in your account.

One-time-code and parcel-delivery scams

Two scams are so common that they deserve their own section. The first is the one-time-code scam. A code sent to your phone is a key to your account, and no genuine company will ever ask you to share it. A scammer may call or message you, pretend to be from your bank or a support team, and ask for the code to verify your identity or to stop a fraudulent payment. The moment you share the code, the scammer can sign in as you. The rule is simple: a code is for your eyes only, and anyone asking for it is not who they claim to be.

The second is the parcel-delivery scam. You get a text saying a parcel could not be delivered and asking you to pay a small fee or confirm your address through a link. The link leads to a fake delivery page that asks for card details. Real delivery companies do not ask for payment through a text link, and they do not ask you to confirm your address by logging in. If you are expecting a parcel, track it through the shop or the delivery company's official app — never through a link in a text.

What to do if you already clicked

First, do not panic — and do not enter anything else on the page. Close the tab or the app, and do not download any file the page offers. If you only clicked and entered nothing, the risk is low, but keep an eye on your accounts for anything unusual.

If you entered a password, change it right away — on the real site, reached by typing the address yourself — and change it anywhere else you used the same password. If you entered card details, contact your bank straight away and ask them to watch the card or block it. If you shared a one-time code, sign in to the account, change the password, and check the recent activity and the recovery settings. Tell the real company what happened through their official contact channels, so they can flag the fake message.

How to report a message

Reporting a phishing message helps protect the next person who receives it. Most email apps have a 'Report phishing' or 'Report spam' option in the message menu — use it, and the message goes to your provider's abuse team. On a phone, forward a suspicious text to your network operator's spam reporting number if your country has one, then delete the message. Many chat apps let you report and block a contact from the chat menu.

You can also report the message to the company it pretends to be from, through the security or abuse contact listed on their official website. When you report, do not forward the message to friends or post screenshots of it in public groups — the link inside it is still live, and someone else might click it.

Questions people ask

What are the signs of a phishing message?

The most common signs are urgency or threats, a sender address or number that does not match the company, spelling mistakes in the company name or the message, and a link that leads somewhere different from what the text promises. A message that asks for a password, a one-time code, or card details is almost always a fake.

Should I click a link to check if it is real?

No. Hover over the link on a computer, or press and hold it on a phone, to preview the address without opening it. If you still need to check, open the official app or type the web address into your browser yourself instead of using the link.

What should I do if I entered details on a fake page?

Change the password on the real site right away, and change it anywhere else you used the same one. If you entered card details, contact your bank immediately. If you shared a one-time code, sign in, change the password, and check the account activity and recovery settings.

Why do phishing messages create urgency?

Urgency stops you from thinking. A message that says your account will be closed or your parcel returned in a few hours pushes you to click first and check later. Real organisations give you time and let you verify the same information in their app or on their official site.
