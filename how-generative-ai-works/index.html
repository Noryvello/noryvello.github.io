How Generative AI Works, Explained Simply

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

Generative AI is software that creates new things, such as text, images, or video, instead of only sorting or analysing what already exists. It learns patterns from huge amounts of training data, then uses those patterns to generate fresh material from your prompt. This guide explains the process step by step, in plain words.

Type a sentence into a chatbot and it writes a poem. Describe a scene in words and an image generator paints it. Ask for a short clip and a video tool produces moving pictures. It feels like magic, but the mechanism underneath is the same in every case: the system learned patterns from examples, and it uses those patterns to make something new. Once you understand that one idea, every generative AI tool becomes much less mysterious.

This guide walks through how generative AI works for text, images, and video, why the same prompt gives different results each time, and what a prompt really is. No technical background is needed, and every idea is explained in ordinary words.

Generative AI creates new material from patterns, not from knowledge. It can invent facts, produce images with impossible details, and repeat biases from its training data. Check anything you plan to use or share, and never present AI output as verified fact without checking it first.

What "generative" actually means

Most software you use every day is not generative. A search engine finds things that already exist. A spreadsheet adds up numbers you typed in. A photo app sorts pictures you took. These tools organise, find, or calculate. Generative AI is different because it produces new material that did not exist before: a paragraph nobody wrote, a picture nobody painted, a video nobody filmed.

The word "generative" points to this creating step. The system does not look up an answer in a library or copy a file from a folder. It builds the answer from scratch, piece by piece, using patterns it learned during training. A text generator builds an answer word by word. An image generator builds a picture pixel by pixel. A video generator builds a clip frame by frame. The output is new each time, even when the prompt is the same.

That novelty is both the power and the risk of generative AI. It can draft, imagine, and explore at great speed, which makes it a wonderful brainstorming partner. But because it generates rather than retrieves, it can also generate things that are wrong, strange, or misleading, and it presents them with the same confidence as the good ones. Knowing that the output is generated, not looked up, is the key to using these tools well.

Training: learning patterns from examples

Before a generative AI system can create anything, it is trained on a large collection of examples. A text model trains on written text: books, articles, websites, and conversations. An image model trains on images paired with descriptions. A video model trains on video clips. In each case, the training data is the raw material from which the system learns what the world of that medium looks like.

Training means adjusting the system's internal settings until it gets good at a guessing game. For text, the game is predicting the next word. For images, the game is reconstructing a picture from a noisy version of itself. For video, the game is predicting how frames follow one another. The system plays this game over an enormous number of examples, improving a little each time, until its guesses capture the deep patterns of the medium.

What the system learns is patterns, not facts. A text model learns how sentences are built, how arguments flow, and which words tend to follow which. An image model learns how light falls on a face, how buildings stand, and what a cat looks like from any angle. These patterns are the fuel for generation: when you ask for something new, the system draws on them to build an answer that fits the patterns it knows.

How text generation works, step by step

Text generation starts with your prompt, the words you type in. The system reads the prompt and works out the most likely next word, based on the patterns it learned. It adds that word to the text, then predicts the next word again, and again, until it has written a complete answer. Each word is a fresh prediction, influenced by everything that came before it in the conversation.

This word-by-word process explains a lot about chatbots. It explains why answers are fluent: the model has learned the patterns of fluent writing very well. It explains why answers can be wrong: a wrong word can be the most likely word in context, and once it is written, the rest of the answer builds on it. And it explains why the same question gets different answers: at each step there are several likely words, and the choice among them changes the result.

It also explains why context matters so much. The model only has your words to work with. A vague prompt gives it little to go on, so it fills the gaps with the most common patterns, which produces generic answers. A specific prompt with detail, examples, and constraints gives it much more to work with, and the answer is usually much better. The quality of the output is, to a large extent, the quality of the prompt.

How image generation works, step by step

Image generation starts with your prompt too, but the process is different. The system begins with a field of visual noise, like the static on an old television, and gradually shapes it into a picture that matches your description. It does this in many small steps, each one removing a little noise and adding a little structure, guided by the patterns it learned from training images.

During training, the system learned what things look like: the shape of a chair, the texture of fur, the way sunlight falls through a window. It also learned how words relate to pictures, because its training images came with descriptions. When you ask for "a cat sitting on a windowsill in the morning light", it uses those word-to-picture links to steer the noise toward a picture that matches.

This is why image generators are wonderful at mood and style but unreliable at details. They know what hands usually look like, but they do not know that a hand has five fingers, so they sometimes draw six. They know what text looks like on a sign, but they do not know how to spell, so the letters come out garbled. The picture is built from patterns of appearance, not from an understanding of the objects in it.

How video generation works, step by step

Video generation adds one more challenge: movement. A video is a sequence of frames, and each frame must follow naturally from the one before it. The system starts from your prompt, and sometimes from a starting image, and generates frames one after another, predicting how the scene should change over time. It has learned from training videos how objects move, how people walk, and how a camera pans across a scene.

Because each frame depends on the ones before it, small errors can grow. A face that looks fine in the first second may drift and change by the fifth. An object may morph into something else, or a hand may pass through a table. These glitches are the video equivalent of a chatbot inventing a fact: the system is generating plausible-looking frames from patterns, not simulating a real world.

That is also why generated clips are usually short. The longer the clip, the more chances for errors to accumulate, and the harder it is to keep the scene consistent. Short clips of a few seconds are the most reliable, and longer videos are usually built by stitching short clips together and editing them, with a person choosing the best takes.

Why outputs vary every time

If you have ever asked a chatbot the same question twice and received two different answers, you have seen this in action. Generation involves choices. At each step, there are many likely next words, many plausible ways to shape a pixel, many possible next frames. The system picks among them, and a small difference early on can lead to a very different result by the end.

This variability is deliberate. A system that always picked the single most likely option would produce the same safe, boring answer every time. By allowing some randomness in its choices, it can produce fresh, varied, and sometimes surprising results. That is what makes generative AI useful for brainstorming and creativity: it explores the space of possibilities instead of always returning the average answer.

For the user, the practical lesson is to treat each output as one possibility among many. If the first answer is not good, ask again, or change the prompt slightly. Regenerating is not a failure; it is part of how the tool works. The best results usually come after a few tries, with you steering the system toward what you actually want.

What a prompt is, and why it matters

A prompt is simply the input you give a generative AI system: the words that tell it what to create. For a chatbot, the prompt is your question or instruction. For an image generator, it is the description of the picture you want. For a video tool, it is the description of the scene and the movement. The prompt is the only steering wheel you have, so learning to write good prompts is the most practical skill in all of generative AI.

A good prompt is specific. Instead of "write a story", try "write a short story about a lighthouse keeper who finds a message in a bottle, in a warm and gentle tone, about two hundred words long". The extra detail gives the system much more to work with, and the result is usually far closer to what you imagined. The same applies to images: subject, setting, style, lighting, and mood all help the system aim at the right picture.

Prompts are also a conversation. You do not have to get everything right in one message. You can ask for a draft, then say "make it shorter", "make it friendlier", or "change the ending". Each follow-up refines the result. The people who get the most from generative AI are not the ones with the cleverest single prompt, but the ones who iterate: prompt, review, adjust, repeat.

Questions people ask

Is generative AI the same as a chatbot?

No. A chatbot is one product built on generative AI, usually a language model. Generative AI is the wider idea: software that creates new text, images, video, music, or code. Image and video generators are generative AI too, and they are not chatbots.

Does generative AI copy from its training data?

It does not copy and paste. It learns patterns from training data and generates new material from those patterns. The result is usually new, but it can sometimes resemble something in the training data, especially for very common phrases or famous images.

Why do two people get different results from the same prompt?

Because generation involves choices among many likely options. At each step the system picks from likely words, pixels, or frames, and small differences in those choices lead to different results. Asking twice rarely gives the exact same output.

Can generative AI create something truly original?

It creates new combinations of patterns it learned, which can feel original, but it has no ideas of its own. Real originality needs intent, experience, and judgment, which belong to people. The best results come from a person guiding the tool with a clear idea.

Is AI-generated content safe to use?

It depends on the use. For drafts, ideas, and learning, it is very useful. For facts, professional work, or anything public, check the content first: AI can invent details, repeat biases, and produce material that looks right but is not. Ownership rules also vary, so check the terms of the tool you use.
