AI and Privacy: What Happens to What You Type

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

When you type a message into an AI chatbot, your words travel to the company's servers, where they may be stored, reviewed for safety, and in some cases used to improve the service. This guide explains what happens to your prompts in general terms, what you should never paste, and how to use AI tools without giving away more than you mean to. It is general information, not legal advice.

A chatbot feels like a private conversation. You type, it answers, and nobody else seems to be in the room. That feeling is misleading. Your words leave your device, travel across the internet, and land on a company's servers, where they are processed, stored, and sometimes read by people. The chat window is not a locked diary; it is a message to a service.

That does not mean you should stop using AI tools. It means you should use them with the same care you would use in any conversation with a stranger. Share what the task needs, and keep the rest to yourself. This guide shows you how to draw that line, and what to do when you are not sure where it falls.

Never paste passwords, identity document numbers, health details, children's personal data, or work secrets into a chatbot. Assume anything you type could be stored and seen by someone else. If a task needs sensitive information, remove it or replace it with placeholders before you ask for help.

What happens when you press send

When you send a message to a chatbot, your device sends the text to the company's servers. The servers run the AI model, generate a reply, and send it back to you. That round trip takes a second or two, and it is the only part you see. Behind the scenes, several other things may happen.

First, your message is usually stored, at least for a while. The company keeps it so it can show you your chat history, fix errors, and check for abuse. How long it is kept depends on the service and your settings. Some services let you delete conversations, and some let you turn off saving altogether.

Second, your message may be reviewed. Companies check a sample of conversations for safety, to catch misuse and to improve the service. That review may be done by software, or by people. You cannot tell which conversations are reviewed, so the safest assumption is that any of them could be.

What companies keep, in general terms

Most AI services keep your conversation history, your account details, and basic information about how you use the service, such as when you signed in and what device you used. Some also keep the files you upload, the images you generate, and the settings you choose. The exact list is in each service's privacy policy.

Retention, meaning how long data is kept, varies widely. Some services delete conversations quickly once you delete them. Others keep deleted data for a limited period for safety and legal reasons. A few keep anonymised data for much longer, to study how the service is used. The policy is the only reliable source for the details, and it is worth reading the retention section at least once.

Your data may also be shared within the company that runs the service, or with contractors who help run it. It is not usually sold to advertisers as a list of your conversations, but the policy may allow uses that surprise you. If a use matters to you, read the policy rather than guessing.

What never to paste

Passwords are the clearest case. Never paste a password into a chatbot, even to ask whether it is strong. A password in a chat log is a password that may be stored, reviewed, and kept. If you need help with passwords, describe the problem in general terms, or use a password manager's own tools.

Identity documents come next. Never paste identity card numbers, passport numbers, or photos of documents. The same goes for bank account numbers, card numbers, and any code sent to you for signing in. These details are the keys to your identity, and a chat log is a poor place to keep them.

Health details deserve the same care. Do not paste medical records, test results, or detailed descriptions of a health condition into a general chatbot. Health information is sensitive, and a chatbot is not a doctor. If you need health information, use official health sources, and keep your personal details out of the conversation.

Children's data and work secrets round out the list. Do not share children's names, photos, school details, or routines. Do not paste confidential work documents, client information, or internal systems details. Your employer may have its own rules about AI use, and breaking them can have real consequences for your job.

Prompts at work and school

Work and school add a second layer of rules. Your employer or your school may have a policy about which AI tools you can use, and what you can paste into them. Some organisations provide their own AI tools with stronger privacy protections. Others forbid AI tools for certain kinds of work entirely. Check the policy before you paste anything work-related.

Even where AI use is allowed, be careful with other people's information. A document that mentions colleagues, clients, or students may contain details they did not agree to share with a chatbot. Remove names and identifying details, or summarise the problem in your own words, before you ask for help.

Students should be especially careful with assessed work. Pasting an assignment into a chatbot may break academic rules, and it may also share personal information in the document. If you are unsure whether AI help is allowed for a piece of work, ask your teacher first. A quick question now is better than a problem later.

Private alternatives in general terms

If you want stronger privacy, several options exist. Some AI tools run entirely on your own device, so your words never leave your computer or phone. These tools are usually less powerful than the big cloud services, but they are good enough for many everyday tasks, such as summarising a document or drafting a message.

Some services offer a private mode, or a business plan with a promise not to train on your data and to delete it quickly. These promises vary, and they are written in the service's terms, so read them before you rely on them. A promise in a privacy policy is worth more than a promise in an advert.

The simplest private alternative is often the oldest one: do the task yourself, or use a tool that does not need your data at all. A calculator, a local text editor, or a paper notebook keeps your information where it belongs. AI is a convenience, not a necessity, and for the most sensitive tasks it is the wrong tool.

Children, photos, and family data

Children cannot judge privacy risks for themselves, so adults have to do it for them. Do not paste children's schoolwork with their names on it, their medical details, or their photos into AI tools. A photo uploaded to a chatbot or an image tool may be stored, and face photos are especially sensitive because faces identify people.

Family photos deserve the same caution. A photo of your home, your children, or your family gathering contains far more information than you might think: faces, locations, routines, and relationships. Before you upload a photo to any AI tool, ask whether you would be comfortable with a stranger keeping a copy.

Talk to children about AI privacy in simple terms. Explain that a chatbot is not a friend, that it remembers what you tell it, and that some things should stay in the family. A short conversation now can prevent an awkward problem later, and it teaches a habit that will serve them for life.

A simple privacy routine

Good AI privacy does not need technical skill. It needs a short routine, used every time. Before you paste anything, ask three questions: does this contain anything personal, does the task need that detail, and can I remove or replace it? If the answer to the first question is yes, take a moment to clean the text before you send it.

Once a month, spend a few minutes on housekeeping. Delete conversations you no longer need, check your privacy settings, and look at what the service has stored about you. If a setting has changed after an update, you will catch it. If you no longer use a service, delete your account rather than leaving your data there.

Finally, keep learning. Privacy policies change, new tools appear, and the best advice shifts over time. The guides linked below cover the privacy settings of the major chatbots in more detail, and they are a good next step once you have the basics from this page.

Questions people ask

Can someone at the AI company read my chats?

Possibly, yes. Companies review a sample of conversations for safety and quality, and that review can involve people. You cannot tell which conversations are reviewed, so it is safest to assume that anything you type could be read by someone other than the model.

Does deleting a chat remove it completely?

Not always at once. Most services keep deleted conversations for a limited time for safety and legal reasons, then remove them. The exact period is in the privacy policy. Deleting is still worth doing, because it shortens how long your data is available.

Is it safe to paste work documents into a chatbot?

It depends on your employer's rules and on what the document contains. Confidential documents, client information, and internal systems details should not be pasted into a general chatbot. Check your workplace policy first, and remove identifying details when in doubt.

What is the safest way to use AI for personal writing?

Write about general situations rather than real people, replace names with placeholders, and avoid pasting anything you would not want a stranger to read. For very personal writing, consider working offline, or using a tool that runs on your own device.

Do AI tools sell my conversations to advertisers?

The major chatbot services say they do not sell conversation data to advertisers. They may still share data within their own company, or with contractors who help run the service. The privacy policy is the only reliable source for what a specific service does with your data.
