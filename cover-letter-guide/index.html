How to Write a Cover Letter

A good cover letter is short, specific to one job, and built in three parts: an opening that names the role and why it caught your eye, a middle that shows how your experience fits what the job needs, and a closing that asks for the next step. This guide walks through that structure, shows how to tailor one letter to one job, and lists the mistakes that get letters ignored.

A cover letter is not a repeat of your CV. Your CV lists what you have done; the cover letter explains why it matters for this particular job. Hiring managers read dozens of applications, and most cover letters get a quick scan rather than a careful read — so a clear structure and short paragraphs matter more than fancy language. The good news is that a strong cover letter follows a simple pattern, and once you know the pattern you can adapt it to any job.

This guide gives general advice about cover letters. Every job and every employer is different, so adapt the advice to the role you are applying for — and always follow the instructions in the job advert itself.

The structure that works

A cover letter that works has three parts, and they always come in the same order. The opening says which job you are applying for and why it interests you. The middle — usually one or two paragraphs — shows how your experience fits the things the job actually needs. The closing thanks the reader and asks for the next step, such as an interview. That is the whole letter. Everything else is decoration, and most decoration hurts more than it helps.

Think of the letter as an argument in three sentences: I want this job, I can do this job, and I would like to talk about it. Every paragraph should support one of those three points. If a sentence does none of those things, cut it. A letter that stays focused on those three points reads as confident; a letter that wanders reads as unsure, even when the writer is well qualified.

The opening paragraph

The first paragraph has two jobs: name the role you are applying for, and give one honest reason the job interests you. Keep it to two or three sentences. A reader should know within the first lines which job you mean and that you chose it on purpose, not at random.

Example opening paragraph — an example, not text to copy blindly:

I am applying for the teaching assistant role at your school. I have spent the last two years helping in a primary classroom, and I would like to bring that experience to a school that values small-group support for its pupils.

Notice what the example does: it names the role, it gives one relevant fact, and it connects that fact to something about the employer. It does not open with a sentence saying you are writing to apply — the reader already knows that — and it does not list qualifications that belong later in the letter.

The middle: showing the fit

The middle of the letter is where you earn the interview. Read the job description and pick the two or three requirements that matter most — the ones repeated in the advert or listed first. Then, for each one, give one concrete example from your own experience. One real example beats a paragraph of general claims.

Example middle paragraph — an example, not text to copy blindly:

In my current role I support a class of thirty children, including five who need extra help with reading. I plan small-group sessions, track each child's progress, and share updates with parents at the end of each term. This has taught me to explain things clearly and to stay patient when progress is slow.

The example works because it is specific: it gives numbers, names real tasks, and shows a result. Compare it with a vague version — a claim about being a hard worker with good communication skills — which says nothing the reader can picture or check. Specific details are what make a letter believable. You do not need dramatic achievements; everyday work, described clearly, is enough.

The closing

The closing should be short: thank the reader for their time, say you would welcome the chance to discuss the role, and sign off. Two or three sentences is plenty. Avoid sounding demanding — saying you look forward to hearing from them is fine — and do not repeat your whole letter in the final paragraph.

Example closing paragraph — an example, not text to copy blindly:

Thank you for considering my application. I would welcome the chance to talk about how I could support your pupils, and I am happy to come in for an interview at a time that suits you.

How to tailor one letter to one job

A generic letter is easy to spot and easy to ignore. Tailoring does not mean rewriting the whole letter each time — it means changing the parts that connect you to this specific job. Start by reading the job description carefully and underlining the skills and experience it asks for. Then check that your letter answers those points, in the employer's own words where that is natural.

Three small changes make a big difference. First, name the role and the employer in the opening — a letter that could be sent to anyone reads as if it was sent to everyone. Second, choose examples that match this job, not your favourite examples: if the job needs someone calm under pressure, pick the time you handled a difficult situation, not the time you organised a party. Third, mirror the language of the advert when it fits — if the advert talks about supporting pupils, use that phrase rather than a different one. This is not copying; it shows you read the advert and understood it.

Keep a base letter with your structure and your best examples, then adjust the opening, the examples, and the closing for each job. The changes take twenty minutes, and they are the difference between a letter that sounds personal and one that sounds mass-produced.

How long should a cover letter be?

One page is the standard, and most strong letters are three or four paragraphs long — roughly 250 to 400 words. Shorter than that, and the letter may look like you did not try; longer, and the reader may not finish it. If you are struggling to fit on one page, the problem is usually too many examples, not too little space: pick your two strongest and cut the rest.

Short paragraphs help too. A paragraph of more than six or seven lines looks heavy on the page, and a tired reader may skip it. Break long paragraphs in two, and leave a blank line between paragraphs so the letter is easy to scan.

Mistakes that get letters ignored

These are the mistakes that most often send a letter to the rejected pile:

  • Sending the same letter to every job, with only the employer's name changed.
  • Repeating your CV word for word instead of explaining what your experience means for this job.
  • Opening with a cliché or a long story about how you have always dreamed of this work.
  • Writing only about yourself — what you want, what you hope to gain — without saying what you offer the employer.
  • Vague claims with no evidence, such as calling yourself an excellent team player.
  • Spelling and grammar mistakes, which suggest the letter was rushed.
  • A letter that is too long, too dense, or formatted in a way that is hard to read.
  • Forgetting to name the role, or naming the wrong role from a copied template.

One final check before you send it: read the letter out loud. Awkward sentences are easy to hear and hard to spot on screen. Then check the employer's name, the role title, and any dates one more time — a letter addressed to the wrong employer is the easiest mistake to make and the hardest to undo.

Questions people ask

How long should a cover letter be?

One page, usually three or four paragraphs — roughly 250 to 400 words. Shorter than that can look half-hearted, and longer than that risks not being read. If you cannot fit on one page, cut examples rather than shrinking the text.

Should I write a new cover letter for every job?

Yes, at least in part. Keep a base letter with your structure and best examples, then change the opening, the examples, and the closing for each job so the letter answers that job's own requirements. A letter sent unchanged to every employer is easy to spot.

What should the first paragraph say?

It should name the role you are applying for and give one honest reason the job interests you, in two or three sentences. The reader should know within the first lines which job you mean and that you chose it on purpose.

What are the most common cover letter mistakes?

Sending the same letter to every job, repeating your CV instead of explaining what your experience means for this role, making vague claims with no evidence, and spelling mistakes. Long, dense letters and letters that only talk about what you want are also commonly ignored.
