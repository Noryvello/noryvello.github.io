Solstices and Equinoxes 2028

The four season markers of 2028 are the March equinox on Monday 20 March at 02:17 UTC, the June solstice on Tuesday 20 June at 20:02 UTC, the September equinox on Friday 22 September at 11:45 UTC and the December solstice on Thursday 21 December at 08:19 UTC. This page explains what each one means and lets you check the season for any 2028 date.

All times on this page are in UTC. In your own timezone the date can fall a day earlier or later when the instant lands near midnight, and the season dates here are for 2028 only.

Each of these four moments is a single instant, the same instant everywhere on Earth. The dates and times above come from the United States Naval Observatory and are given in UTC, the world's shared time standard. Because the instants fall at different local times around the world, the calendar date can differ by a day: the June solstice, for example, falls at 20:02 UTC on 20 June, which is already the early hours of 21 June in much of Asia and Australia. What a solstice is

A solstice marks the point where the Sun reaches its highest or lowest position in the sky at noon. The word comes from the Latin for the Sun standing still, because around the solstice the Sun's noon height changes very little from day to day, as if it were pausing before turning back. There are two solstices each year: the June solstice and the December solstice.

In the Northern Hemisphere, the June solstice brings the longest day of the year and the shortest night, and it marks the start of summer. The December solstice brings the shortest day and the longest night, and it marks the start of winter. The farther you live from the equator, the stronger the difference: near the poles the summer solstice can bring a day with no real night at all, while near the equator the change in day length across the year is small.

The cause is the tilt of the Earth. Our planet spins on an axis that leans to one side, so as the Earth travels around the Sun, first one hemisphere and then the other leans towards the Sun. When your hemisphere leans towards the Sun, sunlight strikes more directly and the days grow longer: that is summer. When it leans away, the sunlight is slanted and the days shorten: that is winter. The solstices mark the two extremes of that lean.

What an equinox is

An equinox marks the point where day and night are nearly equal in length all over the world. The word means equal night, and on the equinox the Sun rises close to due east and sets close to due west. There are two equinoxes each year: the March equinox and the September equinox. In the Northern Hemisphere the March equinox marks the start of spring and the September equinox marks the start of autumn; in the Southern Hemisphere it is the other way round.

The balance is close, but not exact. Day and night are not precisely equal on the equinox, because the way sunrise and sunset are measured, and the bending of sunlight in the atmosphere, both stretch the day a little. The equal split falls a few days away from the equinox itself, and the exact date depends on where you live. Even so, the equinox is the fairest point of the year: the moment when neither hemisphere has the advantage, and the whole planet shares roughly the same length of day.

On the equinox the Earth's axis leans neither towards nor away from the Sun. Sunlight falls straight onto the equator, and the line between day and night runs from pole to pole. From that point on, one hemisphere starts to gain the longer days, until the next solstice turns the balance around again.

Why the seasons are opposite in the two hemispheres

Because the seasons come from the Earth's tilt, the two hemispheres always have opposite seasons. When the Northern Hemisphere leans towards the Sun in June, the Southern Hemisphere leans away, so June brings summer to the north and winter to the south. In December the lean reverses, and the south has its summer while the north has its winter. The solstices and equinoxes are the same instants for everyone, but what they mean depends on which side of the equator you stand on.

This is why the season checker above asks for a hemisphere. The same date in 2028 can be midsummer in one hemisphere and midwinter in the other, and the checker gives the season for the hemisphere you choose.

Why the dates shift from year to year

The solstices and equinoxes do not fall on the same dates every year. They shift by a day or so, because the calendar year and the Sun's cycle do not line up exactly. The leap-year system keeps the calendar close to the Sun over the long run, but the individual dates still drift a little from year to year. That is why a guide for 2028 lists different dates from a guide for any other year, and why the dates on this page are for 2028 only.

Questions people ask

When is the June solstice in 2028?

Tuesday 20 June 2028 at 20:02 UTC. It marks the start of summer in the Northern Hemisphere and the start of winter in the Southern Hemisphere.

What is the difference between a solstice and an equinox?

A solstice marks the longest or shortest day of the year, while an equinox marks the point where day and night are nearly equal in length.

Are day and night exactly equal on the equinox?

Nearly, but not exactly. The balance is close, and the exact split depends on where you live and how sunrise and sunset are measured.

Why is it summer in Australia in December?

The Southern Hemisphere leans towards the Sun in December, so its seasons run opposite to the Northern Hemisphere, and December brings summer there.

Do the solstice and equinox dates change every year?

Yes, by a day or so, because the calendar year and the Sun's cycle do not line up exactly. The dates on this page are for 2028 only.
