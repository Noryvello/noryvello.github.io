Business License Guide Directory

This guide explains the main types of business licence, which kinds of business usually need them, and how to check the official requirements for your own area. Licence rules are set locally, so this page points you to the official sources rather than guessing them.

General information only — this page is not legal advice, and it does not state any country's licence rules as fact. Licence requirements are set by your country, region, and city, and they change over time. Always check the official source for your area before you trade.

What a business licence is

A business licence is official permission to run a particular kind of business in a particular place. It is usually issued by a government office — a city council, a regional authority, or a national agency — and it says that your business has met the requirements for that activity. Some licences are general, covering the right to trade at all; others are specific, covering one activity such as selling food, cutting hair, or carrying passengers.

A licence is different from registering a business. Registration creates the business as a legal thing — it gives it a name and a number in an official register. A licence gives that business permission to do a particular activity. Many businesses need both: they register first, and then apply for the licences their work requires. A licence is also different from a certificate or a qualification, which proves that a person has the skill to do a job; a licence proves that the business is allowed to do it.

Licences exist to protect the public. A food business must show that its kitchen is safe. A builder must show that the work meets safety standards. A taxi driver must show that the vehicle is roadworthy and the driver is fit to drive. The licence is the public proof that those checks have been made, and customers are right to ask to see it.

Common licence types

Licence names differ from country to country, but the same kinds of licence appear in most places. A general business licence, sometimes called a trading licence, is the basic permission to trade in a city or region. A professional licence covers work that needs a qualification, such as accountancy, law, medicine, or electrical work. A health and safety permit covers workplaces, kitchens, and anywhere the public visits. A food licence or food hygiene registration covers anyone who prepares or sells food.

Other common licences include a sign permit for putting a sign outside a shop, a premises licence for selling alcohol or playing music, a street trading licence for selling in a public place, and an environmental permit for work that produces waste or noise. Home-based businesses sometimes need a home occupation permit, which confirms that the business is allowed to operate from a home address. None of these is needed by every business — the list that applies to you depends on what you do and where you do it.

Licences by business kind

The licences you need follow the kind of work you do. A shop that sells clothes has different requirements from a kitchen that sells meals, and a builder has different requirements from a designer working from home. If your business does more than one thing — a cafe that also sells clothes, for example — you may need the licences for both activities. And if your business grows or changes what it sells, check again: a new activity can mean a new licence, even if the business itself has not moved.

How to check what you need

The only reliable way to find out which licences you need is to ask the official source for your area. Start with your country's small business authority, which usually has a guide to licences and a tool or a list that covers the main types. In the United States, the US Small Business Administration explains federal, state, and local licence requirements. In the United Kingdom, GOV.UK business pages list the licences for each kind of business. In Australia, business.gov.au has a licence finder for each state and territory.

After the national source, check your city or regional authority, because many licences are issued locally and the rules differ from place to place. Your city council or local business office can usually tell you which licences apply to your address and your activity, and what the application involves. If your trade has a professional body — an association for builders, food businesses, or accountants — that body is often the best guide to the licences its members need.

Work through the check in this order: first, what does your country require; second, what does your region or state require; third, what does your city require; fourth, what does your trade or profession require. Write down every licence you find, the authority that issues it, and the date by which you must apply. If two sources disagree, trust the official source — the government office that issues the licence — over any other website.

Renewals, fees and record-keeping

Most licences last for a fixed period — often one year — and must be renewed before they expire. A licence that lapses can mean that you are trading without permission, even if you held it for years before. Keep a simple record for every licence you hold: the name of the licence, the authority that issued it, the date it was issued, the date it expires, and the date you must apply to renew it. Set a reminder well before the renewal date, because some renewals need an inspection or new paperwork that takes time.

Licences usually carry a fee, and the amount is set by the issuing authority. Fees vary widely from place to place and from licence to licence, so this page does not state any fee as a fact. Budget for fees as a regular business cost, and keep the receipts: they are business expenses, and the receipt is your proof that the fee was paid. If a fee seems much higher than you expected, check it against the official source — and be careful of websites that charge extra to apply for a licence on your behalf when you could apply directly.

Keep your licences where you can find them. Some licences must be displayed at your premises, where customers can see them. Others must be available if an inspector visits. Keep a copy of each licence separate from the original, and keep a note of every renewal, so that you always know which licences are current and which are coming up for renewal.

What happens if you trade without a licence

Trading without a licence you need can have serious consequences, and the exact consequences are set by local law. In many places, the authority can fine the business, order it to stop trading until the licence is in place, or refuse to renew other permits. In some cases, insurance may not cover a business that is trading without a required licence, and a customer who is harmed may have a stronger claim. The details differ from place to place, which is why this page does not state any specific penalty as a fact.

The risk is not only legal. Customers, suppliers, and landlords often ask to see a licence before they work with a business, and a business that cannot show one loses trust quickly. A licence is also a selling point: it tells customers that your business has been checked and meets the standards for its trade. Getting the right licences is part of building a business that people trust.

If you discover that you have been trading without a licence you needed, do not ignore it. Stop the activity the licence covers if you can, find out what is required from the official source, and apply as soon as possible. In many places, coming forward and putting things right is treated more kindly than being found out later, but the rules are local, so check them for your own area.

Licences for online businesses

An online business still needs to follow the rules of the place where it is based, and often the rules of the places where its customers are. Selling through a website does not remove the need for a business registration, a tax registration, or a licence for a regulated activity. A food business that sells online still needs its food licence. A business that sells regulated products still needs the permits for those products.

Online businesses also face rules that a shop on a street does not. Consumer protection rules usually require clear information about prices, delivery, returns, and the identity of the seller. Data protection rules govern how customer information is collected and stored. And if you sell to customers in other countries, those countries may have their own rules about imports, taxes, and product safety. The official small business sources listed in this guide cover online trading as well as shops, so start there.

If you run an online business from home, check whether your area requires a home occupation permit or has rules about running a business from a residential address. Some areas limit the number of visitors, the amount of stock you can store, or the signs you can display. A quiet online business is less likely to cause a problem than a busy workshop, but the rules still apply.

A simple action plan

If you are starting a business, or checking the licences of one you already run, work through these steps in order. First, write down what your business does, where it operates, and whether it employs anyone or serves the public in person. Second, check the official small business source for your country and note every licence that might apply. Third, check your city or regional authority for local licences. Fourth, check your trade or professional body for anything specific to your work.

Fifth, apply for the licences you need before you start trading, and keep copies of every application and every licence you receive. Sixth, write down every renewal date and set a reminder for each one. Seventh, check again whenever your business changes — a new service, a new location, a new product, or new staff can all mean a new licence. A business that keeps its licences in order is a business that can trade with confidence, and its customers can see that.

Questions people ask

Do I need a licence to start a small business?

It depends on what your business does and where it operates. Many businesses need at least a general business licence or a registration, and some trades need specific licences for food, health and safety, or professional work. Check the official small business source for your country and your local authority to find out what applies to you.

Where do I check licence rules?

Start with your country's official small business authority, then check your city or regional authority, because many licences are issued locally. In the United States, the Small Business Administration explains the requirements. In the United Kingdom, GOV.UK lists licences by business kind. In Australia, business.gov.au has a licence finder.

What is the difference between a licence and a registration?

Registration creates your business as a legal thing, giving it a name and a number in an official register. A licence gives that business permission to do a particular activity, such as selling food or cutting hair. Many businesses need both: they register first, and then apply for the licences their work requires.

Do online businesses need licences?

Yes, in most cases. Selling online does not remove the need for a business registration, a tax registration, or a licence for a regulated activity. A food business selling online still needs its food licence, and consumer protection and data protection rules also apply. Check the official sources for your country and for any country you sell to.

How often must a licence be renewed?

Most licences last for a fixed period, often one year, and must be renewed before they expire. The exact period and the renewal process are set by the authority that issues the licence, so check the terms of each licence you hold and set a reminder well before the renewal date.

What if I work from home?

Some areas require a home occupation permit or have rules about running a business from a residential address, such as limits on visitors, stock, or signs. Check with your local authority before you start. A quiet online business is less likely to cause a problem than a busy workshop, but the rules still apply.
