AI Portrait Prompts: How to Write Prompts for Realistic, Flattering Portraits

By the Noryvello team · Published 20 November 2026 · Updated 20 November 2026

Faces are the hardest thing for image AI to draw well — eyes, hands, and skin go wrong first, and small errors are easy to spot. This guide shows you the portrait prompt formula, the light and lens words that flatter a face, and how to fix the flaws that turn up most often.

Image tools change their menus and buttons often, so if a step below does not match what you see on screen, the tool's own help pages are the authority. Only make portraits of real people with their permission, and never present an AI portrait as a real photograph of a real person.

Why portraits are hard for AI

People are experts at faces. We spend our lives reading them — a glance tells us a mood, a half-second look tells us whether a face is familiar — and that expertise makes us brutally good at spotting when a face is wrong. A landscape with a strange tree goes unnoticed; a portrait with one eye slightly larger than the other jumps out at once.

That is the first reason portraits are hard for image AI. The model has learned the pattern of a face, but it has not learned anatomy, and the errors it makes are exactly the ones we notice fastest: eyes that do not match, a mouth that sits slightly off-centre, skin that is too smooth to be human. A portrait that is ninety-five percent right can still feel wrong, because the five percent that is wrong is in the eyes.

Hands are the second reason. A portrait often includes them — resting on a table, holding a cup, tucked into a pocket — and hands are the most complicated common shape, with many joints and many possible poses. Extra fingers, merged fingers, and hands that bend the wrong way are the classic portrait failure, and they appear in otherwise excellent pictures.

Skin is the third. Real skin has texture — fine lines, pores, freckles, small unevenness — and the line between natural texture and dirty-looking noise is thin. Ask for too little texture and the face looks plastic; ask for too much and it looks rough. The sections below show you how to steer between those two failures, starting with the formula that holds a portrait prompt together.

The portrait prompt formula

A portrait prompt has five parts, in this order: subject, light, lens, mood, background. The order matters because the model weighs the first words most, so the person comes first and the background comes last. Five parts is enough: more than that and the prompt starts pulling in too many directions at once.

The subject is who the portrait shows, described in general terms: an approximate age, a few visible features, what they are wearing. Keep it to what the camera would see — hair, clothing, posture — and avoid long backstories, which the model cannot draw. The light is one phrase from the next section: golden hour, soft window light, studio light, or overcast.

The lens is the camera language: 85mm is the classic portrait lens, and the framing words — head-and-shoulders, close-up, three-quarter view — tell the model how much of the person to show. The mood is one feeling word: calm, joyful, thoughtful. The background is one plain phrase: a plain wall, a blurred garden, a soft grey backdrop.

Put together, the formula reads like this:

Portrait of a woman in her forties with grey-streaked hair, soft window light, 85mm lens, head-and-shoulders framing, gentle smile, plain warm-grey background

That is one sentence and five parts. When a result disappoints you, change one part at a time — swap the light, or change the framing — and compare the results side by side. Changing everything at once teaches you nothing about which part went wrong.

Light words that flatter

Light does more for a portrait than any other single word, because it decides how the skin looks, where the shadows fall, and what mood the picture carries. Four light phrases cover almost every flattering portrait, and knowing what each one does will save you many failed generations.

  • Golden hour. The low, warm light of late afternoon. It flatters almost everyone, warms the skin, and softens small flaws. Golden hour is the safest light word for an outdoor portrait.
  • Soft window light. Gentle, even light coming from a window to one side. It is natural, kind to skin, and easy for the model to render well. Soft window light is the best default for an indoor portrait.
  • Studio light. Clean, controlled light with soft shadows. It suits a formal portrait and gives a polished, professional feeling without harshness.
  • Overcast. The soft, shadowless light of a cloudy day. Overcast light evens out skin tones and removes hard shadows, which makes it forgiving for close-ups.

Two light words are worth avoiding. Harsh midday sun creates hard shadows under the eyes and nose, and shiny patches on the skin. Flash flattens the face and adds a hard edge to every shadow. If a portrait keeps coming back with strange shadows, the light word is the first thing to change — and soft diffused light is a reliable general fixer when you are not sure what went wrong.

Lens and framing words

Lens words tell the model how the camera sees the person, and they change the portrait more than most people expect. The most useful lens word is 85mm, the classic portrait lens, which renders faces with natural proportions and a gently blurred background. It is the default to reach for unless you have a reason to choose something else.

A 50mm lens shows more of the scene and more of the person, which suits a portrait where the place matters — a street portrait, a portrait in a workshop. Wider than that, faces start to stretch at the edges of the frame, which is rarely flattering. Longer than 85mm, the background compresses and the face flattens; it can be elegant, but it is a deliberate choice, not a default.

Framing words say how much of the person to show. Head-and-shoulders is the standard portrait framing: the face fills the picture, the shoulders anchor it, and the hands stay out of trouble. Close-up fills the frame with the face alone, which is powerful when the eyes are right and unforgiving when they are not. Three-quarter view turns the body at an angle while the face stays toward the camera, which looks natural and flattering.

Full-body portraits are the hardest of all, because they bring hands, feet, and posture into the picture at once. If you want one, keep the pose simple and the clothing plain, and expect to generate several versions. Shallow depth of field is a useful extra phrase: it blurs the background and keeps the focus on the face, which is where it belongs.

Expressions and poses that look natural

The most natural portraits come from the smallest descriptions. A gentle smile reads as real; a big grin often shows teeth, and teeth are one of the model's weak points — uneven, too many, or slightly melted. A calm and thoughtful expression is easier for the model than a laughing one, and it ages better as a picture.

Poses work the same way: simple beats dramatic. Relaxed shoulders, a slight turn of the head, and hands doing something ordinary — resting on a table, holding a book, arms crossed — all read as natural. Arms crossed and hands in pockets have a second benefit: they keep the hands out of the picture, which removes the most common source of portrait failure entirely.

Where the eyes look matters too. Looking at the camera creates a direct, confident portrait, but it puts the eyes under the most scrutiny, so both eyes must match. Looking slightly away feels candid and relaxed, and it is more forgiving of small eye errors. Looking down reads as thoughtful, and it suits a quiet portrait.

Avoid asking for perfection in the expression. Perfect smile, flawless face, and ideal beauty all push the model toward a polished, unreal look — the plastic skin and symmetrical features that make a portrait feel artificial. Natural, relaxed, and gentle are the words that keep a face human.

Age and realism — getting natural skin instead of plastic skin

The plastic-skin problem is the portrait flaw people notice second, after the eyes. It happens when the model smooths the skin too far: no pores, no fine lines, no small unevenness, just a waxy surface that no real face has. The fix is to ask for texture, in plain words.

Natural skin texture is the most useful phrase. Add fine lines for an older face, freckles for a younger one, and grey-streaked hair or silver hair where the age calls for it. These small details are what make a face read as real: the model needs permission to include imperfection, because its habit is to polish it away.

Age words should be plain and approximate: in her forties, in his sixties, a young woman, an older man. Avoid asking for a specific age — the model cannot count years — and avoid flawless skin, perfect skin, and airbrushed, which all push toward the plastic look. Realistic and natural are helpful words; photorealistic can overshoot, producing a face that is technically sharp but waxy and unreal.

Wrinkles deserve a gentle hand. Asking for deep wrinkles often produces a caricature; fine lines and a few creases around the eyes give age without exaggeration. And remember that age shows in more than the face: grey hair, the set of the shoulders, and the hands all carry it, so describe the person as a whole, not just the skin.

Backgrounds that don't distract

A portrait background has one job: stay out of the way. The face is the subject, and anything in the background that competes with it — a bright colour, a busy pattern, another face — weakens the portrait. The safest backgrounds are the simplest ones.

A plain wall in a warm or neutral tone is the most reliable choice: plain warm-grey background, plain cream wall, soft beige backdrop. It gives the face room and asks nothing of the viewer. A blurred garden or a softly blurred street works outdoors, because the blur keeps the background present but unreadable — it sets a mood without adding detail.

Two rules keep a background honest. First, the background should be clearly darker or lighter than the subject, not the same tone, or the person melts into it. Second, avoid text, signs, and other faces in the background: the model renders them badly, and a half-formed face behind your subject is worse than no background at all.

Shallow depth of field is the phrase that helps most here, because it tells the model to blur whatever is behind the person. Combined with a plain background phrase, it produces the clean, professional look that most portraits want — the face sharp, everything else soft.

Fixing common flaws

Every portrait prompt produces flaws sometimes, and knowing how to fix each one is more useful than hoping for a perfect first result. Start with the eyes: ask for both eyes looking at the camera and symmetrical eyes, and generate several versions, because eyes vary more between generations than any other feature. One good pair of eyes is worth four attempts.

Hands are the next fix, and the best fix is often avoidance. Hands out of frame, arms crossed, and hands in pockets all remove the problem entirely, and they look natural. If the hands must show, keep them simple — one hand resting on a table, fingers together — and expect to regenerate. Asking for five fingers rarely works; the model does not count.

Teeth cause trouble when the mouth is open. A closed-lip smile removes the risk and still reads as warm. If you want a laughing portrait, expect to generate many versions and pick the one where the teeth behave. Extra fingers, warped ears, and doubled features all respond to the same treatment: regenerate, and change one thing at a time.

The general rule is patience. A portrait prompt is a conversation, not a single command: generate, look closely at what went wrong, change one part, and generate again. Keep the prompt that worked, because you will want it when you make a matching portrait later — and the prompt that worked is the one you will never remember exactly unless you write it down.

  • Winter portrait. Cold air, warm face, soft light.

Use these patterns as starting points. Change the light, swap the framing, try a different background — and keep the version where the eyes are right, because the eyes are what the viewer will remember.

Questions people ask

Why do AI faces look strange sometimes?

Because we are experts at faces, we notice tiny errors — eyes that do not match, a mouth slightly off-centre, skin that is too smooth. The model has learned the pattern of a face but not its anatomy, so small mistakes in the eyes, hands, and skin show up fast.

What words make a portrait look like a real photo?

Natural skin texture, soft window light or golden hour, and an 85mm lens with head-and-shoulders framing are the most reliable combination. Avoid flawless skin and perfect smile, which push the model toward a polished, unreal look.

How do I get soft, flattering light?

Ask for golden hour, soft window light, studio light, or overcast — all four are soft and even. Avoid harsh midday sun and flash, which create hard shadows and shiny skin. Soft diffused light is a good general fixer.

Can I make a portrait of a real person?

Only with that person's permission, and never to mislead anyone — never present an AI portrait as a real photograph of a real person. For practice and for gifts, fictional subjects described in general terms are the safe choice.

What is the best framing for a portrait?

Head-and-shoulders is the standard: the face fills the picture, the shoulders anchor it, and the hands stay out of trouble. Close-up suits a strong face, and three-quarter view looks natural and flattering.

How do I fix bad hands or eyes?

For eyes, ask for both eyes looking at the camera and generate several versions — eyes vary a lot between generations. For hands, the best fix is often to keep them out of frame, with arms crossed or hands in pockets.
