Trademark Search Guide

This guide shows you how to search trademark registers step by step, explains the trademark classes, and tells you honestly what a search can and cannot prove. It covers the current USPTO search, the UK search, and the EU search, with links to each official register.

Not legal advice. A trademark search is a research step, not a legal opinion: a clear search does not guarantee that a name is free to use, and a crowded result does not always mean it is not. For a name your business will depend on, get advice from a qualified trademark professional. Note: the old USPTO TESS system is retired — the current search is at tmsearch.uspto.gov.

What a trademark search is for

A trademark is a name, a logo, or another sign that identifies the goods or services of one business and sets them apart from others. A trademark search is the process of checking the official trademark registers to see whether a name or logo you want to use is already claimed by someone else, for the same or similar goods or services. It is the first step before choosing a business name, a product name, or a brand.

Searching early saves money and embarrassment. A business that builds its signs, its website, and its packaging around a name it cannot use may have to start again, and may face a legal demand from the owner of the existing mark. A search does not cost anything to run — the official registers are free to search — and it takes less than an hour to learn. It is one of the best uses of an hour in the early days of a business.

A search is also useful when you are buying a business, launching a new product, or checking a name you have used for years. Trademark registers change as new marks are filed and old ones lapse, so a search is a snapshot of one moment in time, not a permanent answer. This guide explains how to run a proper search, how to read the results, and what the search cannot tell you.

Before you search: what you need

Before you open a register, gather three things. First, the exact name or logo you want to use, written exactly as you would use it — including any unusual spelling, and noting whether you want to protect the words alone or the words with a design. Second, a clear list of the goods or services the name will cover: not just the general field, but the specific things you sell or provide. Third, the countries where you will trade, because trademark rights are territorial — a mark registered in one country does not automatically cover another.

It also helps to prepare a short list of variations to search. Search the name as it is written, and also search the obvious sound-alikes and spelling variations, because trademark offices consider names that look or sound similar. If your name is made of common words, search the words separately as well as together. A thorough search covers the name you want and the names that are close to it.

Finally, decide what you are searching for. Are you checking whether a name is free to use, or whether a name is free to register? The two questions overlap but they are not the same. A name can be free to register in one class and still clash with an unregistered name that someone has used for years. This guide covers the register search; the wider question of unregistered use is covered in the section on what a search cannot prove.

How to search at the USPTO step by step

The United States Patent and Trademark Office, the USPTO, keeps the federal register of trademarks for the United States. Its current search system is at tmsearch.uspto.gov, and it is free to use. The old search system, called TESS, has been retired and is no longer available, so any guide that sends you to TESS is out of date. The USPTO trademarks pages explain the system and link to the search.

To search, open the USPTO trademark search and type the name into the search box. Start with a simple search for the exact name, and look at the results list: each result shows the mark, the owner, the goods or services it covers, and its status — whether it is live, pending, or dead. Open any result that looks similar to your name and read its details: the goods and services matter as much as the name itself, because a similar name for very different goods may not conflict.

Then run a wider search. Try the name with common spelling variations, and try searching for the distinctive word on its own. The search system offers filters for the status of the mark and for the class of goods or services, and it helps to filter to live marks first, since dead marks no longer block a new application. Make a note of every mark that is similar to yours, with its number, its owner, and the goods or services it covers — you will need that list when you read your results.

How to search in the UK and the EU

In the United Kingdom, the official search is the GOV.UK trademark search, run by the Intellectual Property Office. It works in the same way as the USPTO search: type the name, look through the results, and open any mark that is similar to yours to read the goods and services it covers. The UK register covers the United Kingdom only, so a clear result there says nothing about the United States or the European Union.

For the European Union, the official search is run by the European Union Intellectual Property Office, the EUIPO, at euipo.europa.eu. An EU trademark covers all member states of the European Union in a single registration, so the EU search is the one to run if you plan to trade in Europe. The EUIPO search also covers designs, so make sure you are searching trademarks and not designs when you run it.

If you plan to trade in more than one country, search each register separately. A name can be free in one register and taken in another, and there is no single search that covers the whole world. The World Intellectual Property Organization runs a global search that covers many national registers, which can be a useful extra check, but the national registers themselves are the authoritative source for each country.

Trademark classes explained

Trademarks are registered for specific goods or services, which are organised into 45 classes under an international system called the Nice Classification. Classes 1 to 34 cover goods — things you can buy — and classes 35 to 45 cover services — things done for you. When you apply for a trademark, you choose the classes that match what you sell or provide, and your rights cover those classes.

The class matters because two businesses can use similar names for different things without clashing. A name registered for clothing in class 25 does not normally stop a different business using a similar name for restaurant services in class 43. But classes are not walls: if the goods or services are related, or if customers might think the two businesses are connected, there can still be a conflict. A name for coffee in class 30 and a name for a coffee shop in class 43 are close enough that a trademark office may see a clash.

What a search can and cannot prove

A register search can prove that a mark is on the register, who owns it, what it covers, and whether it is live or dead. That is valuable information, and it is the information a trademark office will use when it examines a new application. If your search finds an identical mark for the same goods or services, that is a strong signal to choose a different name.

A search cannot prove that a name is free to use. Trademark rights can also arise from use, not only from registration: a business that has used a name for years without registering it may still have rights in that name in its area, and those rights do not appear in a register search. A search also cannot tell you how a trademark office will decide a close case, or how a court would rule if there were a dispute. Two marks that look similar to you may be treated as different by an examiner, and two marks that look different may be treated as similar.

A search also has limits of coverage. It covers the register you searched, at the moment you searched it. New applications are filed every day, and a mark filed after your search will not appear in it. A search in one country says nothing about another country. And a search of words says little about logos: a similar design with different words may still conflict, and logo searching needs different tools. Treat a clear search as a good sign, not as a guarantee.

Reading your results

When you read your results, sort them into three groups. The first group is identical marks for the same or similar goods or services: these are the clearest conflicts, and they usually mean you should choose a different name. The second group is similar marks — names that look or sound alike, or share a distinctive word — for related goods or services: these need careful thought, and they are the cases where professional advice is most valuable. The third group is similar marks for unrelated goods or services, or marks that are dead: these are usually less of a problem, but they are still worth noting.

For each mark in the first two groups, read the full record. Check the status: a pending application may still become a live mark, and a dead mark may have been abandoned for a reason that does not help you. Check the owner: a large company with a big legal team is a different prospect from a small business that has not used its mark for years. Check the goods and services in detail, because the class heading alone does not tell the whole story — two marks in the same class can cover very different things.

Also check how the mark is used. A registered mark that its owner has not used for years may be open to challenge in some countries, but that is a legal process with its own rules and costs, not something to assume. Do not decide that a mark is safe to ignore because its owner looks inactive — that is a judgement for a trademark professional, not for a search alone.

What to do next

If your search finds no identical or closely similar marks for your goods or services, you have a good starting point. Keep your search notes, including the date you searched and the registers you checked, because they are useful if you apply later. Consider searching again shortly before you file an application, since new marks are filed all the time. And think about the wider checks: a web search for the name, a check of company name registers, and a check of domain names, so that your brand is consistent across the places customers will look.

If your search finds a possible conflict, do not panic and do not ignore it. Narrow down the conflict: is the mark identical or merely similar, are the goods or services the same or only related, is the mark live or dead, and is the owner active? If the conflict is clear — the same name for the same goods — the cheapest step is usually to choose a different name early, before you have spent money on signs, packaging, and a website.

If the conflict is close but not clear, or if the name matters a great deal to your business, get advice from a qualified trademark professional before you invest in the brand. A professional search goes further than a register search: it covers unregistered use, company names, and domain names, and it comes with an opinion on the risk. That opinion is what a free register search cannot give you, and for a name your business will depend on, it is worth having.

Questions people ask

What is a trademark search?

A trademark search is a check of the official trademark registers to see whether a name or logo you want to use is already claimed by someone else for the same or similar goods or services. The official registers are free to search, and a search is the first step before choosing a business name, a product name, or a brand.

Is the USPTO TESS system still available?

No. The old USPTO search system called TESS has been retired and is no longer available. The current USPTO trademark search is at tmsearch.uspto.gov, and it is free to use. Any guide that sends you to TESS is out of date.

What are trademark classes?

Trademark classes are the 45 groups that goods and services are sorted into under the international Nice Classification. Classes 1 to 34 cover goods, such as clothing and food, and classes 35 to 45 cover services, such as advertising and education. You register a trademark for the classes that match what you sell or provide.

Does a clear search mean my name is safe?

No. A clear register search is a good sign, but it cannot guarantee that a name is free to use. Trademark rights can also arise from use without registration, and those rights do not appear in a register search. A search also covers only the register you checked, at the moment you checked it. For a name your business will depend on, get professional advice.

How far back do trademark records go?

The registers hold records of live marks, pending applications, and dead marks, going back many years. A dead mark is one that has lapsed or been cancelled, and it no longer blocks a new application, although the reasons it died may matter. When you search, filter to live marks first, then check the dead ones for background.

Should I search in more than one country?

Yes, if you plan to trade in more than one country. Trademark rights are territorial, so a mark registered in one country does not automatically cover another. Search each country's register separately: the USPTO for the United States, GOV.UK for the United Kingdom, and the EUIPO for the European Union.
