Vegetable Roasting Time Guide

Pick a vegetable and its cut size and this guide gives you a roasting temperature range, a time range and a turn-halfway reminder. Fifteen vegetables are covered, from dense roots that need a long roast to soft vegetables that cook in minutes.

How to use this guide

Choose the vegetable and the cut size, then press the button. You will get a temperature range and a time range for that combination, plus a reminder to turn the pieces halfway through. Times are ranges because ovens vary and because the exact size of the pieces matters. Start checking at the early end of the range and keep roasting until the pieces are tender inside and browned at the edges.

Cut size is the biggest factor in roasting time. Small pieces cook much faster than large ones, and a tray of mixed sizes will cook unevenly, with small pieces burning before large ones are done. Take a minute to cut the pieces to a similar size, and the whole tray will finish together.

The roasting basics

Roasting works by dry heat, and three things make it work well: a hot oven, enough oil, and enough space. Preheat the oven fully before the tray goes in, because vegetables that start in a cool oven steam instead of browning. Coat every piece lightly in oil so the heat reaches the surface evenly and the edges crisp. Spread the pieces in a single layer with space between them, because a crowded tray traps steam and turns roasting into steaming.

Use a large, heavy tray if you have one, and preheat the tray with the oil for a few minutes before adding the vegetables. Hot oil gives the pieces a crisp start. Do not cover the tray, and do not open the oven door too often, because each opening drops the temperature and slows the browning.

Matching vegetables on one tray

Different vegetables cook at different speeds. Dense roots such as potatoes, carrots, parsnips and beetroot need the longest, usually 35 to 50 minutes depending on size. Medium vegetables such as squash, onions, cauliflower and Brussels sprouts need about 25 to 40 minutes. Quick vegetables such as courgettes, peppers, green beans, aubergine and mushrooms need about 15 to 25 minutes.

To roast a mix on one tray, start the slowest vegetables first and add the quicker ones partway through. For example, put potatoes and carrots in first, add squash and onions after about 15 minutes, and add peppers and courgettes for the last 15 minutes. That way everything finishes at the same time, browned and tender, instead of some pieces burning while others are still hard.

Temperature: hotter is not always better

Most vegetables roast well between 190 and 220 degrees Celsius. Dense vegetables suit the hotter end, because they need strong heat to cook through and brown. Soft vegetables suit the cooler end, because they can burn on the outside before the inside is done. If your oven runs hot, use the lower end of the range and check early. If it runs cool, use the higher end and give the tray a little more time.

Sugar content also matters. Vegetables with more natural sugar, such as sweet potatoes, parsnips and onions, brown faster and can burn if the oven is too hot. Watch them closely in the last few minutes and turn them so the browned side faces up. A little charring at the edges is good, but blackened pieces taste bitter.

Checking doneness

The best test is a knife or a fork: it should slide into the thickest piece with no resistance, and the edges should be browned and crisp. If the outside is dark but the inside is still hard, turn the oven down a little and give the tray more time. If the pieces are soft but pale, turn the oven up for the last few minutes or move the tray to a higher shelf.

Taste one piece before serving. It should be tender all the way through, with a little sweetness from the browning. If the vegetables taste flat, a pinch more salt or a squeeze of lemon will lift them. Serve them while they are hot and crisp, because roasted vegetables soften as they sit.

This page is general cooking guidance, not food-safety advice. Take care with hot trays and oil, and keep the oven door closed as much as possible while roasting. Cool leftovers quickly and keep them in the fridge if you are not serving them straight away.

Seasoning and finishing

Season vegetables before roasting with salt, pepper and any dried herbs or spices you like, tossed with the oil so the coating is even. Fresh herbs, garlic and anything with sugar in it, such as honey or balsamic glaze, are better added near the end, because they burn easily in a hot oven. A squeeze of lemon or a scattering of fresh herbs after roasting lifts the flavour without any risk of burning.

Let roasted vegetables rest for a few minutes after they come out of the oven. They firm up slightly as they cool and the flavours settle. Serve them straight from the tray or tip them into a warm serving dish. Leftovers reheat well in a hot oven for a few minutes, which brings back some of the crispness that a microwave cannot.

Questions people ask

What temperature is best for roasting vegetables?

Most vegetables roast well at 190 to 220 degrees Celsius. Dense root vegetables suit the hotter end of the range, while soft vegetables such as courgettes and mushrooms do better at the cooler end so they do not burn before they cook through.

Why do my roasted vegetables go soggy?

The usual cause is a crowded tray, which traps steam and stops browning. Spread vegetables in a single layer with space between the pieces, use a hot oven, and do not cover the tray.

Should I turn vegetables halfway through roasting?

Yes. Turning halfway gives even browning on both sides and stops the bottom from burning while the top stays pale. One turn is usually enough.

Can I roast different vegetables together?

Yes, if you match their cooking times. Start dense vegetables such as potatoes and carrots first, then add quicker ones such as peppers and courgettes partway through, so everything finishes at the same time.

How much oil do roast vegetables need?

Enough to coat every piece lightly, usually one to two tablespoons for a tray. Too little oil and the vegetables dry out and stick; too much and they fry instead of roasting.
