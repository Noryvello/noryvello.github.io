Excel Budget Template Guide

A budget spreadsheet is three simple sheets: money in, money out, and a summary that shows what is left. This guide gives you the column layouts, the categories and the formulas, so you can build it yourself in Excel.

A budget does not need fancy software. A simple spreadsheet with three sheets — money in, money out, and a summary — will tell you everything you need to know: how much came in, where it went, and what is left. The best part is that you build it yourself, so you understand every number in it.

This guide gives you the structure: the three sheets, the column layout for each one, a category list to use, and the formulas in plain words. It works in Excel and in any other spreadsheet app, and you can set the whole thing up in under half an hour.

This guide describes a spreadsheet for you to build yourself — it does not download any file, and the formulas are written in plain words so they work in any spreadsheet app.

The three sheets

Your workbook has three sheets, and each one has a single job. The Income sheet lists every bit of money that comes in during the month. The Spending sheet lists every bit of money that goes out, one row per purchase. The Summary sheet pulls the totals together and shows, category by category, what you planned to spend, what you spent, and what is left.

Keeping the three jobs apart is what makes the budget easy to use. When money arrives, you add one row to the Income sheet. When you spend, you add one row to the Spending sheet. You never type numbers into the Summary sheet by hand — its cells point at the other two sheets, so it updates itself every time you add a row.

Make one workbook per month, or one workbook with a new set of sheets each month — whichever you find easier. Name the sheets clearly, for example "Income Jan", "Spending Jan" and "Summary Jan", so you can always find the month you want.

Column layouts

Each sheet has its own columns, and keeping them the same every month is what makes the formulas work. The Income sheet has four columns: Date, Source, Amount and Notes. The Date tells you when the money arrived, the Source says where it came from (your job, side work, a refund), the Amount is the number, and Notes is for anything worth remembering.

The Spending sheet has five columns: Date, Category, Item, Amount and Notes. The Category column is the most important one in the whole workbook — it must use the same short list of categories every time, spelled the same way, because the Summary sheet adds up spending by category. If you write "Food" one day and "food" the next, or "Groceries" instead of "Food", the totals will split and your summary will be wrong.

The Summary sheet has four columns: Category, Planned, Spent and Left. There is one row per category. Planned is the amount you meant to spend that month, which you type in at the start of the month. Spent is filled in by a formula that adds up the Spending sheet for that category. Left is Planned minus Spent, and it tells you at a glance which categories are on track and which have run over.

The category list

Start with a short list of categories — eight is plenty. Housing covers rent and household costs. Food covers groceries and eating out (or split them into two categories if eating out is a habit you want to watch). Transport covers fares, fuel and parking. Bills covers phone, internet and utilities. Health covers medicine and appointments. Fun covers films, hobbies and treats. Savings is money you move aside on purpose. Other catches anything that fits nowhere else.

Eight categories is a good starting point because it is enough to see where the money goes without turning every purchase into a decision. If the Other category grows large, look at what is in it — it usually means you need a new category of your own, not that the system has failed.

Resist the urge to make dozens of categories. A budget with thirty categories is a chore to keep, and a budget that is a chore gets abandoned. A budget with eight categories that you actually update every week is far more useful than a detailed one you stopped using in February.

The formulas in plain words

You only need four formulas, and they are all simple. SUM adds up a column: use it at the bottom of the Amount column on the Income sheet to get your total income, and at the bottom of the Amount column on the Spending sheet to get your total spending. The formula names the range of cells to add, like the Amount cells from row 2 down to row 31.

Subtraction gives you what is left: on the Summary sheet, the Left column is Planned minus Spent, one row at a time. At the bottom of the Summary sheet, subtract total spending from total income to see what is left for the whole month. A positive number means you are ahead; a negative number means you spent more than came in.

SUMIF adds up only the rows that match a category: on the Summary sheet, the Spent figure for Food is the sum of every Amount on the Spending sheet where the Category says Food. This is the formula that makes the whole workbook work, and it is the reason the category names must match exactly.

AVERAGE finds the typical value of a column: use it on a few months of spending to see what you usually spend on each category, which is the honest starting point for next month's Planned figures. COUNT tells you how many entries are in a column, which is a handy check that you have not missed a week.

A monthly routine

A budget only works if you keep it up to date, and the routine that works for most people is small and regular. Add spending as you go, or once a day in the evening — it takes a minute, and a day's spending is easy to remember. Once a week, open the Summary sheet and check which categories are close to their limit. Once a month, look back over the whole month, move the leftover into savings if there is any, and set next month's Planned figures based on what actually happened.

If a category runs over, do not delete the rows or fudge the numbers — the whole point of the budget is to see the truth. Instead, look at what pushed it over and decide whether the plan was too tight or the spending was too loose. A budget that shows you overspent on eating out is doing its job; a budget that hides it is not.

After three months you will have enough history to plan properly. The AVERAGE formula on three months of real spending gives you Planned figures you can actually trust, and the monthly review takes only a few minutes once the habit is set.

Questions people ask

Do I need Excel for this?

No. The layouts and formulas in this guide work in any spreadsheet app, including free ones. The formula names are the same in almost every spreadsheet program.

How many categories should I use?

Eight is a good starting point: housing, food, transport, bills, health, fun, savings and other. Add a category only when the Other column grows large enough to need one.

What if my income changes each month?

That is fine — the Income sheet simply lists what actually arrived each month, and the totals update themselves. Budget from the lowest income you expect, and treat extra income as a bonus.

How do I handle a month where I overspend?

Do not delete the rows or change the numbers. Look at which category ran over, decide whether the plan was too tight or the spending was too loose, and set a realistic figure for next month.

Can I use this budget on my phone?

Yes. Most spreadsheet apps work on phones, and adding one row per purchase takes seconds. Many people add spending on their phone and review the summary on a computer once a week.
