7 Mistakes to Avoid When Buying a Guitar

The most common mistake when buying a guitar is choosing the wrong size or a guitar that is hard to play, which makes learning harder than it needs to be. This guide covers the seven mistakes buyers make most often, with a size picker and a first-guitar checklist.

A first guitar should make learning feel possible, not painful. The right guitar is one you can hold comfortably, press the strings down without a fight, and pick up happily every day. The wrong guitar — too big, too hard to play, badly set up — quietly pushes beginners away from playing altogether.

The good news is that choosing well is mostly about fit and feel, not about spending more. Work through the mistakes below, use the size picker, and try the guitar in person if you possibly can.

The 7 mistakes to avoid

  1. Buying the wrong size. A guitar that is too big is hard to hold, hard to reach around, and tiring to play — especially for children and smaller adults. Guitars come in fractional sizes for younger players, and the right size depends on the player's height more than their age. Use the size picker below as a starting point, and if you can, hold the guitar before you buy it to check that it feels comfortable.
  2. Choosing looks over playability. A beautiful finish means nothing if the guitar is hard to play. What matters most is a comfortable neck, strings that sit close to the fretboard, and tuning that stays steady. Press a few strings down and notice how much effort it takes — if your fingers ache after a minute, the guitar will fight you every time you practise. Playability beats appearance every time.
  3. Skipping the setup. A basic setup — adjusting the string height, checking the neck and fitting fresh strings — can turn a hard guitar into an easy one. Many guitars arrive from the factory needing a setup, and many sellers will include one if you ask. Before you buy, check whether a setup is included, and if not, factor it into your plans. It makes a bigger difference than almost anything else.
  4. Buying the cheapest option without checking it. Very cheap guitars can have high string action, rough frets and tuning that slips, all of which make learning harder. That does not mean you need to spend a lot — it means you should check the guitar before you buy it. Play every string, listen for buzzing, and check that the tuners turn smoothly. A careful check matters more than the price tag.
  5. Forgetting the extras. A guitar on its own is only part of what you need. A tuner, spare strings, a case or bag, a strap and some picks are the usual extras, and an electric guitar also needs an amplifier and a cable. Check what comes with the guitar and make a list of what you still need, so your first practice session is not delayed by a missing tuner or a broken string with no spare.
  6. Not trying it in person. Guitars vary, even between two of the same model. If you can, hold the guitar, strum it, and play a few notes before you buy. Notice how it feels against your body, how the neck sits in your hand, and how it sounds. If you are buying online, read the returns policy carefully, because a guitar that looks right on screen can feel wrong in your hands.
  7. Ignoring the type. Acoustic, classical and electric guitars suit different music and different hands. Classical guitars have wider necks and softer strings, acoustic guitars are the all-round choice for strumming and fingerpicking, and electric guitars need an amplifier but are often easier on the fingers. Choose the type that matches the music you want to play, not the one that happens to be on offer.

Before you buy

Once you know the size and type you need, give the guitar a final check. Play every string and listen for buzzing or dead notes, turn each tuner to check it moves smoothly, and look along the neck to check it looks straight. Ask the seller about a setup and about the returns policy. Then take the guitar home and start with something simple — a few chords and a song you like are a much better beginning than a complicated piece that leaves you frustrated.

Questions people ask

What size guitar should a child play?

It depends on the child's height more than their age, so use the size picker on this page as a starting point. A guitar that is too big is hard to hold and play, so try the size in person before you buy.

Acoustic or electric for a beginner?

Choose the type that matches the music you want to play. Acoustic guitars need no extra gear, while electric guitars need an amplifier, so think about practice noise and the extra equipment before you decide.

What should I look for in a first guitar?

Look for the right size, a comfortable neck, strings that are easy to press down, and tuning that stays steady. A guitar that is easy to play will keep you practising far longer than one chosen for its looks.

Do I need lessons to learn guitar?

You can learn a great deal on your own, and many players do. A teacher can help you build good habits early and fix problems before they become hard to change.

How often should I change guitar strings?

It depends on how much you play. Change the strings when they sound dull, feel rough, or will not stay in tune, and keep a spare set ready so a broken string never stops your practice.
