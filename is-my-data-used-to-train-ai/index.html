Is My Data Used to Train AI? How to Check and Opt Out

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

Your chats, uploads, and public posts may be used to train AI systems, depending on the service and the settings you choose. This guide explains how training-data use works in general terms, where to find the controls for the four major services, and how to opt out where that option exists. Settings and policies change often, so the guide also shows you how to re-check them yourself.

When you type a question into a chatbot, your words go to the company's servers. What happens next depends on the service, your plan, and your settings. Some services use conversations to improve their models. Some do not. Some let you opt out, and some offer controls that limit how your data is used. The details vary, and they change over time, which is why this is a confusing topic.

This guide does not try to list every policy in full, because policies change and any list would go out of date. Instead, it explains the general picture, shows you where to find each major service's controls, and gives you habits that protect your privacy whatever the current policy says.

Policies and settings change, and this guide is general information only, not legal advice. Always check the current privacy settings inside each service you use, and read the current privacy policy for anything important. If a control described here has moved or changed, the service's own settings page is the authority.

How training-data use works

AI models learn from data. During training, a model is shown enormous amounts of text, images, or other material, and it adjusts itself until it can predict patterns in that material. The data can come from public sources, licensed material, and, in some cases, from users' interactions with the service.

When a service uses your chats for training, your words may become part of the material the next version of the model learns from. Companies usually say they remove identifying details first, but the process is not perfect, and the safest assumption is that anything you type could be seen by someone, or learned by a model, in some form.

Not all services work this way. Some services promise never to train on your conversations. Some train only on certain plans, or only when you have not opted out. Some use your data to improve the service in other ways, such as fixing errors, without using it for training. The differences matter, and they are written in each service's privacy policy and settings pages.

ChatGPT: where the controls live

ChatGPT is made by OpenAI, and its data controls live in the settings area of the app or website. In general terms, you can find options that control whether your conversations are used to improve the model, and separate options for chat history and memory. The exact names and locations of these settings change as the product is updated.

Look for a settings or privacy section in your account, and read each option before you change it. Turning off one option may not turn off every kind of data use, so it is worth reading the short explanation next to each control. If you use ChatGPT at work or school, your organisation may manage some of these settings for you.

It is also worth knowing that deleting a chat does not always delete the data at once. Companies usually keep deleted data for a limited time for safety and legal reasons, then remove it. The details are in the privacy policy, and they are worth a quick read if you have shared anything sensitive.

Claude: where the controls live

Claude is made by Anthropic, and its privacy approach is described in its settings and privacy pages. In general terms, Claude offers controls over how your conversations are used, and the company publishes explanations of what it does and does not use for training. As with every service, the details and the exact location of the controls change over time.

Open the settings area of your Claude account and look for privacy or data options. Read the explanations attached to each one, rather than guessing from the label alone. If you are unsure what an option does, the company's help pages explain it in plain language.

If you use Claude through another product or through your workplace, different rules may apply. A service built on Claude may have its own data policy, separate from the one on Claude's own site. When in doubt, check the policy of the product you are actually using, not just the underlying model.

Gemini: where the controls live

Gemini is made by Google, and its data controls sit inside your Google account settings. In general terms, you can find options that control whether your Gemini activity is saved, how long it is kept, and whether it is used to improve the service. Because Gemini is part of a larger account system, some of these controls are shared with other Google products.

The activity controls are the most important place to look. They decide whether your conversations are stored, and for how long. There are usually options to delete past activity and to turn off future saving. Read each option carefully, because turning off one kind of saving may not affect another.

Google's privacy policy covers all of its products together, so the Gemini-specific details may be spread across several pages. The help pages for Gemini are the best starting point, and they link to the relevant account settings directly.

Meta AI: where the controls live

Meta AI is made by Meta, the company behind Facebook, Instagram, and WhatsApp. Its data controls live in the privacy settings of those apps and in your Meta account settings. In general terms, Meta offers options about how your activity and public posts are used, and it provides forms and settings for objecting to certain uses of your data.

Because Meta AI appears inside several apps, the controls may be in more than one place. Check the settings inside each app where you use it, and check your central Meta account settings as well. The exact options and their locations vary by country and by app, so the settings you see may differ from what someone else sees.

Public posts are a separate question from chats. Material you have posted publicly may be used differently from private conversations, and the controls for the two may be in different places. If you want to limit how your public posts are used, look for the objection or opt-out options in the privacy settings, and read what they cover.

What opting out does and does not do

Opting out usually means the service will not use your future conversations to train its models. It does not usually erase data that was already used, and it does not stop the service from storing your chats so it can show them back to you. It is a limit on one kind of use, not a full deletion of your data.

Opting out also does not make your chats private in the full sense. The company can still see your conversations for safety checks, abuse prevention, and support, and it may keep them for a limited time after you delete them. If you need true privacy, the only reliable option is not to share the sensitive information at all.

It is worth checking your opt-out status from time to time. Settings can change when a service updates its terms, when you switch plans, or when you sign in on a new device. A quick check every few months takes minutes and catches any change you did not notice.

Everyday habits that limit what you share

The simplest habit is to share less. Before you paste something into a chatbot, ask whether it contains anything you would mind a stranger reading. If it does, remove the sensitive parts, replace real names with placeholders, or describe the problem in general terms instead of pasting the real document.

A second habit is to use the right tool for the job. If you need to work with private documents, look for tools that process your files on your own device, or for services that promise not to train on your data. The privacy pages of the services you use are the place to check these promises.

A third habit is to clean up regularly. Delete old conversations you no longer need, review your saved activity, and check your settings after any major update to the app. These small routines do not require technical skill, and together they reduce the amount of your data that sits on other people's servers.

Questions people ask

Does opting out delete my past conversations?

Usually not. Opting out normally stops future use of your conversations for training. Past conversations are handled separately, and you may need to delete them yourself. Deleted data is often kept for a limited time for safety and legal reasons before it is removed.

Do free and paid plans treat my data differently?

Sometimes. Some services offer stronger privacy controls on paid plans, or promise not to train on paid-plan conversations. The details vary by service and change over time, so check the privacy page for the plan you actually use.

Can I check what a service has stored about me?

Most major services offer a way to download your data or review your saved activity. Look for a data export or activity review option in your account settings. The export usually includes your conversations and account details.

Is my data safer if I do not have an account?

Not necessarily. Using a service without an account may limit what is linked to your identity, but the service can still see and store what you type. An account with strong privacy settings may protect you better than anonymous use with default settings.

What if I already shared something sensitive with a chatbot?

Delete the conversation, check your privacy settings, and change any passwords or codes you shared. If you shared identity documents or financial details, contact the relevant organisation and watch for unusual activity. You cannot undo the sharing, but you can limit the damage.
