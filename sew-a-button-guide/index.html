How to Sew a Button

Sewing a button back on takes a needle, matching thread and a few minutes, and the method depends on whether the button is flat or has a shank. This guide shows you both methods, with a tools checklist and a strength test at the end.

A loose button is one of the smallest repairs in the house and one of the most satisfying. Left alone, it falls off in the street or in the wash, and the garment sits unworn in a drawer. Sewn back on properly, it holds as well as it did when the garment was new.

Buttons come in two kinds. A flat button, also called a sew-through button, has two or four holes and sits flat against the fabric. A shank button has a small loop underneath instead of holes, which holds it away from the fabric. The sewing method is different for each, so pick your type below before you start.

Needles are sharp, so work in good light, keep needles away from children, and use a thimble if you have one. Never put a needle in your mouth, and put it back in a pincushion or needle case when you stop.

Thread colour matters more than thread type for a button repair. A close match disappears into the button, while a contrasting thread draws the eye to the repair. If you cannot match the colour exactly, choose a shade slightly darker than the fabric, which shows less than a lighter one.

Threading and starting

Cut a length of thread about as long as your forearm. Longer thread tangles, and shorter thread runs out before the button is firm. Thread the needle, pull the thread through until the ends are even, and tie a small knot in the doubled end.

Doubled thread is the usual choice for a button, because it is stronger and fills the holes faster. Single thread is fine for a small, light button on thin fabric, but for a coat button or a button that gets a lot of pulling, doubled thread holds far better. Whichever you use, keep the tension even so the stitches sit neatly.

Mark where the button goes. The old stitch holes usually show the spot, and the buttonhole on the other side tells you where the button must line up. Hold the button in place and check that it meets the buttonhole before you sew a single stitch.

Start from the back of the fabric. Push the needle up from underneath so the knot sits hidden on the inside, and take one or two small anchor stitches in the same spot to hold the thread before you start on the button itself.

The strength test

When the button is sewn on, test it before you put the garment away. Tug the button gently in every direction. It should feel firm with no wobble, and the stitches should hold without pulling the fabric into a pucker.

Check that the button passes through the buttonhole. A button sewn too tightly against thick fabric will not fasten, and a button with too long a shank will flop around. If it does not fasten smoothly, undo the finishing stitches and adjust the shank before you finish off again.

Look at the back of the fabric. The finishing stitches should be small and flat, with no loops of thread hanging loose. Trim the thread end close to the fabric, and the repair is done.

Fixing common problems

If the thread keeps tangling, it is probably too long, or you are pulling the needle through too fast. Cut a shorter length, let the needle hang and unwind now and then, and pull each stitch through in one smooth motion rather than in jerks.

If the button sits crooked, the anchor stitches were probably off-centre. Undo the work, re-mark the position using the buttonhole as your guide, and start again. A button that is sewn on crooked pulls the garment out of shape every time it is fastened.

If the thread shows on the outside of the garment, your stitches are landing too far from the button. Keep each stitch close to the button edge and under the button itself, so the thread stays hidden beneath it.

Looking after the repair

A sewn-on button lasts longer if the garment is fastened and unfastened gently rather than yanked. When you wash the garment, fasten the buttons first, which stops them catching on other clothes in the wash and pulling at the stitches. Turn the garment inside out for washing if the fabric allows it, which protects both the buttons and the stitching.

Check the other buttons on the garment while you have the needle out. Buttons on the same garment usually wear at the same rate, so if one has come loose, another is often close behind. A few extra stitches on a wobbly button now saves a second repair later, and it takes only a minute while the thread is already threaded.

Questions people ask

How much thread do I need?

Cut a length about as long as your forearm. A longer thread tangles and knots as you sew, and a shorter one runs out before the button is firm. If it runs out, finish off and start a fresh length.

Why does my thread keep tangling?

Long thread tangles more than short thread, and pulling the needle through too fast twists it. Use a shorter length, let the needle hang and unwind now and then, and pull each stitch through smoothly.

How tight should the button be?

Firm, but with a little room underneath so it can pass through the buttonhole. For a flat button, the thread shank gives that room. A button sewn flat against thick fabric will be hard to fasten.

What if the button has four holes?

Sew through them in pairs, either in a cross or in two parallel lines. Match the pattern of the other buttons on the garment so the repair does not stand out.

Can I sew a button on without a spare?

Yes, if the button itself is still whole. If the button is cracked or missing, check inside the garment for a spare, since many garments have one sewn inside a seam or on a spare tag.
