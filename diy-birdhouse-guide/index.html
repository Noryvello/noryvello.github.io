DIY Birdhouse Guide

A birdhouse is a satisfying first woodworking project, and birds will use a well-built box for years. This guide covers the materials, the cutting and assembly steps, where to hang the finished box, and the safety checks to run before you start.

Safety checklist

Run through the list before you pick up a saw, and tick each item off as you check it.

This page is general DIY guidance only. Use untreated wood, keep cutting tools away from children, and always clamp your work before cutting or drilling. If you are unsure about a tool, ask someone experienced to show you first.

What you need

A birdhouse is a small project with a short materials list. You need one board of untreated softwood, such as pine, long enough to cut the six parts of the box: a floor, a front, a back, two sides, and a roof. You also need wood screws or nails, a saw, a drill, sandpaper, and a pencil and ruler for marking out. A small hinge or a removable panel for the front or roof is worth adding, so you can clean the box out at the end of the season.

Use untreated wood only. Treated timber, plywood with formaldehyde glue, and painted wood can all harm birds, especially in a small closed box on a warm day. The wood should be thick enough to insulate the nest — a board about the thickness of your thumb is a good guide. If you want to decorate the outside, use a water-based exterior paint and leave the inside bare.

Cutting the parts

  1. Measure and mark the floor. Mark out a square floor piece, then mark the front and back pieces to match its width.
  2. Mark the sides. The side pieces are the same width as the floor and tall enough to give the birds room to nest, with a sloped top edge so the roof sits at an angle and rain runs off.
  3. Mark the roof. The roof piece is wider than the box on every side, so it overhangs and keeps rain out of the entrance hole.
  4. Cut along the lines. Clamp each piece to your bench and cut slowly along the marked lines, keeping your hands behind the blade.
  5. Drill the entrance hole. Drill the entrance hole in the front piece, near the top. The right size depends on the birds you hope to attract, so check a local bird guide for the size that suits your target species.
  6. Drill drainage holes. Drill a few small holes in the floor so rain can drain out, and a couple of small holes near the top of the sides for air.
  7. Sand the edges. Sand every cut edge smooth, and roughen the inside of the front piece below the entrance hole so young birds can climb out.

Assembling the box

Start with the floor and the front. Fix the front to the floor with two screws, then add the back the same way. Then fix the sides in place, one at a time, checking that the box sits square as you go. Screws hold better than nails and make the box easier to open for cleaning. Pre-drill each screw hole so the wood does not split, especially near the ends of the boards.

Leave the roof until last. Fix it with screws through the top of the front and back, or hinge it along the back edge so you can lift it to clean the box. A hinged roof is the most practical choice: you can open the box at the end of the season, clear out the old nest, and leave the box clean for the next year. Do not add a perch below the entrance hole — perches help predators, not nesting birds.

Check the finished box before you hang it. Every joint should be tight, with no gaps that let rain in. The entrance hole should be smooth, with no splinters. Give the box a shake: if it rattles, tighten the screws. A well-built box lasts for years with no maintenance beyond an annual clean.

Where to hang it

Place the box where birds will feel safe. Hang it on a tree, a wall, or a post, with the entrance hole facing away from the prevailing wind and out of the hottest afternoon sun. The box should be high enough to be out of easy reach of cats and other predators — about head height or higher — and it should have a clear flight path to the entrance hole, without branches blocking the way.

Give the box time. Birds may take a season to find a new box, and a box that stays empty in its first year may be used the next. Do not open the box while birds are nesting, and do not disturb the nest to look inside. At the end of the season, once the birds have left, open the box, clear out the old nest, and leave it clean for next year. A clean box is healthier for the next family of birds.

Questions people ask

What wood should I use for a birdhouse?

Use untreated softwood, such as pine, thick enough to insulate the nest. Avoid treated timber, plywood with formaldehyde glue, and painted wood, as these can harm birds in a small closed box. Leave the inside of the box unpainted.

How big should the entrance hole be?

It depends on the birds you hope to attract — different species need different hole sizes. Check a local bird guide for the size that suits your target species, and drill the hole near the top of the front piece.

Should I add a perch to a birdhouse?

No. A perch below the entrance hole helps predators reach the nest, and nesting birds do not need one — they fly straight in and out. Leave the front plain and roughen the inside below the hole so young birds can climb out.

Where is the best place to hang a birdhouse?

Hang it on a tree, a wall, or a post, high enough to be out of easy reach of cats, with the entrance facing away from the prevailing wind and out of the hottest afternoon sun. Leave a clear flight path to the entrance hole.

How do I clean a birdhouse?

Wait until the end of the season, once the birds have left, then open the box and clear out the old nest. A hinged roof or a removable panel makes this easy. Do not open the box or disturb the nest while birds are using it.
