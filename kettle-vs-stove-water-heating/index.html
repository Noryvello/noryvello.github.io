Why a kettle is usually faster

A kettle is built for one job: heating water. Its heating element sits in direct contact with the water, so most of the energy goes straight into the water itself. A stove heats a pan first and the water second, and much of the heat escapes around the sides of the pan and into the room. That is why a kettle usually brings the same amount of water to the boil in less time — and why it typically uses less energy to do it.

Energy: where the heat actually goes

The energy any method uses comes down to power multiplied by time: watts times minutes, converted to kilowatt-hours. A kettle often draws more power than a stove ring, but it runs for far fewer minutes, so the total is usually lower. The easiest way to waste energy is to boil more water than you need — a full kettle for one cup heats water you will only reheat later. Match the amount to the job and the kettle wins by more.

Scale and limescale

If your water is hard, white scale builds up inside a kettle over time. A scaled kettle heats more slowly and can make water taste flat, so it is worth descaling now and then following the maker's instructions. A stove pan scales too, but it is usually easier to scrub. Never fill past the maximum mark — boiling water that spills over is a burn risk as well as a waste.

Which suits whom?

A kettle suits anyone who boils water daily for tea, coffee, or cooking — it is faster, usually cheaper per litre, and switches itself off. A stove covers what a kettle cannot: heating a large pot, working where there is no kettle, or simmering something that needs to stay on the heat. Should I fill the kettle only as much as I need?

Yes, as a rule — heating extra water wastes energy and time. Most kettles have a minimum fill mark; stay above it so the element stays covered, and do not boil the same water over and over.
