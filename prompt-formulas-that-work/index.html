7 Prompt Formulas That Work for Any AI Chatbot

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

A prompt formula is a reusable pattern: fill in the blanks and you get a clear prompt without starting from scratch. These seven formulas cover the most common jobs — writing, planning, learning, and improving — and each one works on any chatbot.

Good prompts are not written from nothing. Behind most of them is a pattern — a shape that works again and again, whatever the topic. Learning a few patterns is faster than learning a hundred separate prompts.

Each formula below comes with a filled example, so you can see it working, and a blank template, so you can copy it and fill it in with your own task. Try one today on something real.

Formulas improve your prompts, but they cannot guarantee correct answers. AI chatbots can still be wrong or out of date, so check important facts against a trusted source before you rely on them.

What a Prompt Formula Is

A formula is a skeleton with slots: some words stay the same every time, and the bracketed parts change with your task. "Act as a [role] and help me [task] for [audience]" is a formula; fill in the brackets and you have a prompt. Formulas help in two ways. First, they remind you what a good prompt needs — a role, a task, a limit — so you stop forgetting the parts that matter. Second, they make prompting fast: instead of composing from scratch, you adapt a pattern you trust. The seven below are the most versatile ones, and they combine well.

Formula 1: Role + Task + Context

Use it when the tone, level, or angle of the answer matters. Filled example: "Act as a friendly gardening coach. Help me plan what to plant in a small sunny balcony in spring, for someone who has never grown anything. Keep it to five plants and explain why each one is a good first choice." Blank template: "Act as a [role]. Help me [task], for [audience or situation]. Keep it to [limit] and [extra instruction]." The role sets the voice, the task sets the job, and the context keeps the answer realistic. This is the most useful formula to learn first, because it fits almost any task.

Formula 2: Task + Format + Limits

Use it when you want a tidy answer in a specific shape. Filled example: "List the main steps of changing a bicycle tyre, as seven numbered steps, one sentence each, for a complete beginner. Leave out any tools a beginner would not own." Blank template: "[Task], as [format], for [audience]. [Limit or thing to leave out]." This formula is the fastest way to get a usable answer: the task says what, the format says how it should look, and the limit cuts the clutter. Use it whenever you plan to copy the answer into a message, a note, or a plan.

Formula 3: Example-Led

Use it when you want a particular style, tone, or structure that is hard to describe. Filled example: "Write three short welcome messages for new members of a walking club, in the warm, plain style of this one: 'Welcome aboard — lace up, show up, and the rest takes care of itself.' Keep each under twenty words." Blank template: "[Task], in the style of this example: '[your example]'. [Any limits]." An example does what adjectives cannot: it shows the chatbot exactly what you mean by "warm" or "plain." One good example is often enough; two or three set a stronger pattern.

Formula 4: Step-by-Step

Use it when the task has stages, or you want to see the reasoning. Filled example: "Show me, step by step, how to work out how many litres of paint I need for one wall that is 4 metres wide and 2.5 metres high, if one litre covers 10 square metres. Show each calculation on its own line." Blank template: "Show me, step by step, how to [task]. [Any facts the chatbot needs]. Show each step on its own line." Step-by-step prompts make the reasoning visible, so you can spot where an answer goes wrong — and they give you a plan you can actually follow.

Formula 5: Persona Interview

Use it when you want to explore an idea by answering questions rather than reading a lecture. Filled example: "You are an interviewer helping me decide whether to start a small vegetable garden. Ask me one question at a time about my space, my time, and my experience, and wait for my answer before asking the next one." Blank template: "You are an interviewer helping me [decision or topic]. Ask me one question at a time about [three things to cover], and wait for my answer before asking the next one." The chatbot asks, you answer, and the plan builds from your real situation instead of a generic template.

Formula 6: Critique and Improve

Use it when you have a draft and want it better, not replaced. Filled example: "Here is a draft of a short speech for a school prize-giving. First, tell me three things that work well and three things to improve. Then rewrite it with your improvements, keeping it under 200 words." Blank template: "Here is my draft: [paste your draft]. First, tell me [number] things that work well and [number] things to improve. Then rewrite it with your improvements, [any limits]." Asking for critique first keeps you in control: you see what would change and why before the rewrite appears.

Formula 7: Table Request

Use it when you want to compare options side by side. Filled example: "Compare three ways to learn the guitar — self-taught with videos, weekly lessons with a teacher, and a group class — in a table with these columns: approach, time needed each week, main advantage, main drawback. Keep each cell to one short sentence." Blank template: "Compare [options] in a table with these columns: [column 1], [column 2], [column 3]. Keep each cell to [limit]." Tables force the chatbot to be brief and parallel, which makes differences easy to see at a glance. Name the columns yourself — the chatbot will invent unhelpful ones if you do not.

Mixing Formulas for Harder Tasks

The formulas are not separate tools; they combine. A role plus a table request gives you a comparison in a chosen voice. An example-led prompt plus step-by-step gives you reasoning in a style you like. A persona interview that ends with a table gives you a plan shaped by your answers and laid out neatly. When a task feels too big for one formula, split it: use one formula for the outline and another for the details. The goal is not to memorise seven patterns perfectly — it is to reach for a shape instead of a blank box. Pick the formula that fits today's task, fill in the brackets, and let the pattern do the work.

Questions people ask

Do these formulas work on every chatbot?

Yes. The formulas are patterns of clear instructions, and every chatbot responds to clear instructions. A formula may need a small tweak on one chatbot or another, but the pattern itself transfers.

Which formula should I try first?

Role plus task plus context. It is the most versatile pattern and fits almost any job. Once it feels natural, add the task plus format plus limits formula for tidy answers.

Can I combine two formulas?

Yes, and harder tasks often need it. A role plus a table request, or an example-led prompt plus step-by-step, gives you the strengths of both patterns in one prompt.

What if the filled example does not fit my task?

Ignore the example's topic and copy its shape. The brackets are the point: swap in your task, your audience, and your limits, and the same pattern works.

How do I make a formula my own?

Save the ones you use most, with your own filled examples. Over time you will adjust the wording to fit how you think, and the formula becomes a habit rather than a template.
