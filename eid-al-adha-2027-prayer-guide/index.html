Eid al-Adha 2027 Prayer Guide

By the Noryvello team · Published 19 November 2026 · Updated 19 November 2026

Eid al-Adha in 2027 is expected on Sunday 16 May, subject to the sighting of the moon. This guide explains when and where the Eid prayer happens, how the prayer works, what to wear and bring, and how the day runs from the prayer to the feast.

Eid al-Adha is the festival of sacrifice, celebrated by Muslims around the world at the end of the Hajj pilgrimage season. The day begins with the Eid prayer, prayed in congregation in the morning, and continues with the Qurbani sacrifice, visits to family, and a shared feast. The prayer is the heart of the morning: it gathers the community together before the rest of the day begins.

If this is your first Eid prayer, or if you are taking children along, a little preparation makes the morning smooth. Prayer times and arrangements vary from mosque to mosque, so the most important step is to check with your local mosque. This guide covers the shape of the day, how the prayer works, and the etiquette that helps everyone enjoy it.

The date of Eid al-Adha depends on the sighting of the moon, so Sunday 16 May 2027 is expected rather than certain. Prayer times, venues, and arrangements vary by mosque, so confirm the details with your local mosque. Practices differ between traditions: follow your imam and your local community.

When and where the prayer happens

The Eid prayer is prayed in the morning, usually a short while after sunrise, in a mosque, a prayer hall, or an open space such as a park or a large hall. Many mosques hold more than one congregation on Eid morning to fit everyone in, so check which session suits you and arrive early. Outdoor prayers are common in good weather and can hold large crowds, so bring something to sit on if you are praying outside.

Because the date depends on the moon, mosques usually confirm the day only a day or two before. Keep an eye on your mosque announcements in the days before Eid, and plan your morning around the confirmed time. If you are travelling for Eid, find out about prayer arrangements at your destination before you go, so you are not searching for a venue on the morning itself.

How the Eid prayer works

The Eid prayer is two rakats, prayed in congregation behind an imam. It begins with extra takbirs, the declaration of the greatness of God, at the start of each rakat. The exact number of extra takbirs differs between traditions, so the simplest approach is to follow your imam and the congregation around you. There is no call to prayer before the Eid prayer in the usual way, and there are no extra prayers immediately before or after it in most traditions.

After the prayer, the imam gives a short sermon, called the khutbah. It is good practice to stay and listen to the sermon rather than leaving straight after the prayer. The whole gathering, prayer and sermon together, usually takes less than an hour, though large crowds can make arrival and departure slower. If you arrive late and the prayer has started, join the congregation quietly and follow the imam.

What to wear and bring

Eid is a day for wearing your best clothes, and many people wear new or freshly cleaned outfits. Choose something comfortable as well as smart, because you may be sitting on the ground and walking between venues. Modest dress is expected, and women usually bring a head covering. Children enjoy dressing up for Eid, so let them choose something they feel proud in, and dress them for the weather if the prayer is outdoors.

Bring a prayer mat if you are praying outdoors, and a small bag for your shoes. A bottle of water is sensible on a warm day, and a little money for charity is a thoughtful addition, since giving is part of the spirit of Eid. Leave valuables at home where you can, and keep your phone silent during the prayer.

The morning of Eid: getting ready

Eid morning has a rhythm of its own. Many people bathe and dress in their best clothes before heading out, and it is common to eat something light before the prayer. Families often gather at home first, exchange greetings, and travel to the prayer together. If you are hosting guests later, set out what you can the night before so the morning stays calm.

Allow more time than usual for the journey: roads near mosques are busy on Eid morning, and parking can take a while. Arriving early also means you can find a good place to pray and settle before the congregation begins. If you are bringing children, explain what will happen before you arrive, so they know to stay quiet during the prayer and the sermon.

After the prayer: the order of the day

Once the prayer and sermon are over, people greet each other, embrace family and friends, and head home or on to visits. For many families, the Qurbani sacrifice follows the prayer, and the meat is then shared with family, relatives, and people in need. The afternoon is usually given over to visiting, eating, and spending time together.

The feast is the centre of the afternoon. Many families cook from the Qurbani meat, sharing dishes with neighbours and guests. If you are visiting several homes, pace yourself: small portions at each stop are kinder than a full meal at the first house. The day often ends with a quieter evening meal and time with close family.

Eid etiquette and greetings

Greetings are a big part of Eid. The most common greeting is Eid Mubarak, meaning blessed Eid, and people often add warm wishes for the year ahead. Shake hands, embrace family and friends, and take a moment to greet people you have not seen in a while. Visiting elders is especially valued, and many families make time to call relatives who live far away.

Good manners at the prayer matter too. Keep your voice low inside the prayer space, keep children close, and avoid blocking walkways. If you are hosting, welcome guests warmly and do not worry about perfection: a simple table and a warm welcome are what people remember. If you receive gifts or money, accept them graciously.

Eid is also a time to mend fences and reconnect. If you have drifted apart from a relative or a friend, a greeting on Eid morning is a natural way to reach out. A short visit, a phone call, or even a kind message can mean a great deal, especially to someone who is spending Eid alone. The prayer gathers the community; the greetings afterwards knit it together.

Praying with children and first-timers

Children are welcome at the Eid prayer, and many mosques set aside family areas. Bring young children with you if you can: Eid morning is one of their happiest memories in the making. Explain the prayer in simple terms beforehand, bring a small quiet activity for very young children, and sit near the edge of the congregation so you can step out if needed.

If this is your first Eid prayer, do not worry about getting everything perfect. Stand where there is space, follow the people around you, and ask a steward or a friendly face if you are unsure where to go. Mosques expect newcomers on Eid and are used to helping. The most important thing is to be present, to listen, and to enjoy being part of the community.

After the prayer, take a moment to enjoy the atmosphere before heading home. The minutes after the sermon, when everyone is greeting each other and children are running between families, are part of the celebration. If you are new, introduce yourself to the people you prayed beside: Eid morning is one of the easiest times to make a new friend.
