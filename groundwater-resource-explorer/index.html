Groundwater explained simply

When rain falls on soil, some of it runs off into streams and some of it soaks down into the ground. The water that soaks down keeps going until it reaches rock or clay it cannot pass, and there it gathers, filling the cracks and pores in the rock and soil like water filling a sponge. That stored water is groundwater, and the level it reaches is called the water table. Above the water table the ground is damp; below it, the ground is saturated, and a hole dug that deep will fill with water.

Groundwater moves, but slowly. It creeps through cracks and pores, often only a few metres a year, on its way to a spring, a stream or the sea. Because it moves so slowly, groundwater can be very old: the water in a deep well may have fallen as rain hundreds or thousands of years ago. That slowness is both a strength and a weakness. It means groundwater is filtered clean by its long journey through rock and soil, and it means that once groundwater is polluted, it stays polluted for a very long time.

Almost every place has groundwater under it, but how much, and how deep, varies enormously. In a valley with gravel and sand, the water table may be only a few metres down, and a shallow well reaches it easily. On a hill of hard rock, the water table may be tens of metres down, held in cracks, and a well may need to go deep to find enough. The rock beneath your feet decides what your groundwater is like, and the official information for your area will describe it.

Aquifers and wells

An aquifer is a layer of rock or soil that holds useful amounts of groundwater and lets it flow. Sand and gravel make good aquifers, because water moves easily through the gaps between the grains. Sandstone and cracked limestone make good aquifers too. Clay makes a poor aquifer: it holds water but barely lets it move, which is why clay layers act as floors and lids that trap groundwater between them. A well is simply a hole drilled or dug down into an aquifer, with a pump or a bucket to bring the water up.

A well works by lowering the water level inside itself. When you pump water out of a well, the water table around the well dips into a cone shape, and water flows in from the aquifer to fill it. As long as the aquifer holds more water than you take, the well keeps flowing. Pump too fast, or pump for too long in a dry year, and the cone deepens until the well runs dry, even though the aquifer still holds water further away. That is why a well that worked for years can suddenly fail in a drought: the water table has fallen below the bottom of the well.

Wells come in many kinds. A dug well is wide and shallow, lined with stone or concrete, and reaches the water table near the surface. A drilled well is narrow and deep, and can reach aquifers far below. A spring is a place where groundwater reaches the surface on its own, usually where the land dips below the water table. Each kind has its own care: dug wells need their lining checked, drilled wells need their pump serviced, and springs need their surroundings kept clean, because anything spilled near a spring can reach the water fast.

What lowers groundwater

Groundwater levels fall when more water leaves the aquifer than enters it. The main natural cause is a lack of rain: in a dry year, less water soaks down, and the water table falls. The main human cause is pumping: wells for homes, farms and industry take water out, and if the total taken is more than the rain puts back, the water table falls year after year. In some places the fall is slow and steady; in others it is fast enough that wells must be deepened every few years.

Land use matters too. Paving, roofing and compacted ground send rain into drains instead of letting it soak in, so less water reaches the aquifer. Cutting down trees can change how much rain reaches the ground and how fast it runs off. Draining wetlands removes a store that used to feed groundwater slowly through the dry season. Each change is small on its own, but across a whole area they add up to a lower water table.

A falling water table has visible effects. Springs dry up, streams that used to flow all year become seasonal, and shallow wells fail. The ground itself can sink slightly where a lot of water has been removed, which can crack buildings and roads. And as the water table falls, the water that remains can become saltier or pick up minerals from deeper rock, changing its taste and its suitability for drinking. Groundwater is not endless, and treating it as if it were is how aquifers get into trouble.

Finding official information

Official groundwater information for your area is usually published by a geological survey, an environment agency or a water authority. Search for your country's geological survey together with the words groundwater map or aquifer, and look for the official map or report. Many countries publish maps showing aquifer types, water table depth and the areas where groundwater is protected, and some publish live or recent readings from monitoring wells, which show whether levels are rising or falling.

When you find information for your area, note four things. First, the aquifer type under your home: sand, gravel, sandstone, limestone or fractured rock, because each behaves differently. Second, the depth of the water table, and whether it rises and falls with the seasons. Third, whether your area is a protected groundwater zone, which affects what can be built or spilled there. Fourth, the trend: are levels in your area stable, rising or falling over the years?

If your country has no public groundwater map, your water supplier or local authority may hold the information, and a local well driller will know the ground from experience: how deep wells go in your street, what rock they pass through, and whether levels have changed. Well records, where they exist, are a goldmine: a driller's log of a nearby well tells you more about your ground than any general map can.

Wells and water safety

Well water can be excellent, and it can also be unsafe, and the difference is testing. Groundwater is filtered by its journey through rock and soil, which removes many germs, but it can still carry bacteria from a nearby septic system or animal waste, nitrates from fertiliser, and metals from rock or old pipes. A well that has never been tested is a question mark, however clear the water looks, and clear water can carry things you cannot see, smell or taste.

Test a well at least once a year, and test again after flooding, after building work near the well, or if the water changes in taste, smell or colour. The usual panel covers bacteria, nitrates and hardness, with lead added for older homes. Use a certified lab and follow its sampling instructions, and keep the reports so you can compare one year with the next. A result that changes over time tells you something is happening in the aquifer, and catching it early is far easier than fixing it late.

Keep the area around your well clean. Nothing that could pollute water should be stored, spilled or buried near it: no fuel, no pesticides, no paint, no waste. Keep animals away from the wellhead, and make sure surface water drains away from the well rather than towards it. A wellhead cover that keeps out insects and rain is a small thing that prevents large problems, and a well that is looked after will serve a home for decades.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is thinking groundwater is endless. An aquifer holds a great deal of water, but it fills only as fast as rain soaks in, and pumping more than the rain replaces will lower the water table. A well that runs dry in a drought is not bad luck: it is the aquifer telling you its limits.

The second mistake is a well that is never tested. Water that looks clear and tastes fine can carry bacteria, nitrates or metals, and the only way to know is to test. Test once a year, keep the reports, and act on any result above the official limit. A test costs far less than the illness it can prevent.

The third mistake is storing or spilling pollutants near a well or a spring. Fuel, pesticides and chemicals spilled on the ground soak down into the aquifer, and because groundwater moves so slowly, the pollution stays for years. Keep the ground around your well clean, and never pour anything down a disused well: it is a direct route into the aquifer.

The fourth mistake is ignoring a falling water table. If your well runs dry, or your pump starts sucking air, or a nearby spring disappears, the aquifer is telling you something. Ask your neighbours whether their wells have changed, check the official monitoring data for your area, and consider whether pumping in the area has grown beyond what the rain can replace.
