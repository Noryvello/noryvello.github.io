AI Images and Copyright: What Creators Should Know

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

AI image generators raise copyright questions that do not have simple answers yet: who owns an image made by a machine, whether you can sell it, and what happens if it resembles someone else's work. This guide explains the questions in general terms, how platform terms differ, and how to keep records that protect you.

Copyright law was written for human creators. It assumes a person made the work, made creative choices, and deserves control over how it is used. AI image generators complicate that picture, because the creative work is split between the person who writes the prompt, the company that built the model, and the huge collection of images the model learned from. Courts and lawmakers in different countries are still working out how the old rules apply to this new situation.

That uncertainty does not mean you should avoid AI images. It means you should understand what is settled, what is not, and what depends on the terms of the tool you use. This guide covers the questions creators ask most often, in general terms that apply broadly. It is not legal advice, and for decisions that involve real money or real risk, a qualified lawyer in your own country is the right person to ask.

Who owns an AI-generated image

The honest answer is that ownership of AI images is unsettled in many places. Traditional copyright protects works made by human authors, and an image produced by typing a short prompt into a generator sits in an awkward middle ground: the human contributed an idea, but the machine made most of the creative decisions about composition, colour, and detail.

In general terms, the more creative input a person adds, the stronger their claim tends to be. A person who writes a detailed prompt, generates many variations, selects the best, and then edits it by hand has contributed far more than someone who typed two words and downloaded the first result. But how much human input is enough, and what kind counts, is a question that different countries may answer differently, and that courts are still deciding.

There is also the question of the training data. Image models learn from large collections of images, many of which were made by human artists and photographers. If a generated image closely resembles a specific work in that training data, it can raise questions about copying, even if the person who prompted it never saw the original. This is one reason some generators filter their outputs and block certain prompts.

For most everyday creators, the practical point is this: do not assume that generating an image gives you the same rights as taking a photograph or painting a picture. Your rights depend on the tool's terms, the law where you live, and how much of your own creative work went into the final image.

How platform terms differ

Because the law is unsettled, the terms of service of the tool you use matter a great deal. The terms are a contract between you and the company, and they decide what you may do with the images you generate, regardless of what copyright law might say in theory.

Terms differ in several important ways. Some tools give you broad rights to use, share, and sell your generated images. Others give you rights only for personal use, or only on certain plans. Some tools keep a licence to use your images themselves, for example to show them in a public gallery or to improve their service. Others promise that your images stay private to you.

Terms also differ on what happens to your prompts and uploaded images. Some services use them to train future models. Others do not. Some delete your data after a set period, and some keep it. These differences are easy to overlook, but they can matter if you generate images for a business or upload your own photos as a starting point.

The practical habit is to read the terms of any tool before you use it for work that matters, and to keep a copy of the terms as they were when you generated the image. Terms change, and the version in force at the time you made the image is usually the one that governs your use of it. If a tool's terms are hard to find or hard to understand, that is a reason to be cautious.

Commercial use: questions to check

Using an AI image on a personal blog is one thing. Putting it on a product, in an advertisement, or on a book cover is another, because commercial use raises the stakes. Before using an AI image commercially, work through a short checklist.

  • Does your plan allow commercial use? Some tools restrict commercial use to paid plans, or to images generated under certain settings. Check the terms for your specific plan, not the tool in general.
  • Does the image contain a trademark or logo? AI generators sometimes reproduce brand marks, logos, or distinctive product designs. Using those commercially can cause trouble even if you did not ask for them.
  • Does the image show a recognizable real person? A generated face that happens to resemble a real person, especially a well-known one, can create problems. The same applies to recognizable characters from films, games, or books.
  • Does the image copy a distinctive style? Prompting in the style of a living artist is a grey area, and some tools block artist names entirely. Describing the style in general words is safer.
  • Does your client or platform have its own rules? Some marketplaces, publishers, and stock libraries have their own policies on AI images. Check them before you submit work.

If any answer on the checklist is unclear, the cautious choice is to use a different image, or to create the image yourself. Commercial use is where disputes actually happen, so it is where caution pays off most.

Using other people's images as inputs

Many image tools let you upload a photo and ask the AI to change it, extend it, or use it as a starting point. This raises a separate set of questions, because now the input image has its own owner.

If the input is your own photo, there is no problem. If it belongs to someone else, you need their permission before using it as an input, just as you would need permission to edit and republish their photo in any other way. Uploading a copyrighted photo to an AI tool does not make it yours, and the output can still infringe the original owner's rights if it keeps recognizable parts of their work.

The same caution applies to images you find online. A photo on a search results page is not free to use just because it is visible. Stock photos, press photos, and artwork all have owners and licences. If you want to use an image as an input, use one you made yourself, one with a licence that allows it, or one that is clearly in the public domain.

There is also a privacy angle. Uploading a photo of another person as an input, especially to change their appearance or place them in a new scene, can be harmful and may break privacy rules. Never use someone else's photo as an AI input without their consent.

How to keep good records

Good records will not settle every question, but they make your position much stronger if anyone asks how an image was made. They also help you remember what you did, which matters when you generate hundreds of images over months.

For each image you publish or sell, keep the prompt you used, the name of the tool and the plan you were on, the date you generated it, and a note of any edits you made afterwards. If you made significant edits by hand, keep a copy of the version before and after editing, because that shows your own creative contribution.

Keep a copy of the tool's terms as they stood on the date you generated the image. Terms pages change, and a screenshot or saved copy is much more useful than a memory of what the terms said. If the tool offers a licence or usage statement for your images, save that too.

Organize your records simply. A folder per project, with the image, a short text file holding the prompt and details, and a copy of the terms, is enough. The goal is to be able to answer, months later, where an image came from and what you were allowed to do with it.

What to do if someone uses your image

If you find someone using an image you generated, your options depend on how much of the image is genuinely yours and where the use is happening. The first step is to gather your records: the prompt, the generation date, and your edits. These show that you made the image, even if the legal strength of your claim is uncertain.

Many platforms have a reporting process for copied content. A polite message to the person using the image, asking them to credit you or remove it, often works. For commercial misuse, or for a dispute that involves real money, a lawyer can tell you what your options are in your country.

It is worth being realistic about the limits. If an image was made with a short prompt and little editing, it may be hard to claim strong rights over it, and another person could generate a very similar image independently. The images you have the strongest claim over are the ones where you added the most of your own work: detailed prompts, careful selection, and substantial editing.

AI images, trademarks, and recognisable characters

Copyright is not the only issue. Trademarks protect brand names, logos, and distinctive product designs, and they work differently from copyright. A generated image that includes a brand logo, even by accident, can cause problems if you use it commercially.

Recognisable characters are a similar risk. A generated image of a famous cartoon character, a film hero, or a well-known mascot is likely to reproduce protected work, and the companies behind those characters protect them actively. Prompting for a character by name is the most obvious risk, but a generator can also produce a lookalike from a general description.

The safest approach is to avoid brand names, logos, and character names in your prompts, and to check every generated image for accidental lookalikes before you use it. If an image contains a logo or a recognisable character, either edit it out or generate a different image. A few minutes of checking is much cheaper than a dispute.

Questions people ask

Do I own the AI images I generate?

It depends on the tool you used and where you live. Many image generators give you broad rights to use your outputs, while others keep more rights for themselves. Copyright law in many places is still unsettled on whether an image made mostly by a machine can be owned at all, so read the terms of the tool you use.

Can I sell products with AI images on them?

Often yes, but it depends on the tool's terms and on what the image contains. Check whether your plan allows commercial use, and avoid images that reproduce trademarks, logos, or recognizable characters belonging to someone else.

Can someone else use my AI-generated image?

Possibly. If an AI image has weak or no copyright protection in your country, it may be hard to stop others from using a similar image. Keeping records of your prompts and generation dates helps show how the image was made, but it does not guarantee protection.

Is it safe to use a real artist's name in an image prompt?

It is risky. Prompting in the style of a living artist can raise legal and ethical questions, and some tools block artist names for that reason. Describing the style in general words, such as watercolour landscape or bold poster style, is a safer approach.

What records should I keep for AI images I publish?

Keep the prompt, the tool and plan you used, the date, and any edits you made afterwards. If you use the image commercially, keep a copy of the terms that applied at the time, since terms change and the version in force when you generated the image is the one that matters.
