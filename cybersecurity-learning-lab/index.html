Cybersecurity Learning Lab

Staying safe online is a set of learnable skills, not a talent you are born with. This learning lab teaches those skills in five short lessons — passwords, phishing, two-factor authentication, safe browsing and updates — each ending with questions you can check yourself against.

This lab teaches you how to protect yourself online. It never teaches hacking or how to break into accounts, devices or networks — if a page ever offers that, it is not a learning resource you should trust.

How this learning lab works

Each lesson below teaches one skill, explains why it matters, and shows you exactly what to do. The lessons build on each other: strong passwords come first because everything else depends on them, then phishing because it is the most common attack, then two-factor authentication because it protects you when a password fails. Read them in order the first time, then come back to any lesson whenever you need a refresher.

At the end of the lab you will find self-check questions with answers. They are not a test and there is no score — they are there so you can confirm that the ideas have stuck. If you cannot answer one, read the lesson again; the answer is always in the lesson. There is no account, no login and nothing to install. Everything here works with the devices and accounts you already have.

Lesson 1: passwords that actually protect you

A good password is long, unique and unguessable — and the easiest way to get all three is to use a passphrase: three or four ordinary words strung together, with a number or a symbol if the site asks for one. A passphrase like a line from a song you love is far stronger than a short jumble of letters, because length is what makes a password hard to crack, and it is far easier to remember.

The second rule matters even more: never reuse a password. When one website is breached, attackers try the stolen passwords on every other site, and a reused password hands them your accounts one after another. A password manager solves this by remembering a different long password for every site, so you only need to remember one strong master passphrase. Turn on the manager's own protection, keep its master passphrase written down somewhere safe at home, and change any password you know has been reused.

  • Long beats complicated: a passphrase of several words is strong and memorable
  • One password per site — reuse is the fastest way to lose several accounts at once
  • A password manager remembers them all, so you only remember one
  • Change a password at once if you learn a site was breached

Lesson 2: spotting phishing

Phishing is a message that pretends to be from someone you trust — a bank, a delivery company, a colleague — in order to steal your password or your money. The message usually creates urgency: your account will close, a parcel will be returned, a payment failed. Urgency is the trick. Real organisations rarely demand instant action by message, and they never ask for your password by message.

Before you click, check the sender properly. Look at the full address, not just the display name, and hover over any link to see where it really leads — a link that claims to be your bank but points to a strange address is a trap. If a message worries you, do not reply and do not click: open the organisation's site or app yourself, or call the number on your card, and ask. Reporting the message to your email provider helps protect everyone else too.

  • Urgency is the warning sign: slow down when a message rushes you
  • Check the full sender address and the real destination of every link
  • Never enter a password from a link in a message — go to the site yourself
  • When in doubt, ask the organisation directly through a channel you trust

Lesson 3: two-factor authentication

Two-factor authentication, often shortened to 2FA, adds a second check after your password. Even if someone steals your password, they still cannot get in without the second factor — usually a code from an app on your phone, or a tap on a security key. It is the single most effective step you can take after fixing your passwords, and most major services offer it free.

An authenticator app is the best common choice: it generates a new code every thirty seconds, it works offline, and it cannot be intercepted the way a text message can. Text-message codes are better than nothing, but they can be stolen if an attacker tricks your phone company. Whichever method you choose, save the backup codes the service gives you — print them or write them down and keep them somewhere safe at home, because they are your way back in if you lose your phone.

Lesson 4: safe browsing and public Wi-Fi

Safe browsing starts with two habits: keep your browser updated, and pay attention to the address bar. An updated browser blocks known attacks automatically, and the address bar tells you whether you are on the site you think you are — check it before you sign in, especially after following a link. A padlock icon means the connection is encrypted; it does not mean the site is honest, so a padlock on a strange address is still a strange address.

Public Wi-Fi in cafes, airports and hotels is convenient and worth treating with care. Avoid signing in to banking or other sensitive accounts on a network you do not trust, and never enter a password on a site that does not use a secure connection. If you must use public Wi-Fi for sensitive things, a virtual private network from a provider you trust encrypts your traffic — but a trustworthy provider matters more than the technology, so choose one with a clear reputation.

Lesson 5: keeping your devices updated

Updates are not just new features — most of them fix security holes that attackers already know about. Turn on automatic updates for your operating system and your browser, and update your apps when prompted. The few minutes an update takes are far cheaper than recovering from an attack that a patch would have stopped.

Backups are the other half of this lesson. If a device is lost, stolen or locked by malicious software, a recent backup is what turns a disaster into an inconvenience. Keep at least one copy of your important files somewhere other than the device itself — a cloud service you trust or an external drive kept at home — and check sometimes that the backup actually works by opening a file from it.

Self-check: test what you learned

Read each question, answer it in your head, then read the answer. If you get one wrong, go back to the lesson it comes from — the answer is always there.

  • Question: Why is a long passphrase stronger than a short jumble of letters? Answer: because length is what makes a password hard to guess or crack, and a passphrase is long enough to be strong while still easy to remember.
  • Question: A message says your account will close in one hour unless you click a link. What do you do? Answer: slow down, do not click, and go to the organisation's own site or app to check. Urgency is the classic sign of phishing.
  • Question: What does two-factor authentication protect you against? Answer: it protects you when your password is stolen, because the attacker still lacks the second factor — the code from your app or your security key.
  • Question: What should you do with the backup codes a service gives you? Answer: write them down or print them and keep them somewhere safe at home. They are your way back in if you lose your phone.
  • Question: Is a site safe because it shows a padlock icon? Answer: no. The padlock only means the connection is encrypted. A dishonest site can have a padlock too, so always check the address itself.
  • Question: Why do software updates matter for security? Answer: because most updates fix security holes that attackers already know about. An unpatched device is an open door.

Building a personal security routine

Good security is a routine, not a one-time project. Once a month, spend ten minutes on it: check for updates you have postponed, look at the sign-in alerts on your main accounts, and delete accounts you no longer use — every forgotten account is a door you are not watching. Once a year, review your passwords, check that your backups work, and make sure a trusted person could reach your important accounts if you ever could not.

One check worth doing today is to see whether your email address appears in a known data breach. The free service Have I Been Pwned (haveibeenpwned.com) lets you look up an address safely, and the Stay Safe Online site (staysafeonline.org) has plain-language guides for staying safe. If you find a breach, change that password everywhere you reused it — and then stop reusing passwords for good.

What to do if something goes wrong

If you think an account has been breached, act quickly and in order. First, change the password from a device you trust, and turn on two-factor authentication if it was off. Then check what the intruder may have reached: sent messages, changed settings, added forwarding rules or new devices. If money is involved, contact your bank at once and ask what to freeze or reverse.

Then report it. Report phishing messages to your email provider, report fraud to the police or the fraud reporting service in your country, and tell the service whose account was affected — they can check for wider abuse. Finally, learn from it: work out how the attacker got in, fix that gap, and update the lesson in your own routine. Most breaches happen through a reused password or a clicked link, and both are fixable.

Questions people ask

What makes a password strong?

Length matters most: a passphrase of several ordinary words is strong and memorable. It must also be unique — never reuse a password across sites — and a password manager is the easiest way to keep a different long password for every account.

How can I tell if a message is phishing?

Look for urgency, a sender address that does not match the organisation, and links that lead somewhere unexpected. Real organisations rarely demand instant action by message and never ask for your password that way. When in doubt, go to the site yourself instead of clicking.

Is two-factor authentication really worth the trouble?

Yes — it is the most effective step after fixing your passwords. Even if someone steals your password, they still cannot sign in without the second factor from your phone or security key. An authenticator app is the best common choice.

Is public Wi-Fi safe to use?

It is fine for ordinary browsing, but avoid signing in to banking or other sensitive accounts on a network you do not trust. Never enter a password on a site without a secure connection, and consider a virtual private network from a provider you trust for sensitive use.

How do I check if my email address was in a data breach?

The free service Have I Been Pwned lets you look up an address safely and see which breaches it appears in. If you find one, change that password everywhere you reused it, and turn on two-factor authentication.

What should I do first if I think my account was hacked?

Change the password from a device you trust, turn on two-factor authentication, and check what the intruder may have reached. If money is involved, contact your bank at once, and report the incident to the service and to the fraud reporting service in your country.
