What this is

A project handover is the moment a finished build passes from the builder to you. You receive the keys, the documents, the warranties and the manuals, and the builder's main work comes to an end. It sounds like a formality — a handshake, a set of keys, a signature — but it is the most important hour of the whole build.

That is because handover is your last easy chance to find defects. A problem raised at handover gets fixed: the builder is still on site, the trades are still available, and the final payment is still in your hands. The same problem raised a month later gets argued about — was it there at handover, did you cause it, is it covered? The checklist on this page exists to make that hour count.

Handover covers four things: the documents you must receive, the tests you must see, the keys and access details you must collect, and the snag list — the written record of every defect, however small. This page gives you a working checklist for all four, with your progress saved in this browser, and a copy and print button so the finished list lives outside the browser too.

How to use the checklist

Walk through the build with the checklist open on your phone and the builder beside you. Go room by room, in daylight, and tick each item only when you have actually seen it — the document in your hand, the test run in front of you, the key in the lock. A tick means checked, not assumed.

The checklist has twenty-four items in four groups: documents, tests and checks, keys and access, and the snag list. Your progress shows at the top as a count and a percentage, and your ticks are saved in this browser automatically, so you can stop halfway and pick up where you left off on the same device.

When you are done, copy the list or print it. A printed copy with the builder's signature is the best record of what was agreed at handover: what was delivered, what was tested, and what is still on the snag list with a fix date. Keep it with the other build documents.

One rule for the walk-through: take your time. An hour spent checking now saves weeks of chasing later. If the builder rushes you, that is information — a builder confident in their work has no reason to hurry the handover.

The documents to demand

Start with the building completion certificate, which confirms the work was finished to the required standard. Without it, you may have trouble selling, insuring or remortgaging the property later. If it is not ready at handover, get a written date for when it will be.

Next, the warranties and guarantees: for the structure, for the roof, for windows and doors, and for every appliance and system installed. Each one should say what is covered, for how long, and who to contact. A guarantee you cannot find is a guarantee you cannot use.

Ask for the manuals for heating, ventilation, appliances and any smart systems — and for the as-built drawings, which show what was actually built, including where pipes and cables run behind the walls. Future work depends on these drawings more than on any other document.

Test certificates come next: for electrics, gas and plumbing, each signed by the qualified person who did the test. Finally, the final account, showing what was paid, what is still owed, and that the two match the contract. If any document is missing, write it on the snag list with a delivery date — never accept the keys with no paperwork at all.

The tests to see

Paper certificates matter, but your own eyes matter more. Run the heating through a full cycle while you are there: does every radiator warm up, does the hot water run hot, does the thermostat do what it should? A heating system that has never run is a heating system that has never been tested.

Check the plumbing at every joint you can reach. Run every tap, flush every toilet, and look under every sink for the slow drip that only shows when the water has been running a minute. Check water pressure at the highest tap and drainage at the lowest drain — both reveal problems that a quick glance misses.

Open and close every window and door, and lock each one. A door that sticks in dry weather will stick worse when it rains, and a lock that needs a trick to work will fail when you most need it. Check that every window opens fully and that trickle vents move.

Finally, test every smoke and safety alarm, and check that the electrics match the plan: every socket live, every switch working, every light fitting in place. A test you have not seen is a test that did not happen, and handover day is the day to see them.

The snag list

A snag list is the written list of every defect you find at handover, from a scratched worktop to a door that does not close. The word sounds minor, and most snags are minor — but a snag list is a legal record, and it is the document that gets defects fixed after the builder has left.

Each snag needs three things: what is wrong, where it is, and when it will be fixed. 'Scratch on the kitchen worktop, left of the sink, to be repaired by Friday' is a snag. 'Kitchen not perfect' is not — it cannot be fixed because nobody knows what it means. Be specific about every item, however small it feels.

Photograph every defect, in daylight, with something in the frame that shows the scale — a coin, a hand, a tape measure. Photos settle arguments that words cannot, and they protect you if a defect gets worse before it is repaired.

Agree the fix dates in writing, with the builder, before you sign anything. A snag list without dates is a wish list. And keep the final payment until the snags are cleared — once the builder holds all the money, your leverage is gone.

What if something is not finished

It is common for a build to reach handover with a few things unfinished: a door still to be hung, a patch of painting still to be done, a document still to arrive. That is not a reason to refuse the handover — but it is a reason to be careful about how you accept it.

Write every unfinished item on the snag list, with a fix date, and get the builder to sign the list. The signature turns a conversation into a commitment. If the builder will not sign, do not sign either — and do not hand over the final payment.

For anything that affects safety or the use of the building — no heating, no hot water, a missing handrail — the answer is different: do not accept handover until it is fixed, or until a written plan with a date is agreed. Living in a half-finished build while waiting for fixes is miserable, and it weakens your position every week it goes on.

If the builder stops responding after handover, your records are your protection: the signed snag list, the photos, the contract, and the paper trail of messages. Keep them together, keep them dated, and get advice before the trail goes cold.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is accepting the keys with no paperwork. Documents feel like admin, and the keys feel like the real thing — but the keys without the completion certificate, the warranties and the test certificates leave you holding a building you cannot prove is finished.

The second is no photos of the snags. Memory fades and stories change; a photo taken in daylight at handover does not. Photograph every defect, however small, and keep the photos with the snag list.

The third is paying the final instalment before the snag list is cleared. The final payment is your only real leverage after handover, and once it is paid, you are relying on goodwill. Hold it until the list is done.

The fourth is rushing the walk-through. Handover takes an hour at least, in daylight, room by room. A ten-minute tour in the evening finds nothing — which is exactly what a builder in a hurry is counting on.

The fifth is accepting verbal promises. 'I will come back next week' is not a fix date. If it is not written down, with a date and a signature, it does not exist.

This checklist helps you take over a build properly — it is not a professional survey, and it does not replace one. If anything looks structurally wrong, stop and get a professional surveyor before you accept the keys.
