How the timeline works

Enter the fair date and your project type, and the planner counts back from the fair to build your milestones. If you have eight weeks or more, you get a full week-by-week plan with a date for each milestone. If you have less time, the planner gives you the same milestones in order and asks you to work through one or two per week. Either way, the order stays the same, because each step builds on the one before it.

The plan is a guide, not a contract. If your experiment needs more time than the plan allows, move the display work later and protect the experiment time: a strong experiment with a simple display beats a beautiful display with weak results.

Picking a project type

Most fair projects fall into four types. An experiment tests a question with a fair test, changing one thing at a time. An invention or build makes something that solves a problem, and is judged on how well it works. A research study gathers and compares information to answer a question. An observation study watches and records something over time, without changing it.

Pick the type that fits your question, not the one that sounds most impressive. A small, well-run experiment will always beat a grand project that ran out of time. If you are unsure, ask your teacher which type fits the question you want to answer.

The milestones, in order

Every project follows the same rough path. First comes the question: one sentence, clear enough that you could explain it to a friend. Then background research, so you know what is already known. Then the method: what you will do, what you will measure, and what you will keep the same. Then the experiment or build itself, with results recorded as you go, not afterwards from memory.

The last three milestones are where projects are won and lost. Leave real time to analyse your results, because a clear chart and an honest conclusion matter more than extra trials. Leave time to build the display properly, and leave time to practise explaining the project out loud. A two-minute explanation, practised, is worth more than a perfect display that nobody understands.

Gathering materials

List everything you need before you start, and tick items off the materials checklist as you get them. Check the measuring tools early: a missing ruler or timer in the middle of an experiment is a problem you do not need. Keep a notebook for your project log from the first day, and date every entry, because a dated log is the best evidence that you did the work yourself.

Photograph each stage of the work. Photos of your setup, your trials and your results make the display stronger, and they are a backup if something goes wrong with the project itself.

The week of the fair

In the last week, stop experimenting and start finishing. Build the display, print your charts, and practise your explanation until it fits comfortably in two minutes. Pack everything the night before, and keep a copy of your notes and photos somewhere safe, away from the project itself.

On the day, the judges will ask you questions. The best preparation is knowing your own project: why you chose the question, what you did, what you found, and what you would do differently next time. An honest answer about something that went wrong is worth more than a perfect-sounding one.

Questions people ask

How far ahead should I start my project?

Eight weeks is a comfortable amount of time for most projects, and the planner is built around that. If you have less time, the planner compresses the same milestones into the weeks you have, but start as soon as you can.

What if my experiment fails?

A clear result is still a result, even if it is not the one you expected. Write up what happened and why you think it happened, and say what you would change next time. Judges value honest results far more than perfect ones.

Do I need a hypothesis?

For an experiment, yes: a short statement of what you expect and why, written before you start. For an invention, a research study or an observation study, a clear goal or question takes its place.

What should my display include?

Your question, your method, your results with a chart or table, and your conclusion. Keep the text short and the headings clear, and make sure everything on the board can be read from a step or two away.

Can I work with a partner?

That depends on your fair's rules, so check them first. Some fairs allow teams and some do not, and team projects usually need to show what each person did.
