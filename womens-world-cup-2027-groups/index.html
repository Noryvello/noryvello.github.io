The group stage at a glance

The Women's World Cup 2027 begins with a group stage of eight groups, each with four teams. Every team plays the other three teams in its group once, so each team plays three matches. The top two teams in each group reach the round of 16, which means sixteen teams advance and sixteen go home after the group stage.

The group stage runs from the opening match on 24 June, when Brazil play at the Maracana in Rio de Janeiro, through the first weeks of the tournament. With 64 matches in total across the tournament, the group stage accounts for the largest share: 48 matches, six in each group, played across the eight host cities.

For fans, the group stage is the busiest and most varied part of the tournament: several matches most days, teams from every confederation, and new stories with every round of results.

How the groups are formed

The groups are formed by the final draw, scheduled for December 2026. The draw places the thirty-two qualified teams into eight groups of four, following the usual draw rules: teams are seeded and drawn so that the groups are balanced, and teams from the same confederation are kept apart where the rules require it.

The draw is a public event and a major moment in the build-up to the tournament. Once it is made, every team knows its three opponents, its venues, and its route through the group stage. Until then, the groups are simply slots in the schedule framework: Group A through Group H, waiting to be filled.

What the December 2026 draw will decide

The draw decides everything about the group stage: which teams are in each group, who plays whom, and in which order. It also shapes the knockout rounds, because the group a team finishes in decides which part of the knockout bracket it enters. A favourable draw can give a team a kinder route to the final; a hard draw can put the strongest teams on a collision course early.

The draw also decides the stories of the tournament. Rivalries, rematches, and first meetings all come from the draw, and fans will spend the months after it debating which group is the hardest and which teams have the best path. Until the draw, all of that is speculation, and this page will not add to it.

How teams qualify

Thirty-two teams take part in the 2027 World Cup, and qualification runs through the confederations around the world. Most places are decided by regional qualifying competitions, and the process is still running as the draw approaches. Brazil qualify automatically as hosts.

The last places in the tournament come from an inter-confederation play-off in March 2027, after the draw. That means the full list of qualified teams will not be settled until the spring of 2027. Until qualification is complete, any list of the teams in the tournament is incomplete, and any prediction about the groups is premature.

Qualification itself is part of the story. Regional tournaments and qualifying matches give fans a first look at the teams that will shape the World Cup, and surprise qualifiers often become the stories of the tournament. By the time the play-off is done in March 2027, the field of thirty-two will finally be complete.

What decides a group

Within each group, teams are ranked by the points they win: three for a win and one for a draw, the standard convention in football tournaments. The top two teams go through to the round of 16. When teams finish level on points, tiebreakers decide the order, starting with goal difference and goals scored, as is standard in FIFA tournaments.

The final round of group matches is where groups are usually decided. Because the last two matches in each group are played at the same time, teams cannot play for a result based on another match, and the drama runs to the final whistle. A single goal in the last minutes can change who goes through, which is why the group stage produces some of the most memorable moments of any World Cup.

Goal difference often matters as much as points. A team that wins its first two matches can afford to rest players in the last game, while a team that lost its opener may need not just a win but a win by a margin. That is why coaches talk about every goal counting from the very first match, and why the group stage rewards teams that start fast.

Brazil and the host's path

Brazil, as hosts, have the clearest path of any team. They open the tournament at the Maracana in Rio de Janeiro on 24 June, play their second group match at Arena Itaquera in Sao Paulo on 29 June, and finish the group stage at the Estadio Nacional in Brasilia on 4 July. Their opponents in those matches are decided by the draw.

The host nation always carries extra attention, and Brazil will be no exception. Playing at home, in front of home crowds, in stadiums across the country, the hosts begin the tournament with the eyes of the football world on them. Which group they are drawn into, and who they face, will be one of the first things fans look for after the draw.

What to watch for once the groups are drawn

Once the draw is made in December 2026, the groups become the story of the tournament. Look first at the balance of each group: whether one group is much stronger than the others, and whether any group has no clear favourite. Then look at the routes: which group winners face which runners-up in the round of 16, and how the bracket shapes up.

Also watch for the matches that will decide each group. The final round of group matches, played at the same time, usually settles who goes through. And watch for the teams that surprise: every World Cup has a team that nobody expected to go far, and the group stage is where those stories begin.

Finally, remember that the groups are only the beginning. The teams that lift the trophy are not always the ones that top their groups in style: some champions grow into the tournament, winning narrowly in the group stage and finding their best form in the knockouts. The draw sets the stage, but the football decides the rest.
