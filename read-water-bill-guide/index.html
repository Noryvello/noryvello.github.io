How to Read a Water Bill

A water bill is mostly two things: a meter reading turned into a volume, and the charges your supplier adds on top. This guide shows you how to read your own meter, what the units and charges on the bill mean, and how to check for a leak with a simple overnight test. With those three skills, you can check any bill yourself instead of taking it on trust.

A water bill looks complicated the first time you read it, but almost everything on it comes from just two sources: your meter and your supplier's charging rules. The meter measures how much water has passed into your home since the last reading, and the bill turns that into a volume, adds the fixed charges for keeping you connected, and asks you to pay the total. Once you know where each figure comes from, you can check the bill yourself and catch a leak before it turns into a large bill.

This guide explains water bills and meters in general terms. Bills, meters and charging rules differ from one supplier to another, so check with your own supplier for anything specific to your bill, your meter or your tariff.

Finding and reading your water meter

Finding your meter is the first step. In a house it is usually outside, near the boundary of the property, inside a small box set into the ground with a lid you can lift. In a flat it may be in a utility cupboard or a shared meter room. If you cannot find yours, your supplier can tell you where it is.

There are two common kinds of meter. A digital meter shows the reading as a row of numbers, like a car odometer: read it from left to right. An analogue meter has a row of clock-like dials instead; read each dial in turn, noting that neighbouring dials turn in opposite directions. On both kinds, the black digits are the whole units your bill is based on, and the red digits are fractions of a unit.

Always write down the date with the reading: a reading without a date is hard to compare with anything. Reading it on the same day each month gives you a steady record for the first time a bill looks wrong.

What the units on your bill mean

Most water bills measure volume in cubic metres, written as m³. One cubic metre is 1,000 litres. Some bills use litres or gallons instead, so always check the unit printed next to the figures before you do any sums.

Your bill shows two readings: the previous reading and the current reading. Your usage is the difference between them. If the previous reading was 412 and the current reading is 428, you used 16 units — on a bill measured in cubic metres, that is 16 cubic metres, or 16,000 litres, over the billing period.

The billing period matters as much as the volume. A bill covering 60 days will naturally be larger than one covering 30 days, even if you used water at exactly the same rate. If your usage seems high, check the dates on the bill first.

You may also see the word estimated next to a reading. That means the supplier guessed your usage from past bills instead of reading the meter, and the guess is corrected when the next real reading is taken — which is why a bill after an estimate can look surprisingly high or low.

Fixed charges vs usage charges

Most water bills have two kinds of charge. The fixed charge, sometimes called a standing charge, pays for keeping your home connected: the pipes, the meter, the meter readings and the billing itself. You pay it even in a month when you use no water at all, because the connection is still there.

The usage charge is the part that depends on you. It is the volume you used, multiplied by the supplier's rate for each unit. Use more water and this part of the bill goes up; use less and it goes down. This is the part you can control, and it is the part a leak affects.

Some bills also carry a wastewater or sewerage charge, which pays for taking water away after you have used it. It is often linked to the volume you used, on the assumption that most of the water that comes in goes back out again.

Estimating your own water use

You do not need to wait for a bill to know how much water you use. Read your meter once a week for a month and write each reading down. Subtract one reading from the next to get each week's use, then divide by seven for a daily figure. After a few weeks you will know your household's normal range.

Here is a worked example. Suppose your meter reading goes from 412 to 428 over a 30-day billing period. That is 16 cubic metres, or 16,000 litres. Divide 16,000 by 30 days and you get about 533 litres a day for the whole household. Divide again by the number of people at home and you have a rough figure per person per day.

You can also measure a single activity. Read the meter, run one washing machine cycle and nothing else, then read the meter again. The difference is roughly what that cycle used. The same trick works for a bath or a long shower.

A simple leak check anyone can do

The simplest leak check needs nothing but your meter and a quiet night. Last thing at night, read the meter and write the number down. Then use no water at all overnight: no washing machine, no dishwasher, and no toilet flushes if you can avoid them. First thing in the morning, read the meter again. If the number has moved, water went somewhere during the night, and you probably have a leak.

There is a quicker version. Turn off every tap, make sure no appliance is using water, then watch the meter for a few minutes. Most meters have a small dial or a flow indicator that spins when water is passing through. If it keeps moving with everything off, water is escaping somewhere.

The usual hiding places are worth checking first. A toilet cistern that keeps refilling is the most common household leak: listen for a faint hiss or trickle. Dripping taps, a garden hose left running, and damp patches on walls or floors are the other usual suspects. If the meter test points to a leak but you cannot find it, call a plumber — and if the leak is on the supplier's side of the meter, tell the supplier.

When a bill looks wrong

Before you dispute a bill, run through a short list of checks. Is the reading marked as estimated? Is the billing period longer than usual? Was the previous bill an estimate that is now being corrected? Has anything changed at home — more people staying, a new appliance, a garden being watered more often? Any of these can explain a jump without anything being wrong.

If none of them explains it, do the overnight leak check. A leak is the most common cause of a genuinely surprising bill, and your own dated meter readings are the clearest evidence you can show anyone.

When you contact your supplier, a few questions cover most cases. Was this reading actual or estimated? Can the meter be read again to confirm the figure? If the reading was wrong, will the bill be corrected? And if the meter itself seems faulty, how is that checked? Asking these plainly, with your own readings in hand, usually sorts the matter out faster than arguing about the total.

Questions people ask

How do I read my water meter?

Find your meter, usually in a small box near your boundary or in a utility area, and read the black digits from left to right — those are the whole units. The red digits are fractions of a unit, so you can ignore them for a simple check. Write down the reading and the date so you can compare it with your next reading.

What do the units on a water bill mean?

Most bills measure water in cubic metres, and one cubic metre is 1,000 litres. Your usage is the current meter reading minus the previous reading, so if the reading went from 412 to 428, you used 16 cubic metres, which is 16,000 litres. Some bills use litres or gallons instead, so check the unit printed next to the figures.

Why did my water bill jump suddenly?

The most common reasons are a longer billing period than usual, an estimated reading being corrected by a real one, or a leak somewhere in your home. A toilet that keeps running or a dripping tap can also add up quietly over a month. Read your meter, compare it with the bill, and run the overnight leak check described above.

How can I check for a leak at home?

Read your meter last thing at night, then use no water overnight and read it again first thing in the morning. If the reading has moved, water is going somewhere it should not be. You can also turn off every tap and watch the meter's small dial for a few minutes — if it keeps moving, there is a leak.
