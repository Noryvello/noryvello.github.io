Bean and Lentil Cooking Guide

Pick a bean or lentil and this guide gives you its soak method, its cooking time range and a simple doneness test. Twelve types are covered, from quick red lentils to slow-cooking chickpeas, with the soaking and safety notes that matter.

How to use this guide

Choose the bean or lentil you are cooking and press the button. You will see whether it needs soaking, how long it takes to cook on the stove, and how to tell when it is done. Times are ranges, not exact minutes, because older beans take longer than fresh ones and because altitude, water hardness and pot size all make a difference. Start testing at the early end of the range and keep cooking until the doneness test passes.

Always pick through dried beans and lentils before cooking and throw away any stones, shrivelled pieces or broken bits. Rinse them well in a sieve under running water. For beans that need soaking, use plenty of water, because they swell to about twice their dried size, and drain and rinse them before cooking in fresh water.

Soaking: overnight, quick soak, or none

Soaking does two useful things: it shortens the cooking time and it helps the beans cook more evenly. The overnight soak is the simplest method. Cover the beans with plenty of cold water, leave them for 8 to 12 hours, then drain and rinse. If you forget to soak, the quick soak works well: cover the beans with water, bring to a boil for two minutes, take the pot off the heat, cover it and leave it for one hour, then drain and rinse.

Lentils and split peas do not need soaking at all. They are small and thin-skinned, so they cook quickly from dry. Soaking them does little except make them mushy, so a rinse is enough. Chickpeas and kidney beans benefit most from a full overnight soak, because they are large and dense. If you are short on time, the quick soak is a good second choice for them too.

One thing soaking does not do is replace proper cooking. Soaked beans still need to be boiled and simmered until fully tender, and kidney beans in particular must be boiled hard for at least ten minutes, even after soaking. Never rely on soaking alone to make beans safe to eat.

Cooking: water, heat and timing

Use plenty of water, about three times the volume of the dried beans, and start with cold water. Bring the pot to a boil, skim off any foam that rises, then turn the heat down to a gentle simmer and partly cover the pot. A hard rolling boil breaks beans apart and makes the cooking uneven, while a gentle simmer keeps them whole and cooks them evenly.

Do not add acidic ingredients such as tomatoes, vinegar or lemon juice until the beans are nearly done, because acid slows softening and can leave beans firm no matter how long they cook. Salt is different: a little salt early in cooking is fine and seasons the beans all the way through. A pinch of baking soda in the cooking water can speed up softening for very old beans, but use only a small amount, because too much makes the beans mushy and can affect the flavour.

Cooking times vary more than most recipes admit. Beans bought recently cook faster than beans that have sat in a cupboard for a year. Hard water slows cooking, and cooking at high altitude takes longer because water boils at a lower temperature. The ranges in this guide are a starting point, and the doneness test is the real judge.

How to tell when they are done

The doneness test is simple: fish out a few beans or lentils and press them between your fingers or bite one. A cooked bean should crush easily with gentle pressure and feel creamy all the way through, with no hard or chalky centre. Lentils should be tender but still hold their shape, unless you want them to break down into a thick soup or dal, in which case cook them a little longer.

Test a few, not just one, because beans in the same pot do not always cook at exactly the same rate. If some are done and some are still firm, give the pot a few more minutes and test again. If the beans seem to cook forever without softening, they may be very old, or the water may be very hard, or an acidic ingredient may have gone in too early. In that case, keep simmering gently and test often rather than turning up the heat.

Food safety matters with dried beans. Dried kidney beans must be boiled hard for at least ten minutes before simmering, and never cooked only in a slow cooker on a low setting. Never eat undercooked dried beans. Cool cooked beans quickly, keep them in the fridge, and eat them within 3 to 4 days. When in doubt, throw them out.

Storing cooked beans and lentils

Cooked beans and lentils keep in the fridge for 3 to 4 days in a covered container, and they freeze well for longer storage. Freeze them in portions with a little of their cooking liquid, and label the container with the date. To use them, thaw in the fridge overnight or warm them gently from frozen in a covered pan.

Dried beans and lentils keep for a long time in a cool, dry cupboard in a sealed container, but they do get harder with age. Beans that are more than a year old may take much longer to cook, and very old beans may never soften fully. If you find a bag you cannot date, cook a small test batch first before planning a meal around it.

Questions people ask

Do lentils need soaking?

No. Lentils and split peas cook well without soaking, and a short rinse is enough. Dried beans are different: most need an overnight soak or a quick soak before cooking.

What is the quick soak method?

Cover the beans with water, bring to a boil for two minutes, then take the pot off the heat, cover it and leave it for one hour. Drain, rinse and cook in fresh water. It is faster than an overnight soak and works for most beans.

Why must kidney beans be boiled hard?

Raw and undercooked kidney beans contain a natural toxin that is only destroyed by a hard boil of at least ten minutes. Never cook dried kidney beans only in a slow cooker on a low setting, because the temperature stays too low.

Should I add salt while beans cook?

A little salt early in cooking is fine and seasons the beans all the way through. Acidic ingredients such as tomatoes or vinegar are best added near the end, because acid can keep beans firm and slow down softening.

How long do cooked beans keep?

Cooked beans keep in the fridge for 3 to 4 days in a covered container, and they freeze well for longer storage. Cool them quickly after cooking and reheat them until steaming hot all the way through.
