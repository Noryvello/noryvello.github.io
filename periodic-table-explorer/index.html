Reading the table — groups, periods, and numbers

The periodic table is arranged by atomic number: the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom. Hydrogen has 1 proton, so it is number 1. Helium has 2, lithium has 3, and so on all the way to oganesson with 118. The atomic number is the identity of an element — change the number of protons and you change the element itself. The number in each cell is the atomic number, and the letters are the symbol: usually one or two letters, often from the English name, sometimes from an older Latin name.

The columns are called groups, numbered 1 to 18. Elements in the same group behave in similar ways because they have the same number of electrons in their outer shell. Group 1, the alkali metals, are all soft metals that react violently with water. Group 17, the halogens, are all reactive nonmetals. Group 18, the noble gases, barely react with anything at all. If you know the group, you can often predict how an element will behave without ever seeing it.

The rows are called periods, numbered 1 to 7. A period tells you how many electron shells the atom has: elements in period 2 have two shells, elements in period 3 have three, and so on. Moving across a period, the atomic number rises by one at each step, and the character changes gradually from metal on the left to nonmetal on the right. The two rows at the bottom — the lanthanides and the actinides — belong inside periods 6 and 7, but they are shown separately to keep the table from becoming too wide to read.

The elements you meet every day

Oxygen (O, number 8) is the element you use most: about a fifth of the air is oxygen, and your body uses it to release energy from food. It is also what makes things burn — a fire is oxygen joining rapidly with a fuel. Carbon (C, number 6) is the backbone of all living things: your body, your food, wood, plastic and coal are all built on carbon chains. Two of the most famous forms of pure carbon are diamond, the hardest natural material, and graphite, the soft grey stuff in pencils.

Iron (Fe, number 26) is the metal of buildings, bridges, cars and railways — more iron is used than all other metals put together, mostly as steel. Its symbol, Fe, comes from the Latin word ferrum, which is why it does not match the English name. Calcium (Ca, number 20) builds your bones and teeth, and is the main ingredient of cement, chalk and limestone. Milk is famous for calcium because growing bones need a steady supply of it.

Look around any room and you will find a dozen more: aluminium in window frames and cans, copper in wires, silicon in phone screens and computer chips, sodium and chlorine as table salt, nitrogen in fertiliser bags, hydrogen in water, and gold and silver in jewellery. The table is not an abstract poster — it is the inventory of the physical world.

The quiz — how to test yourself

The quiz asks one question at a time: "What is the symbol for this element?" Four symbols appear as big buttons; tap the one you think is right. You get your answer at once — right or wrong, with the correct symbol shown — and then the Next question button takes you to the next one. After ten questions you get your score out of ten and a short message about how you did.

The questions are picked at random from all 118 elements, so every round is different. That means you will sometimes meet an element you have never heard of, like thulium or dubnium. That is fine — the quiz is a learning tool, not an exam, and a wrong answer with the right one shown straight after is one of the fastest ways to learn. If you want a gentler start, spend a few minutes tapping through the table first, then try the quiz on the first 20 elements by learning them from the plan in the next section.

A score of 10 out of 10 on a random draw is genuinely hard, because the heavy elements at the bottom of the table are rarely taught at school. Do not be discouraged by a low first score. Play again: the symbols you missed will start to stick, and within a few rounds you will find the common ones — H, O, C, N, Fe, Au, Ag, Na, Cl — coming to mind without thinking.

Learning the first 20 elements in a week

You do not need to memorise all 118 elements at once. School chemistry mostly uses the first 20, and a week is enough to learn them well. Day 1: learn hydrogen, helium, lithium, beryllium and boron — numbers 1 to 5. Say each name and symbol out loud, and write them down once. Day 2: add carbon, nitrogen, oxygen, fluorine and neon — numbers 6 to 10. Test yourself on all ten before you sleep.

Day 3: add sodium, magnesium, aluminium, silicon and phosphorus — numbers 11 to 15. Note the odd symbols: Na for sodium (from the Latin natrium) and the "ph" in phosphorus. Day 4: add sulfur, chlorine, argon, potassium and calcium — numbers 16 to 20. Potassium's symbol is K (from kalium), another Latin leftover. Day 5: test yourself on all 20 with the quiz and with a blank piece of paper. Day 6: learn the ones you missed, and learn one use for each element — a use is a hook that holds the symbol in memory. Day 7: test again. If you score 18 or better, you know the first 20.

The trick that makes it work is testing, not re-reading. Reading the list ten times feels productive and teaches almost nothing. Covering the symbols and trying to recall them feels harder and teaches far more — every failed recall, corrected at once, strengthens the memory. The quiz on this page does exactly that: it asks, you answer, it corrects. Use it little and often rather than in one long session.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is memorising without understanding: knowing that Fe is iron without knowing that iron sits in group 8, in period 4, and is a transition metal. The table's shape is the memory aid — group tells you the behaviour, period tells you the shells, and the position tells you the neighbours. Learn the shape and the symbols come with it.

The second mistake is confusing a symbol with a name. Symbols are case-sensitive: Co is cobalt, a metal, while CO is carbon monoxide, a poisonous gas made of carbon and oxygen. One capital letter changes everything. The same trap catches "SI" (not an element — silicon is Si) and "FE" (not an element — iron is Fe). Always write symbols with exactly one capital letter, followed by a small letter if there is a second one.

The third mistake is assuming the symbol always matches the English name. Most do — C for carbon, O for oxygen — but the famous exceptions come from Latin: Na for sodium, K for potassium, Fe for iron, Cu for copper, Ag for silver, Sn for tin, Sb for antimony, Au for gold, Hg for mercury, Pb for lead. These eleven are worth learning as a set, because they appear in almost every exam. The fourth mistake is treating the atomic number as a mass: the number counts protons, not weight, and the mass of an atom is roughly twice its atomic number only for light elements.

It does not show electron arrangements, atomic masses, melting points or how elements react with each other; those need a textbook and a teacher. Questions people ask

How many elements are there?

118 elements are known today, from hydrogen (1) to oganesson (118). The first 94 occur in nature; the rest were made in laboratories. The last four were officially named in 2016.

What is an atomic number?

The atomic number is the number of protons in the nucleus of an atom. It is the identity of an element: hydrogen has 1 proton, carbon has 6, and gold has 79. Change the number of protons and you change the element.

What is the difference between a group and a period?

A group is a column of the table (1 to 18); elements in a group behave similarly because they have the same number of outer electrons. A period is a row (1 to 7); it tells you how many electron shells the atom has.

Which element is the lightest?

Hydrogen, number 1. A hydrogen atom has one proton and one electron, and it is the lightest of all elements. It is also the most common element in the universe.

How can I remember the symbols?

Learn them in small groups, test yourself instead of re-reading, and learn one use for each element as a memory hook. The quiz on this page asks, corrects and repeats — a few short rounds beat one long session.

Can I test myself here?

Yes. Press Start the quiz below the table: ten questions, each asking for the symbol of a named element, with four answers to choose from and your score at the end. Every round picks different elements.
