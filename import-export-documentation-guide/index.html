Import Export Documentation Guide

Importing or exporting goods needs a small set of trade documents, and the exact set depends on what you ship and where it goes. This guide explains each document, what it must say, and where to check the rules before you send anything.

Trade rules differ by country and change often — this guide is general information only, not legal or customs advice. Always check the official customs guidance for the countries involved before you ship, and confirm any document requirement with your freight forwarder or customs broker.

What trade documents actually do

Every shipment that crosses a border travels with paperwork, and that paperwork does three jobs at once. It tells customs officers what is inside the boxes, it proves who owns the goods and what they are worth, and it shows where the goods came from. Without clear documents, a shipment can sit in a warehouse for days while officers ask questions that the paperwork should already have answered.

Think of the documents as the shipment speaking for itself. The people handling your goods will never open every box, so they trust the papers. That trust is why accuracy matters so much: a wrong weight, a vague description or a missing signature can turn a routine delivery into a delay, a fine or a refused shipment. The good news is that the core set of documents is small, and once you understand each one, preparing them becomes a routine.

  • A clear description of the goods, in plain words, not codes alone
  • The quantity, weight and value of the shipment
  • The seller, the buyer and the country of origin
  • The transport details: how the goods travel and who carries them

The commercial invoice

The commercial invoice is the most important trade document. It is the seller's bill to the buyer, and customs uses it to work out duty and to check that the shipment matches the sale. A good invoice leaves nothing to guess: it names the seller and the buyer with full addresses, describes each item, states the quantity and the unit price, and shows the total value and the currency spelled out in words.

It should also state the terms of sale — who pays for transport and insurance, and where responsibility passes from seller to buyer — and the country where the goods were made or grown. Be honest about value: understating it to save duty is fraud in most countries and can lead to penalties far larger than the duty saved. Keep the invoice consistent with every other document; if the invoice says one weight and the packing list says another, customs will stop and ask questions.

  • Seller and buyer names and full addresses
  • Item descriptions, quantities, unit prices and total value
  • Currency spelled out, terms of sale and country of origin
  • Invoice number, date and a signature where required

The packing list

The packing list describes the physical shipment: how many packages there are, what is in each one, how much each weighs and how big it is. Customs officers use it to check the shipment without opening everything, and the warehouse at the other end uses it to confirm that nothing is missing. It looks similar to the invoice, but it answers a different question — not what the goods are worth, but what the boxes actually hold.

A careful packing list marks each package with a number and lists its contents, net weight, gross weight and dimensions. If a shipment has ten cartons and only nine arrive, the packing list is what proves the tenth existed. Write weights that you have actually measured rather than guessed, and make sure the totals match the invoice and the transport document. Small mismatches are one of the most common reasons shipments are held for inspection.

The certificate of origin

A certificate of origin proves where goods were made, grown or substantially changed. Some destinations ask for it before goods may enter, and trade agreements often use it to decide whether a lower duty rate applies. Not every shipment needs one, so check the destination country's requirements before you pay for a certificate you may not need.

These certificates are usually issued by a chamber of commerce or another authorised body in the exporting country, and the exact process differs by country. There are two broad kinds: preferential certificates, used to claim a lower duty under a trade agreement, and non-preferential certificates, which simply state origin. Either way, the origin you claim must be true — goods that were only repacked in a country do not usually count as originating there, and the rules for what counts as a substantial change are set by the destination country.

Transport documents

The transport document is the carrier's receipt and, in many cases, the title to the goods. For sea freight it is called a bill of lading; for air freight it is an air waybill; for road freight within Europe it is usually a CMR consignment note. Each one records what the carrier received, where it is going and who should receive it, and each one is issued by the carrier or the freight forwarder who arranged the transport.

Some transport documents are negotiable, which means the original paper itself proves ownership — lose it and the goods can be very hard to collect. Others are simple receipts. Ask your forwarder which kind you have and how many originals exist. Keep a copy of every transport document with your invoice and packing list, and check that the names, weights and package counts match across all three before the shipment leaves.

Licences, permits and special goods

Ordinary goods usually need only the documents above, but some goods need extra permission before they may cross a border. Food, plants, animals, medicines, chemicals and some kinds of technology often need licences, health certificates or safety approvals, and the requirements depend entirely on the goods and the countries involved. Never assume a product that sells freely at home can be shipped freely abroad.

The safe approach is to ask early: check with the destination country's customs guidance, ask your freight forwarder whether the goods are controlled, and leave enough time to apply for any permit. Permits can take weeks, and a shipment that arrives without one may be refused entry, destroyed or sent back at your expense. If your goods fall into a special category, treat the permit as part of the product, not as an afterthought.

How the rules differ by country

There is no single worldwide rulebook for trade documents. Every country sets its own import rules, its own duty rates and its own list of goods that need special permission, and trade agreements between countries can change the picture further. A document set that works perfectly for one destination may be incomplete for another, and rules that were true last year may have changed since.

That is why verification matters more than memory. The World Trade Organization site (wto.org) explains how the world trading system works and where to find member countries' rules, and the United States International Trade Administration site (trade.gov) publishes practical export guidance. Use them as starting points, then confirm the details with the official customs guidance of the countries involved or with a licensed customs broker.

Language matters too. Some countries accept documents in English, others require a translation, and a few require documents to be legalised or stamped by an embassy. Ask about language and legalisation at the same time as you ask about licences, because these steps also take time. When in doubt, the freight forwarder who handles your route every week is often the fastest source of practical answers.

A step-by-step routine for your first shipment

If you have never shipped across a border, follow a routine and keep a copy of everything. Start by identifying exactly what you are sending and confirming the destination country's rules for those goods. Then prepare the commercial invoice and the packing list from the real measurements, arrange the transport document with your carrier, and check whether you need a certificate of origin or any licence.

  • Identify the goods and confirm the destination rules before you pack
  • Prepare the invoice and packing list from measured weights and counts
  • Arrange the transport document and confirm who holds the originals
  • Apply for any certificate of origin or licence with time to spare
  • Check every document against the others, then keep copies of the full set
  • Track the shipment and answer customs queries quickly if they come

After the shipment arrives, file the whole document set together and note anything that caused a question or a delay. That note becomes your checklist for the next shipment, and after two or three trips the routine will feel straightforward. Most experienced exporters keep a simple folder for each destination with the document list that worked last time.

Mistakes that delay shipments

The most expensive mistakes in trade paperwork are the ordinary ones. A vague description such as parts instead of the real name of the item invites inspection. A value that does not match the payment invites suspicion. Weights copied from a previous shipment instead of measured invite a mismatch that customs will notice. None of these is dramatic, and all of them are easy to avoid with a slow final check.

Other common problems are unsigned documents, missing origin proof and the assumption that last year's rules still apply. Build a habit of checking every document against the others before anything leaves your premises: same names, same counts, same weights, same values. Five quiet minutes of checking at your desk can save five days of storage charges at a port.

  • Vague descriptions instead of plain, specific item names
  • Values, weights or counts that differ between documents
  • Missing signatures or missing origin certificates
  • Assuming the rules have not changed since your last shipment

Questions people ask

What documents do I need to export goods for the first time?

Start with a commercial invoice, a packing list and a transport document from your carrier. Whether you also need a certificate of origin or a licence depends on the goods and the destination, so check the official customs guidance for the countries involved before you ship.

What is the difference between a commercial invoice and a packing list?

The commercial invoice is the bill for the sale: it states what the goods are worth, who bought them and on what terms. The packing list describes the physical shipment: how many packages there are, what is in each one and how much each weighs.

Do I always need a certificate of origin?

No. A certificate of origin is only needed when the destination country or a trade agreement asks for it. Check the requirements for your destination before you pay for one, and make sure the origin you claim is genuinely where the goods were made or grown.

Who issues a certificate of origin?

Certificates are usually issued by a chamber of commerce or another authorised body in the exporting country, and the exact process differs by country. Your freight forwarder or local chamber can tell you where to apply and how long it takes.

What happens if a document is wrong?

The shipment can be delayed for inspection, held in storage at your expense, or refused entry. Fix the error quickly, send the corrected document, and keep copies of everything so you can show what was corrected and when.

Where can I check the import rules for a country?

The World Trade Organization site and the International Trade Administration site are good starting points for understanding the system and finding guidance. Always confirm the details with the official customs guidance of the countries involved, or with a licensed customs broker.
