Cricket Rules for Beginners Guide

Cricket is a bat-and-ball game where one team scores runs while the other tries to get its batters out. Once you know what an innings, an over and a wicket are, the rest of the game starts to make sense.

Cricket has a reputation for being complicated, but its heart is simple: one team bats and scores runs, the other team bowls and fields and tries to get the batters out, and the team with more runs wins. Everything else is detail, and most of that detail follows naturally once you know a handful of words.

This guide explains innings, overs, wickets and runs in plain words, walks through the common ways a batter can get out, and describes the players and the field. Use the term picker to look up any word you hear during a match.

This guide explains the basic rules shared by all forms of cricket. Match lengths and fielding rules vary between formats, so the details depend on the game you are watching.

The aim of the game

Cricket is played between two teams of eleven players. One team bats and tries to score as many runs as possible, while the other team bowls and fields and tries to stop them — by getting the batters out and by limiting the runs. After the first team has finished batting, the teams swap, and the second team tries to beat the first team's total.

The game is played on an oval field with a rectangular strip in the middle called the pitch. At each end of the pitch stands a wicket: three wooden stumps with two small bails balanced on top. Two batters are on the field at once, one at each end, and they score runs by running between the wickets after hitting the ball — or by hitting it to the boundary.

A match can last a few hours or several days, depending on the format, but the aim is always the same: score more runs than the other team. If the second team passes the first team's total, it wins; if it is bowled out before reaching the total, it loses; and if time runs out first, the match is a draw.

Innings and overs

An innings is a team's turn to bat. In most matches each team has one innings, and the innings ends when ten of the eleven batters are out — the last batter cannot bat alone — or when the team has used up its overs. The team that bats first sets a target, and the team that bats second chases it.

An over is a set of six legal balls bowled by one bowler from one end of the pitch. After each over, a different bowler bowls the next over from the other end, so bowlers take turns and batters face a fresh challenge every six balls. A wide ball or a no-ball does not count towards the six, so an over can sometimes stretch to seven or eight balls.

Formats are often named by their overs. A twenty-over match gives each team a maximum of twenty overs to bat, which keeps the whole game to a few hours. A fifty-over match gives each team fifty overs. Longer matches have no over limit at all and are played over several days, with each team batting twice.

Runs and wickets

Runs are the points of cricket, and they are scored in a few ways. The most common is for the two batters to run between the wickets after the ball is hit — each completed swap is one run, and the batters keep running until they decide to stop. If the ball reaches the boundary rope after bouncing, it scores four runs automatically. If it clears the boundary without bouncing, it scores six.

Extra runs are added for bowling errors: a wide ball (too far from the batter to hit) and a no-ball (an illegal delivery, usually for overstepping the line) each add a run to the batting team's total, and the ball must be bowled again. The batting team's total is always shown as runs for wickets — "150 for 4" means 150 runs scored and four batters out.

A wicket is both the stumps and, in everyday cricket language, a batter getting out. When a bowler takes a wicket, a batter is out and the next batter comes in. Ten wickets end an innings, because the last batter has no partner left to run with.

Common dismissals

There are several ways for a batter to get out, and the common ones are worth knowing. Bowled: the bowler's ball hits the stumps and knocks the bails off. Caught: a fielder catches the ball after it is hit and before it bounces. LBW (leg before wicket): the ball hits the batter's body in front of the stumps when it would otherwise have hit them.

Run out: a fielder breaks the stumps with the ball while a batter is out of their ground during a run. Stumped: the batter steps forward out of their ground, misses the ball, and the wicketkeeper breaks the stumps before they get back. Hit wicket: the batter knocks the bails off their own stumps with bat or body. Of these, bowled, caught and LBW are the most common, and the term picker below explains each one in plain words.

When a batter is out, they leave the field and the next batter in the order comes in. The batting order matters: the best batters usually bat near the top, and the bowlers, who are often weaker batters, bat near the bottom. A team that has lost few wickets with plenty of overs left is in a strong position.

The players and the field

Each team has eleven players, and each player has a role. The bowler delivers the ball, the wicketkeeper stands behind the stumps to catch balls the batter misses, and the other nine players are fielders, spread around the field to stop runs and take catches. The captain decides where the fielders stand, and the field changes with every bowler and every batter.

Fielders have names for their positions — slip, gully, mid-off, fine leg — that sound like a secret language at first, but they simply describe where on the field a player is standing. You do not need to learn them to enjoy a match; the important thing is that the fielding team is trying to stop runs and take wickets, while the batting team is trying to score.

Two umpires stand on the field and make the decisions: whether a batter is out, whether a ball is wide or a no-ball, and how many runs were scored. A third umpire, watching replays off the field, helps with close decisions in major matches. The umpire's word is final, and players are expected to accept it without argument.

Questions people ask

How many players are in a cricket team?

Each team has eleven players on the field. Two batters are at the crease at once, one bowler bowls, one player keeps wicket behind the stumps, and the other nine field.

What is an over?

An over is a set of six legal balls bowled by one bowler from one end of the pitch. After each over, a different bowler bowls from the other end.

How does a batter get out?

The common ways are bowled, caught, LBW, run out, stumped and hit wicket. When ten batters are out, the innings ends, because the last batter has no partner.

What is LBW?

LBW stands for leg before wicket. A batter is out LBW when the ball hits their body in front of the stumps and the umpire judges it would have gone on to hit the stumps.

Why does a cricket match last so long?

It depends on the format. A twenty-over match lasts a few hours, a fifty-over match lasts most of a day, and the longest matches are played over several days with each team batting twice.
