Leap Year Explained

2027 is not a leap year: it has 365 days, and February 2027 has 28 days, running from Monday 1 February to Sunday 28 February. The next leap year is 2028, when 29 February falls on a Tuesday.

The leap year rule in three steps

The Gregorian calendar — the calendar used for everyday civil dates in most of the world — follows a simple three-step rule for deciding whether a year is a leap year. A leap year has 366 days instead of 365, with the extra day added as 29 February. Here is the rule, in order:

  1. Step 1: A year divisible by 4 is a leap year. So 2024, 2028, and 2032 are leap years, because each divides evenly by 4.
  2. Step 2: EXCEPT century years — years divisible by 100 — which are not leap years. So 1900 and 2100 are not leap years, even though both are divisible by 4.
  3. Step 3: UNLESS the century year is also divisible by 400, in which case it IS a leap year. So 2000 was a leap year, and 2400 will be one, because both divide evenly by 400.

The order matters. You check the steps in sequence: first divide by 4, then check whether it is a century year, then check whether that century year divides by 400. For most years, only step 1 is needed — the century rules only come into play once every hundred years. But when they do, they are the reason the calendar stays accurate over the centuries rather than slowly drifting out of step with the seasons.

A handy way to remember it: "four, except a hundred, unless four hundred." If a year divides by 4, it is a leap year, unless it ends in two zeroes — and if it ends in two zeroes, it is only a leap year when the first part of the number divides by 4. That is the whole rule. There is nothing else to check.

Worked examples

Here are five years worked through the rule, one by one. Each example has been checked by hand.

YearLeap year?Why
2024YesDivisible by 4 (2024 ÷ 4 = 506), and not a century year.
2027NoNot divisible by 4 (2027 ÷ 4 = 506 remainder 3).
2028YesDivisible by 4 (2028 ÷ 4 = 507), and not a century year.
1900NoDivisible by 100, but not by 400 (1900 ÷ 400 = 4 remainder 300), so the century exception applies.
2000YesDivisible by 400 (2000 ÷ 400 = 5), so the exception to the exception applies.

Take 2027 as the first check. To test whether a year is divisible by 4, you only need to look at its last two digits: if the last two digits form a number divisible by 4, the whole year is divisible by 4. The last two digits of 2027 are 27, and 27 divided by 4 leaves a remainder of 3, so 2027 fails at step 1. It is not a leap year, and no further steps are needed.

Now compare 1900 and 2000, the two century years in the table. Both are divisible by 4, so both would be leap years under step 1 alone. Step 2 knocks them both out, because both are divisible by 100. Then step 3 saves 2000 — it divides evenly by 400 — while 1900 stays out, because 1900 divided by 400 leaves a remainder of 300. This is the most common point of confusion about leap years, and it is why the rule needs three steps rather than one.

Finally, 2028: the last two digits are 28, which divides evenly by 4, and 2028 is not a century year, so it passes step 1 and there is nothing more to check. It is a leap year, and 29 February 2028 falls on a Tuesday.

Why leap years exist

The reason for leap years is that Earth takes about 365.25 days to complete one orbit around the Sun. A calendar year of exactly 365 days is therefore about a quarter of a day shorter than the solar year. That missing quarter-day is small in any single year, but it adds up: after four years, the calendar would be about one whole day behind the seasons, and after a hundred years it would be about 25 days behind.

Adding one day every four years — 29 February — keeps the calendar aligned with the seasons. The extra day makes up for the quarter-days that were left out of the three ordinary years before it. Without leap years, the calendar would drift by about a quarter-day every year, and over time the months would slowly slide through the seasons. Dates that now fall in spring would eventually fall in summer, then autumn, then winter, and back again. The leap day is what holds the calendar still against the turning of the year.

The century rules exist because 365.25 days is an approximation: the solar year is slightly shorter than that, so adding a leap day every four years without exception would eventually overcorrect. Skipping the leap day in most century years, and keeping it only in century years divisible by 400, trims the correction just enough. That is why 1900 was skipped and 2000 was kept: the rule is tuned to match the real length of the year as closely as a simple calendar rule can.

Leap years from 2024 to 2040

Between 2024 and 2040 there are five leap years:

  • 2024
  • 2028
  • 2032
  • 2036
  • 2040

They come every four years, as expected, because no century year falls inside this range — the century rules only matter in years like 1900 and 2000, and the next one, 2100, is well beyond this list. So for planning purposes between now and 2040, the simple version of the rule is all you need: a year divisible by 4 is a leap year.

2027 calendar facts

Since 2027 is not a leap year, it has 365 days. The year starts on Friday 1 January and ends on Friday 31 December. That is 52 weeks plus 1 day — 52 weeks make 364 days, and the extra day is why the year both starts and ends on a Friday. February 2027 has 28 days, running from Monday 1 February to Sunday 28 February, so there is no 29 February in 2027. Anyone planning a calendar, a schedule, or a date calculation for 2027 should use 365 days and a 28-day February.

29 February birthdays

People born on 29 February — leap day — only have a birthday on the calendar once every four years. In non-leap years, such as 2027, they customarily celebrate on either 28 February or 1 March. There is no single rule for which day to choose; it is a matter of personal or family preference, and both are widely used. Some people keep 28 February because it is the last day of February, the month they were born in. Others keep 1 March because it is the day after 28 February, the point in the year where their birthday would fall. Either way, the birthday itself is still 29 February, and the next time it appears on the calendar is Tuesday 29 February 2028. Questions people ask

Is 2027 a leap year?

No. 2027 is not a leap year because it is not divisible by 4. It has 365 days, and February 2027 has 28 days, running from Monday 1 February to Sunday 28 February.

What is the leap year rule?

The Gregorian rule has three steps: a year divisible by 4 is a leap year, except century years divisible by 100, which are not leap years, unless the century year is also divisible by 400, in which case it is a leap year.

Why was 1900 not a leap year but 2000 was?

1900 is divisible by 100 but not by 400, so the century exception applies and it was not a leap year. 2000 is divisible by 400, so the exception to the exception applies and it was a leap year.

When is the next leap year?

The next leap year is 2028, when 29 February falls on a Tuesday. The leap years from 2024 to 2040 are 2024, 2028, 2032, 2036, and 2040.

How many days are in 2027?

2027 has 365 days. It starts on Friday 1 January and ends on Friday 31 December, which is 52 weeks plus 1 day. February 2027 has 28 days.
