Northern Lights Explained

The northern lights are made when charged particles streaming from the Sun collide with gases high in Earth's atmosphere and make them glow. Earth's magnetic field steers those particles towards the poles, which is why auroras appear mostly in the far north and far south.

On a dark night in the far north, the sky can come alive with curtains of green light that ripple and fold like fabric in the wind. The display is called the aurora borealis, or the northern lights, and it is one of the most striking sights in nature. It is also a direct glimpse of the Sun's power reaching across space to touch our atmosphere.

Auroras are not weather and they are not magic. They are light made by collisions high above the Earth, and they follow rules that are simple to understand: particles from the Sun, a magnetic field that steers them, and gases that glow when they are struck.

The solar wind

The Sun does not only shine. It also streams a constant flow of charged particles into space, called the solar wind. Most of the time that wind is a gentle breeze, but the Sun also throws off bursts of particles in solar storms, and those bursts carry far more energy.

When the solar wind reaches Earth, it meets our planet's magnetic field, which acts like a shield. The field deflects most of the particles around the Earth, but some are funnelled along the field lines down towards the magnetic poles, where the field lines dive into the atmosphere.

That funnelling is why auroras gather around the poles. The particles are steered to the same regions again and again, forming a ring of aurora activity around each magnetic pole, called the auroral oval. When solar activity is high, the oval widens, and auroras can appear at lower latitudes than usual.

Why the sky glows

High in the atmosphere, well above where aeroplanes fly, the incoming particles collide with gas molecules and pass energy to them. The gases release that energy as light, the way a glow stick glows when its chemicals mix. The colour depends on which gas is struck and how high up the collision happens.

Green is the most common aurora colour. It comes from oxygen, and it is the colour of most displays. Red also comes from oxygen, but from collisions higher up, and it often appears as a red fringe along the top of a green curtain. Blue and purple come from nitrogen, and they often appear as edges and folds in a strong display.

The shapes change constantly because the stream of particles changes and the magnetic field shifts. Curtains ripple, rays shoot upwards, and whole arcs can brighten and fade in minutes. No two displays are ever quite the same, which is part of why people travel so far to see them.

Where auroras appear

The northern lights, the aurora borealis, appear in a ring around the north magnetic pole, over the far north of Canada, Alaska, Greenland, Iceland, Norway, Sweden, Finland and Russia. The southern lights, the aurora australis, appear in a matching ring around the south magnetic pole, mostly over Antarctica and the southern ocean, and sometimes over the far south of Tasmania, New Zealand and South America.

The two displays often happen at the same time, because the same solar wind feeds both poles. An observer in the far north and an observer in the far south can be watching the same storm from opposite ends of the Earth.

Strong solar storms can push the auroral oval far from the poles. During the strongest storms of recent centuries, auroras have been seen from places far from the polar regions, which is why aurora watchers pay attention to solar activity as well as to the weather.

What affects whether you can see them

An aurora can be happening overhead and still be invisible if the conditions are wrong. Darkness matters most: auroras happen all year round, but in summer at high latitudes the sky never gets properly dark, so the lights are hidden by daylight. The dark winter months are the best season at high latitudes, not because auroras are stronger then, but because the nights are long and dark.

Clear skies matter too, because clouds hide the display. Light pollution matters as well: town and city lights wash out faint auroras, so the best viewing is away from built-up areas, under a dark sky. A bright Moon can also wash out a faint display, though a strong aurora will shine through moonlight.

Solar activity is the last piece. The Sun goes through an activity cycle of roughly eleven years, with more sunspots and more solar storms near the peak of the cycle. Aurora watchers follow that cycle, because the years near the peak bring more frequent and more vivid displays.

Watching the lights

Seeing an aurora takes patience. Even in the best places, the lights may not appear on a given night, and a display can fade as quickly as it starts. Dress warmly, find a dark spot with a clear view towards the pole, and give your eyes time to adjust to the dark, which takes around twenty minutes.

Faint auroras can look like pale grey clouds at first, because our eyes do not see colour well in dim light. A camera will often pick up green that the eye misses, which is why photographs of auroras usually look more colourful than the display looked in person. A strong display needs no camera at all: the green curtains are unmistakable.

Auroras are harmless to watch. The collisions happen far above us, and the light is simply light. The same solar storms that make the brightest auroras can disturb radio signals and power grids, which is a matter for engineers, not for anyone standing outside looking up.

This guide carries no forecasts or live data. The checklist is general guidance about viewing conditions, not a prediction. Auroras cannot be guaranteed on any given night, and solar activity changes from day to day.

Questions people ask

What causes the northern lights?

Charged particles streaming from the Sun, called the solar wind, collide with gases high in Earth's atmosphere and make them glow. Earth's magnetic field steers the particles towards the poles, which is why auroras appear mostly in the far north and far south.

Why are auroras usually green?

Green is the most common aurora colour and it comes from oxygen. Red also comes from oxygen, at higher altitudes, while blue and purple come from nitrogen.

Do auroras happen in the southern hemisphere too?

Yes. The southern lights, the aurora australis, appear in a ring around the south magnetic pole, and the two displays often happen at the same time because the same solar wind feeds both poles.

Why are auroras best seen in winter?

Auroras happen all year round, but in summer at high latitudes the sky never gets properly dark, so the lights are hidden by daylight. The long dark nights of winter make them visible.

Can auroras be seen from lower latitudes?

Sometimes, during strong solar storms, the auroral oval widens and auroras can appear far from the poles. Such displays are rare and depend on solar activity.
