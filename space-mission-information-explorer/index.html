Space Mission Information Explorer

A space mission is a planned journey beyond Earth, carried out by a spacecraft with a crew or with robots, to explore, study or test something in space. This guide explains the famous missions in plain words, from the first Moon landing to the farthest spacecraft ever built. It also shows how missions are planned and how you can follow a live mission through the official sources.

How to use this guide

This guide is for anyone who has ever looked up at the night sky and wondered what is out there, and what humans have already sent into it. You do not need a science background to read it, and you do not need to know any mathematics. Each section explains one idea in plain words, starting with the missions themselves, then moving on to how they are planned, and finally to how you can follow a mission that is flying right now.

Read the sections in order if you are new to the subject, because each one builds on the last. If you already know the famous missions, skip straight to the section about following a live mission, which explains where the official information lives and how to read it. The questions at the end answer the things people ask most often, and the honest limits box near the end says plainly what this page cannot do.

One thing to know before you start: this page teaches you about missions, but it does not track them live. A static guide page cannot show where a spacecraft is right now, and any page that claims to do so without an official feed is guessing. Whenever you want live information, this guide points you to the official source, which is the space agency that runs the mission.

The missions that changed everything

A small number of missions changed what humanity believed was possible. The first satellite, Sputnik 1, launched in 1957, proved that a human-made object could orbit the Earth, and it started the space age in a single evening. Four years later, Yuri Gagarin became the first person in space, completing one orbit of the Earth in 1961 and coming home safely.

The Apollo programme took the next great step: landing people on the Moon. Between 1969 and 1972, six Apollo missions landed astronauts on the lunar surface, and twelve people walked there in total. No one has walked on the Moon since Apollo 17 in December 1972, which is why those landings still feel so remarkable more than fifty years later.

Robotic missions have gone much further than any crew. Voyager 1, launched in 1977, has travelled beyond the edge of the Sun's influence and is now in interstellar space, still sending back faint signals. Mars rovers have driven across another planet, and telescopes in space have shown us galaxies billions of light years away. Each of these missions answered questions that no one could answer before it flew.

Apollo 11: the first Moon landing

Apollo 11 launched in July 1969 with three astronauts: Neil Armstrong, Buzz Aldrin and Michael Collins. The journey to the Moon took about four days. Once in lunar orbit, Armstrong and Aldrin climbed into a small landing craft called the Eagle and descended to the surface, while Collins stayed behind in the command module, orbiting the Moon alone.

On 20 July 1969, Armstrong stepped onto the lunar surface, becoming the first person to walk on another world. Aldrin joined him a short time later. The two astronauts spent a few hours outside, collecting rock samples, taking photographs and setting up simple experiments, while an estimated 600 million people watched on television around the world.

The crew returned to Earth safely, splashing down in the Pacific Ocean. Apollo 11 mattered for more than the landing itself: it proved that humans could travel to another world and come home, and the technology developed for Apollo, from computers to materials, went on to shape everyday life. The rock samples the astronauts brought back are still studied by scientists today.

Voyager 1: the farthest traveller

Voyager 1 launched in 1977, in the same year as its twin, Voyager 2. The two spacecraft were built to take advantage of a rare alignment of the outer planets, which let them visit Jupiter and Saturn and use each planet's gravity to speed on to the next. Voyager 1 flew past Jupiter in 1979 and Saturn in 1980, sending back the first close-up pictures of those giant worlds and their moons.

After Saturn, Voyager 1 kept going, heading out of the Solar System. In 2012 it crossed into interstellar space, the region between the stars, becoming the first human-made object to do so. It is now more than 15 billion miles from Earth, so far away that its radio signals take over twenty hours to reach us, travelling at the speed of light.

Voyager 1 still talks to Earth, faintly, using a nuclear power source that has lasted far longer than anyone expected. It carries a golden record with greetings in many languages, music and sounds of Earth, made in case it is ever found by someone else. The spacecraft will fall silent one day when its power finally runs out, but it will keep drifting through the space between the stars for a very long time.

Mars rovers: robots on another world

Mars rovers are robotic vehicles that land on Mars and drive across its surface, doing the work of a geologist without a human aboard. The first, Sojourner, arrived in 1997 and was about the size of a microwave oven. Two larger rovers, Spirit and Opportunity, landed in 2004; Opportunity kept working for nearly fifteen years, far beyond its planned ninety days.

The rovers that followed were bigger and more capable. Curiosity, which landed in 2012, is the size of a small car and runs on nuclear power, which lets it work through the cold Martian nights. Perseverance landed in 2021 in an ancient river delta called Jezero Crater, and it carried a small helicopter, Ingenuity, which proved that powered flight is possible in the thin air of Mars.

Rovers work slowly, by Earth standards. A signal takes between about three and twenty minutes to travel between Earth and Mars, depending on where the planets are, so nobody can drive a rover with a joystick. Instead, engineers send a day's worth of instructions, the rover carries them out, and the pictures and data come back the next day. That slow conversation has still taught us that Mars was once warm and wet, with rivers and lakes.

How a space mission is planned

A space mission begins years, sometimes decades, before launch, with a question: what do we want to learn, and what kind of spacecraft can answer it. Scientists and engineers work out what instruments the mission needs, how much it will weigh, how much power it needs, and how it will talk to Earth. Every part is chosen to survive the shaking of launch, the cold of space and, for a lander, the shock of arrival.

Then comes the journey itself, which is planned like a route on a map that keeps moving. Planets move around the Sun, so a mission must aim at where its target will be when the spacecraft arrives, not where it is at launch. Engineers use gravity assists, where a spacecraft flies close to a planet and borrows some of its speed, to reach distant targets without carrying impossible amounts of fuel.

Testing takes a huge share of the time. Spacecraft are shaken, frozen, heated and baked in vacuum chambers that copy the conditions of space, because once a spacecraft launches, nobody can go and fix it. Even with all that testing, missions carry backup systems for the parts that matter most. When a mission finally launches, the team that built it has usually worked on it for years, and many of them will stay with it for the whole flight.

Following a mission today

The best way to follow a mission is through the space agency that runs it. NASA and ESA both publish mission pages, news updates and images on their official sites, and those are the only sources that can tell you what a mission is actually doing right now. If a claim about a mission does not appear on the official site, treat it with caution until it does.

Most missions have a rhythm you can learn. Launch day brings live coverage and a burst of news. Then comes the quiet cruise, when the spacecraft travels for months or years with only occasional updates. Arrival day is the next big moment, when the spacecraft reaches its target and the first pictures come back. Knowing that rhythm helps you understand why a mission can go quiet for months and then suddenly fill the news again.

You can also follow missions through the images they send back. Space agencies release the raw pictures from their cameras, and the processed versions, free for anyone to look at. The famous photographs of Mars, of Saturn's rings and of distant galaxies all began as data sent home by spacecraft, and they are still being released today as new missions fly.

Common mistakes when reading about missions

The most common mistake is mixing up missions. Apollo 11 landed on the Moon, but Apollo 13, the mission made famous by its onboard explosion, never landed at all: its crew looped around the Moon and came home safely after an oxygen tank failed. Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 are twins, but they took different paths, and Voyager 2 is the only spacecraft to have visited Uranus and Neptune.

The second mistake is trusting a random video or social media post for facts about space. Space attracts dramatic claims, from secret bases to hidden discoveries, and the more dramatic the claim, the more carefully it should be checked. A good habit is to ask one question: does the official mission page say this. If the answer is no, and no reputable science news outlet reports it either, the claim is probably wrong.

The third mistake is thinking that a mission ends when the news moves on. Voyager 1 still sends data, nearly fifty years after launch. The Mars rovers keep driving long after their landing days are forgotten. Missions are long stories, and the quiet years often produce the most important science, because that is when the spacecraft is doing its real work, far from the cameras.

For live information, always check the official source of the agency that runs the mission, and treat launch dates and mission news from unofficial pages with caution.

Questions people ask

What was Apollo 11?

Apollo 11 was the first mission to land people on the Moon. It launched in July 1969 with Neil Armstrong, Buzz Aldrin and Michael Collins aboard. Armstrong and Aldrin walked on the lunar surface on 20 July 1969, while Collins stayed in orbit in the command module. The crew returned safely to Earth, and the mission proved that humans could travel to another world and come home.

What is Voyager 1 doing now?

Voyager 1 is travelling through interstellar space, the region between the stars, more than 15 billion miles from Earth. It still sends back faint data about the space around it, and its signals take over twenty hours to reach Earth. The spacecraft runs on a nuclear power source that is slowly fading, so it will fall silent one day, but for now it keeps reporting from the farthest place any human-made object has ever reached.

How do Mars rovers work?

A Mars rover is a robotic vehicle that lands on Mars and drives across the surface, carrying cameras and science instruments. Engineers on Earth send it a day of instructions at a time, because radio signals take minutes to travel between the planets, so nobody can drive it live. The rover carries out the instructions, takes pictures and measurements, and sends the results back to Earth the next day.

How are space missions planned?

A mission starts with a science question, then engineers design a spacecraft with the instruments, power and communications to answer it. The journey is planned around the moving positions of the planets, often using gravity assists from one planet to reach the next. The spacecraft is then tested for years in chambers that copy the shaking, cold and vacuum of space, because once it launches, nobody can go and fix it.

Where can I follow a live mission?

The best place is the official site of the space agency that runs the mission, such as NASA or ESA. Their mission pages carry the real status, the latest news and the newest images. Unofficial pages and social media posts can be out of date or wrong, so if a claim about a mission does not appear on the official site, check there before believing it.

What is the most important space mission?

There is no single answer, because missions matter in different ways. Apollo 11 matters most for human exploration, because it put the first people on another world. Voyager 1 matters most for distance, as the farthest human-made object. The Mars rovers matter most for the search for past life, because they found that Mars was once warm and wet. The mission that matters most to you depends on what question you care about.
