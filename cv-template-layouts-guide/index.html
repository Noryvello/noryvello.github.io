CV Template Layouts Guide

A CV layout is the order your sections appear in, and the right layout depends on your work history. This guide shows three proven structures and helps you pick the one that puts your strongest points first.

Most CVs fail not because the person is unqualified, but because the layout buries the best parts. A hiring manager spends only a short time on a first read, so the order of your sections matters as much as what is in them: the strongest evidence should come first, and nothing important should be hiding on page two.

This guide explains the three standard CV layouts — chronological, functional and hybrid — with the section order for each and what goes in every section. Use the layout chooser to find the structure that fits your work history, then build your own CV around it.

These are layout structures to copy into your own document, not finished CVs — write every section in your own words and keep the whole CV to one or two pages.

The three layouts

Every CV has the same raw material — contact details, a profile, work history, education and skills — but the order changes everything. The chronological layout lists work history in date order, newest first, and suits people with a steady career path. The functional layout groups experience by skill instead of by date, and suits people with little work history, a career break or a change of direction. The hybrid layout combines both: a skills section up front, followed by a shorter work history.

There is no single best layout — the right one depends on your story. A steady career looks best in date order, because the dates themselves are the evidence. A career change looks best with skills first, because the reader needs to see what transfers before they see where you worked. Chronological layout

The chronological layout is the most common and the easiest for hiring managers to scan, because it tells your story in the order it happened. Section order: contact details, short profile, work history (newest first), education, skills. Each job in the work history lists the role, the employer, the dates and two or three lines on what you achieved.

This layout works best when your career moves forward in a clear line, with no long gaps and no sudden changes of direction. It is the default choice for most applicants, and it is what hiring managers expect to see, so use it unless you have a clear reason not to.

The weakness of the chronological layout is that it puts dates front and centre. If you have a long gap, a short job you would rather not highlight, or a career change, the date order will draw the reader's eye to exactly the parts you would rather play down. In those cases, one of the other layouts serves you better.

Functional layout

The functional layout groups your experience by skill instead of by date. Section order: contact details, short profile, skills groups, education, work history (brief). The skills groups have headings like "Organising", "Customer care" or "Working with numbers", and each group lists two or three examples of that skill in action, drawn from work, study, volunteering or projects.

This layout works best for a first job, a career change or a return after a break, because it leads with what you can do rather than when you did it. It also suits people whose best experience comes from outside paid work — running a club, helping at a charity, or completing a big project at school.

The weakness of the functional layout is that some hiring managers distrust it, because it can hide gaps and short jobs. To keep their trust, still include a brief work history with dates near the end, and make sure every skill claim has a real example behind it.

Hybrid layout

The hybrid layout takes the best of both: a skills section up front, followed by a work history in date order. Section order: contact details, short profile, key skills, work history, education. The skills section is short — three or four skill groups with one or two examples each — and the work history below it is briefer than in a chronological CV, often one line per role.

This layout works best when you have solid experience but want to steer the reader towards particular strengths, or when your career has changed direction and you want the transferable skills to land before the dates. It is also a good choice for experienced applicants whose early career matters less than their recent skills.

The hybrid layout takes more space than the other two, so it needs discipline: keep the skills section tight, keep the work history to one line per role, and cut anything that does not support the job you are applying for.

What goes in each section

Contact details. Your name, phone number and email address, and the town or city where you live. You do not need your full home address, your date of birth or a photo unless the job specifically asks for them.

Profile. Two or three lines at the top that say who you are, what you offer and what you are looking for. Write it last, after the rest of the CV is done, so it sums up what is actually on the page.

Work history. Role, employer and dates for each job, newest first, with two or three lines on what you achieved. Achievements beat duties: "trained six new starters" says more than "responsible for training".

Education. Your qualifications, newest first, with the school or university and the year. Recent graduates can put education before work history; everyone else puts it after.

Skills. The abilities the job needs, with a short example for each. Only list skills you can back up in an interview — a claimed skill you cannot demonstrate is worse than no claim at all.

Printing and formatting

Keep the formatting simple: one clear font, one size for body text and a larger bold size for headings, and generous white space between sections. A CV that looks crowded will not be read carefully, however good its content is.

Print a copy and read it on paper before you send it — mistakes that hide on a screen often jump out on the page. Check the spelling of every name, every date and every place, and ask someone else to read it too. A single spelling mistake in a CV can cost an interview, and it is the easiest mistake to avoid.

Save the finished CV as a PDF unless the employer asks for another format, because a PDF keeps your layout exactly as you made it on every screen and every printer.

Questions people ask

Which CV layout is best?

It depends on your work history. A steady career suits the chronological layout, a first job or a career break suits the functional layout, and a career change or mixed history suits the hybrid layout.

How long should a CV be?

One page is ideal for a first job or a short career, and two pages is fine for an experienced applicant. Anything longer is rarely read in full, so cut what does not support the job you want.

Should I include a photo?

Only if the job specifically asks for one. In most cases a photo adds nothing and takes up space that your skills and experience could use.

What if I have no work experience?

Use the functional layout and lead with skills from school, volunteering, clubs and projects. A well-organised school project or a volunteer role is real experience and counts.

Should the education section come first?

Recent graduates can put education before work history, since it is their strongest evidence. Everyone else should put work history first and education after it.
