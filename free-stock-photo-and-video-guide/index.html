Free Stock Photos and Videos Guide

Free stock photos and videos are easy to find, but free does not always mean free to use however you like. This guide points you to legitimate sources, explains the licence types you will meet, and gives you a licence checker so you can test any photo or video before you use it.

Every website, presentation and school project needs pictures, and stock libraries exist so you do not have to take every photo yourself. The good news is that large libraries of photos and videos are genuinely free. The catch is that each photo carries a licence, and the licence decides what you can do with it.

This guide covers legitimate sources only. It names the libraries worth using, explains the licence types in plain words, and gives you a checker to test any licence before you use a photo.

Legitimate sources

The safest places to find free photos are the well-known stock libraries that publish their licence terms in plain words. Unsplash, Pexels and Pixabay are the three most widely used: all three offer photos and videos free of charge, and all three state on each photo page what you may do with the file. Wikimedia Commons is a different kind of library — a huge collection of photos, many in the public domain or under Creative Commons licences, with the licence stated on every file page.

For video, the same three stock libraries carry free clips, and Coverr offers free video backgrounds. Government archives are another honest source: many national archives and space agencies publish photos in the public domain, which means anyone can use them. Whatever the source, the rule is the same: the licence is stated on the page where you found the file, and that page is the one to trust.

Be careful with search results that lead to unfamiliar sites, and with sites that ask you to create an account or install something before you can download. A legitimate stock photo is just a file you can save; anything that asks for more than that deserves suspicion.

Licence types explained

Public domain means no copyright applies, so you can use the work for almost anything without asking permission. Works enter the public domain when their copyright expires, or when a government or institution releases them. A public domain photo needs no credit, though crediting the source is good practice.

CC0 is the closest thing to public domain for a living creator: the creator has given up all rights to the work. You can use a CC0 photo or video for almost anything, including commercial work, and attribution is not required. Many stock libraries release their files under terms very close to CC0.

CC BY means you can use the work, including for commercial work, as long as you credit the creator. The credit should name the creator and link to the licence where the work is used. Failing to credit a CC BY work is a breach of the licence, even though the work itself is free.

A royalty-free stock licence means you pay nothing per use, but the terms are set by the stock site and vary from site to site. Most allow commercial use, but most forbid reselling the image itself or using it in a way that suggests the people in the photo endorse your product. Editorial use only is stricter: the photo may be used in news, commentary and education, but not in advertising or on products. Attribution rules

Attribution means saying who made the work. When a licence requires it, the credit should include the creator's name and, where the work is used online, a link to the licence. A short credit line near the photo is enough — it does not need to be long or formal.

When attribution is optional, giving credit is still good practice. Photographers who share their work for free earn nothing from it; a credit costs you nothing and helps them. If you use many photos from one photographer, a credits page that lists them all is a tidy way to do it.

Never remove a watermark or a credit line from a photo. A watermark means the photo is not free to use as it stands, and removing it does not make it free. If a photo you like is watermarked, look for a free alternative of the same subject instead.

What to check before you use a photo

First, check the licence on the page where you found the photo, not on a page that linked to it. Licences are sometimes stated wrongly by sites that republish photos, and the original page is the one that counts.

Second, think about the people in the photo. A photo of a recognisable person used in advertising can cause problems even when the photo itself is free, because the person in it has not agreed to endorse your product. Stock libraries handle this with model releases, and their licences usually cover it — but a photo found elsewhere may not.

Third, watch for trademarks and logos in the frame. A photo of a street scene with a shop sign in it is usually fine for editorial use, but using it in an advert can imply a connection with the brand. When in doubt, pick a photo without visible brands.

Finally, check the quality. A photo that looks fine as a thumbnail can be too small for a full page or a slide. Download the largest size the library offers, and check it at the size you will actually use before you build anything around it.

A photo that is free to download is not always free to use in every way. Always read the licence on the page where you found the photo, and never use a photo from a source you cannot identify.

Questions people ask

Are free stock photos really free?

Yes, from legitimate stock libraries, the photos are free to download and use under the licence shown on the page. Free does not mean free of all rules, so always read the licence first.

Do I need to credit the photographer?

It depends on the licence. CC BY requires credit, while public domain, CC0 and most royalty-free stock licences do not. Crediting the photographer is good practice even when it is not required.

Can I use free stock photos for commercial projects?

Usually yes, under public domain, CC0, CC BY and most royalty-free stock licences. Editorial-only photos are the exception: they may not be used in advertising or on products.

What does editorial use only mean?

It means the photo may be used in news, commentary and educational writing, but not in advertising, marketing or on products for sale.

Where should I look for free stock videos?

The same libraries that offer free photos — Unsplash, Pexels and Pixabay — also carry free video clips, and Coverr offers free video backgrounds. Check the licence on each clip before you use it.
