Compost Ratio Guide

Compost works best when greens and browns are mixed in the right balance. Enter the weight of greens you have, and this guide shows how much browns to add at the two common ratios, 1:2 and 1:3, with the working shown.

Greens and browns

Everything you compost falls into one of two groups:

  • Greens are wet and nitrogen-rich. They feed the microbes that break the pile down. Examples: fruit and vegetable scraps, grass clippings, coffee grounds, fresh leaves.
  • Browns are dry and carbon-rich. They add structure, let air in and soak up moisture. Examples: dry leaves, shredded cardboard, straw, newspaper, small twigs.

Too many greens and the pile turns wet and smelly. Too many browns and it breaks down very slowly. The two common ratios below are typical guidance — a starting point, not an exact rule.

How much browns to add

The 1:2 and 1:3 ratios are typical guidance. Use the same measure for both sides — this calculator works in kilograms.

Worked examples:

  • 5 kg of greens → 10 kg of browns at 1:2, 15 kg of browns at 1:3.
  • 2 kg of greens → 4 kg of browns at 1:2, 6 kg of browns at 1:3.
  • 12 kg of greens → 24 kg of browns at 1:2, 36 kg of browns at 1:3.
The 1:2 and 1:3 ratios are typical guidance, not exact science — a working pile also needs air and moisture. Do not compost meat, dairy or diseased plants.

Keeping the pile working

  • Turn it. Turn the pile every week or two to let air in. Air keeps the microbes working and stops the pile going slimy.
  • Keep it damp. Aim for damp like a wrung-out sponge. Too dry and everything stops; too wet and it smells.
  • Chop big pieces. Shred cardboard and chop thick stems so they break down faster.
  • Read the signs. If the pile smells, add browns and turn it. If nothing is happening, add greens and a little water.

Questions people ask

What is the right ratio of greens to browns?

A common starting point is 1 part greens to 2 or 3 parts browns. Use the same measure for both sides — this calculator works in kilograms. The ratios are typical guidance, not exact science, so adjust by how your pile looks and smells.

What counts as greens and what counts as browns?

Greens are wet and nitrogen-rich: fruit and vegetable scraps, grass clippings, coffee grounds and fresh leaves. Browns are dry and carbon-rich: dry leaves, shredded cardboard, straw, newspaper and small twigs.

What should I never put in compost?

Do not compost meat, dairy, fats, diseased plants or treated wood. They attract pests, smell bad or spread disease. Keep cooked food out too unless your composting setup is designed for it.

My compost smells bad — what is wrong?

A bad smell usually means the pile is too wet or has too many greens and not enough air. Add a good layer of browns, turn the pile to let air in, and hold off adding more greens until the smell clears.
