Travel Insurance Coverage Guide

Travel insurance pays for the unexpected costs of a trip, such as medical treatment abroad, a cancelled flight or lost luggage. This guide explains what a typical policy covers, what it almost never covers, and how to read the fine print before you buy. It is general information, not financial advice, and every policy is different, so the policy document always wins.

What this guide is and who needs it

Travel insurance is a contract that pays for some of the costs when a trip goes wrong. You pay a price, called a premium, before you travel, and the insurer agrees to cover certain losses listed in the policy, such as a hospital bill abroad, the cost of cancelling a trip, or the value of luggage that is lost. It does not make a bad trip good, but it stops a bad trip from becoming a large bill, and that is the whole point of buying it.

This guide is for anyone who is about to travel, whether it is a first holiday abroad, a family trip, a business visit, or a long stay in another country. It is written for people who have never read a policy document, and it explains the words insurers use in plain language. It does not sell policies, it does not quote prices, and it does not recommend one insurer over another. It teaches you how to read a policy, how to compare cover, and how to avoid the mistakes that leave travellers paying out of their own pocket.

How travel insurance works, step by step

Buying travel insurance follows the same steps almost everywhere. First, you choose the dates of your trip, the places you will visit, and who is travelling, because the price and the cover depend on all three. Then you pick a level of cover, from a basic policy that handles medical emergencies only, to a full policy that also covers cancellation, luggage and delays. You answer questions about your health and your plans, you pay the premium, and the insurer sends you a policy document, which is the contract. Read it before you travel, because what it says is what you bought.

If something goes wrong, the order of events matters. For a medical emergency, call the insurer's emergency helpline first, because the insurer may need to approve treatment or arrange it. Keep every receipt, medical note and report, and take photographs of damaged items. Report a theft to the local police and ask for a written report, because most insurers will not pay a theft claim without one. When you are home, file the claim with the documents inside the deadline written in the policy. The insurer checks the claim against the policy, subtracts the excess, which is the part you agreed to pay yourself, and pays the rest up to the policy limit.

What travel insurance usually covers, and what it does not

Most policies are built from the same sections. Medical cover pays for treatment if you fall ill or are injured abroad, and emergency evacuation pays to move you to a hospital or fly you home when local care is not enough. Trip cancellation cover repays the costs you cannot get back if you must cancel for a reason the policy lists, such as illness or a family emergency. Luggage cover pays for bags that are lost, stolen or delayed, and travel delay cover pays a fixed amount when a flight or train is delayed beyond a set number of hours. Personal liability cover pays if you accidentally injure someone or damage their property.

The exclusions matter as much as the cover. Almost no policy covers travel to a place that official advice says not to visit, injuries linked to alcohol or drug use, or treatment you travelled to receive. Pre-existing health conditions are usually excluded unless you declared them and the insurer agreed to cover them in writing. Risky activities, from skiing to scuba diving, often need extra cover, and expensive items such as cameras and jewellery often have a low single-item limit. Every section also has a maximum amount, so a policy can cover medical treatment and still leave you short if the limit is low. Read the schedule of benefits, which lists each limit in one place.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

The price of travel insurance is set by risk. Older travellers usually pay more, because medical claims rise with age. Destinations with expensive medical care cost more to insure than places where treatment is cheap, and a long trip costs more than a short one. The level of cover matters too: a policy with high medical limits and cancellation cover costs more than a basic medical-only policy. Health conditions, risky activities and travelling with valuable items can add to the price. No figure in this guide is a real premium, because prices differ by insurer, country and season; the only way to know your price is to get quotes from licensed insurers and compare them side by side.

Buying a policy takes less than an hour, but reading it properly takes longer, and that reading is the most valuable hour of the whole trip. Before you start, gather the dates of the trip, the full list of countries you will visit, the names and ages of everyone travelling, and a list of any health conditions and medicines. If you have bookings you cannot refund, note what they cost, because that is the amount cancellation cover should match. A single-trip policy covers one journey, while an annual multi-trip policy covers every trip you take in a year, which can be better value if you travel often.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The most expensive mistake is buying the cheapest policy without reading the exclusions. A low price usually means low limits, a high excess, or missing sections, and you only discover the gap when you claim. The second mistake is staying silent about health. If you do not declare a condition you know about, the insurer can refuse any claim linked to it, and the refusal can extend to the whole policy. Declare everything, answer honestly, and get the cover confirmed in writing.

Other mistakes are smaller but just as costly. Many travellers assume the cover that comes with a bank account or a card is enough, without checking its limits or whether it covers their destination and activities. Many wait until the day before departure to buy, which means cancellation cover starts too late to protect the booking. And many throw away receipts, forget to report a theft to the police, or miss the claim deadline, which is often measured in weeks, not months. Every one of these mistakes is avoidable, and the checklist below exists to catch them before they happen.

A final mistake is buying cover for the wrong trip. A policy for one country may not cover a stopover in another, and a policy bought for a beach holiday may not cover a ski trip added later. If your plans change after you buy, tell the insurer and ask whether the change is covered. An extra call before you travel is far cheaper than a refused claim afterwards.

A practical checklist you can follow

Work through this list before you pay for any policy, and again the week before you travel. It takes about twenty minutes and catches the mistakes above.

  • Compare at least three policies from licensed insurers, looking at limits and exclusions, not only price.
  • Check the medical limit and that emergency evacuation is included.
  • Read the exclusions page in full, including the sections on health, activities and valuables.
  • Declare every health condition and get the cover confirmed in writing.
  • Match the cancellation cover to the non-refundable cost of your bookings.
  • Note the excess for each section, so you know what you would pay yourself.
  • Save the emergency helpline number on your phone and carry a copy of the policy.
  • Know the claim deadline and keep every receipt, report and medical note.

When the list is done, you have done the real work of buying travel insurance. The policy itself is only a document; the value is in knowing exactly what it covers, what it does not, and what you must do if you need it. Keep the policy where you can reach it during the trip, and tell a travelling companion where it is, so someone else can call the helpline if you cannot.

Tips from real practice

Buy your insurance as soon as you book the trip, not the week before you leave. Cancellation cover usually starts on the day you buy the policy, so an early purchase protects the booking from the start, while a late purchase leaves the weeks before departure uncovered. If you travel more than twice a year, compare an annual multi-trip policy with single-trip policies, because the annual option is often better value and removes the risk of forgetting to buy.

Before you leave, check the practical details that policies never explain. Save a photograph of the policy on your phone and send a copy to someone at home, so the details are reachable even if your bag is lost. Check whether your destination needs anything special, such as winter sports cover for a ski trip or extra cover for diving, and check the single-item limit if you carry expensive equipment. Finally, remember that the cheapest policy is not the best policy: the best policy is the one whose cover matches your trip, your health and your bookings, with limits high enough to matter when you need them.

Questions people ask

What does travel insurance usually cover?

Medical treatment abroad, emergency evacuation, trip cancellation, lost or delayed luggage, travel delays, and personal liability are the usual sections. Each section has its own limit, and the details differ by policy, so the schedule of benefits in the policy document is the real answer.

What is never covered by travel insurance?

Almost no policy covers travel to a place against official advice, injuries from alcohol or drug use, or routine treatment you could have had at home. Pre-existing conditions, extreme sports and valuable items are often excluded unless you declare them and pay for extra cover.

Do I need cover for pre-existing conditions?

Yes, if you have one. Tell the insurer about any condition you have been treated for or take medicine for, and get the cover confirmed in writing. If you do not declare it, a claim linked to that condition can be refused.

What is trip cancellation cover?

It repays the non-refundable costs of a trip you have to cancel for a covered reason, such as illness, injury or a family emergency. It does not cover changing your mind, and the list of covered reasons is in the policy.

Should I buy insurance for a short trip?

Yes, if the trip involves travel abroad or non-refundable bookings. A short trip can still bring a large medical bill or a cancelled flight, and a single-trip policy for a few days is usually the cheapest kind of cover.

What should I do if something goes wrong abroad?

Call the insurer's emergency helpline first if it is medical, then keep every receipt, report and medical note. Report theft to the local police and get a written report, and file the claim as soon as you are home, within the deadline in the policy.
