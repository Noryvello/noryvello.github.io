50 AI Image Prompt Examples, Broken Down by Part

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

This page gives you fifty original image prompts in five groups of ten: portraits, landscapes, products, illustrations, and fantasy. Each prompt is broken into its subject, style, lighting, and camera parts, with a note on what each part does. Copy them, change them, and use them to learn how each part of a prompt shapes the picture.

Reading a finished prompt is useful, but understanding why it works is better. A prompt is not a magic spell; it is a description built from parts, and each part does a job. The subject says what is in the picture, the style says how it should look, the lighting sets the mood, and the camera words say where the viewer stands. When you can see the parts, you can change them on purpose instead of guessing.

Every prompt below was written fresh for this page. They are grouped by subject so you can find the kind of image you want, and each one comes with a short breakdown that names its parts and explains what each part contributes. Try them as written first, then change one part and compare the results. That comparison is where the learning happens.

Image generators are unpredictable: the same prompt can produce a different picture on every run, and the same words will look different in different tools. Treat these prompts as starting points, generate a few versions of each, and expect to adjust the wording to suit the tool you use.

How to read each breakdown

Each entry shows the full prompt first, then breaks it into four parts. The subject is the main thing in the picture and the detail that makes it specific. The style is the medium or look, from photorealistic to watercolour to flat illustration. The lighting is the light in the scene, which sets the mood more than almost anything else. The camera is the viewpoint and framing, which decides how much of the scene you see and from where.

The note at the end of each breakdown explains what the parts do together, or points out the one word that carries the most weight. When you adapt a prompt, change the subject first to make it yours, then change the style or lighting to change the feeling. Leave the camera words alone until the picture is close to what you want, because framing changes can shift the whole composition.

How to adapt these prompts to your own ideas

The fastest way to make any of these prompts your own is to swap the subject and keep the rest. Take the structure of a portrait prompt, replace the fisherman with a baker, a teacher, or a friend, and the prompt still works, because the style, lighting, and camera words are doing the same job for a new subject. Two or three changes are enough to make a prompt produce something that belongs to you.

The next step is to change the style. A landscape that works as a photograph will feel completely different as a watercolour painting or an oil painting, and an illustration that works in flat vector style will feel different again as a storybook illustration. Style is the biggest lever for changing the feeling of a picture without changing what is in it.

Finally, change the lighting when the mood is wrong. Morning light feels hopeful, golden hour feels warm and nostalgic, moonlight feels quiet and mysterious, and storm light feels dramatic. Lighting words are short and powerful, and they are often the difference between a picture that is technically fine and a picture that feels like something.

Keep a notebook of the prompts that work for you, with a note about which tool you used and what you changed. Over time you will build a personal collection of style words, lighting words, and camera words that you trust, and new prompts will start from a stronger place. The fifty prompts on this page are a starting point, not a limit.

Questions people ask

Can I copy these prompts exactly as written?

Yes, every prompt on this page was written for you to use, copy, and change as you like. The best results come from adapting a prompt to your own subject, so use these as starting points rather than fixed text.

Why is each prompt broken into parts?

Breaking a prompt into subject, style, lighting, and camera parts shows you what each word does, so you can change one part at a time when a result is close but not right. It turns prompt writing from guessing into a skill you can practise.

Do these prompts work in every image generator?

The prompts are written in plain descriptive language, so they work in any image generator that takes text prompts. The same words will give a different result in each tool, because every generator has its own default style.

What should I change first if a result looks wrong?

Change the part that controls what is wrong: the style words if the look is off, the lighting words if the mood is off, and the camera words if the framing is off. Change one part at a time so you can see what each change does.

How do I make a prompt my own instead of copying it?

Swap the subject for your own idea, change the setting, and pick a different style or mood. Two or three changes are enough to make any prompt on this page produce something that belongs to you.
