Camera Settings Guide

Aperture, shutter speed and ISO are the three settings that decide how bright a photo is and how it looks. This guide explains each one in plain words, with example settings for portraits, landscapes, sports and night shots that you can use as starting points and adjust to your light.

There is no single best setting for any scene. The numbers in this guide are starting points, not rules: light changes, cameras differ, and your own taste matters, so take a test shot, look at it, and adjust one setting at a time.

The three settings that control every photo

Every photograph is made by letting light fall on a sensor for a short time. Three settings decide how much light gets in and what the picture looks like: aperture, shutter speed and ISO. Photographers call them the exposure triangle, because changing one of them changes the balance of the other two. Once you understand what each one does, you can stop guessing and start choosing.

Aperture is the size of the opening in the lens. A wide opening lets in a lot of light; a narrow opening lets in a little. Shutter speed is how long the sensor is exposed to the light: a fast shutter freezes movement, a slow shutter lets movement blur. ISO is how sensitive the sensor is to light: a low ISO gives a clean image in bright light, a high ISO helps in the dark but adds grain. None of the three is good or bad on its own. A good photo comes from the combination that suits the light and the subject in front of you, and automatic modes do a fine job in easy light. The trouble starts in hard light, a dim room or a fast-moving subject, and that is when knowing the three settings pays off.

Aperture: how much light, and how much is sharp

Aperture is measured in f-numbers, written like f/2.8, f/8 or f/16. The confusing part is that a small f-number means a wide opening, and a large f-number means a narrow one. Think of it as a fraction: f/2 is a half, f/16 is a sixteenth, and a half is bigger than a sixteenth. An aperture of f/2.8 lets in far more light than f/16, so it suits dim light; f/16 lets in far less, so it suits bright light or a tripod.

Aperture does a second job that matters just as much: it controls depth of field, which is how much of the scene is sharp from front to back. A wide aperture gives a shallow depth of field, so your subject is sharp and the background melts into a soft blur, which is why portrait photographers love wide apertures. A narrow aperture gives a deep depth of field, so the flowers at your feet and the hills in the distance are both sharp, which is why landscape photographers often choose f/8 to f/11. A very wide aperture makes focusing harder, because only a thin slice of the scene is sharp, so a portrait taken at f/1.8 can have one eye sharp and the other soft. Use the widest aperture when you want strong background blur, and stop down a little when you want more of the scene in focus.

Shutter speed: freezing motion or showing it

Shutter speed is measured in fractions of a second: 1/1000, 1/250, 1/60, or whole seconds for very long exposures. A fast shutter speed, such as 1/500 or 1/1000, freezes movement: a running child, a bird in flight, water droplets in the air. A slow shutter speed, such as 1/15 or one full second, lets movement blur: flowing water turns silky, car headlights draw lines through a night street. Both effects are useful, and the choice depends on what you want the photo to say.

Shutter speed also decides whether your own hands blur the photo. Below about 1/60 of a second, most people cannot hold a camera still enough for a sharp handheld shot. A useful rule of thumb is the reciprocal rule: keep the shutter speed faster than one divided by your focal length, so a 50 mm lens wants 1/50 or faster, and a 200 mm lens wants 1/200 or faster. Image stabilisation helps with your own shake, but it cannot freeze a moving subject. When you need a slow shutter speed, a tripod changes everything: with the camera held still, you can use exposures of seconds or even minutes for night skies, light trails and smooth water.

ISO: brightness when light is short

ISO is the sensor's sensitivity to light. A low ISO, such as 100 or 200, gives the cleanest image with the most detail and the least grain. A high ISO, such as 3200 or 6400, lets you shoot in much darker places, but the price is noise: a grainy, speckled look, especially in the shadows, and colours that lose some of their richness. Modern cameras handle high ISO far better than older ones, but the trade-off is still real on every camera.

The sensible habit is to keep ISO as low as the light allows, and to raise it only when aperture and shutter speed cannot give you a bright enough photo on their own. In daylight, ISO 100 to 400 covers almost everything. Indoors, ISO 800 to 1600 is common. At night, ISO 3200 and above may be the only way to get a sharp handheld shot. A slightly noisy photo is always better than a blurred one, so do not be afraid of high ISO when the alternative is camera shake. Many cameras have an automatic ISO setting with a maximum you can set, which is a good compromise: you control aperture and shutter speed, and the camera handles ISO within limits you choose.

How the three settings work together

The three settings trade against each other. If you narrow the aperture to get more depth of field, less light gets in, so you must either slow the shutter speed or raise the ISO to keep the same brightness. If you speed up the shutter to freeze action, you must either open the aperture or raise the ISO. Every change has a cost somewhere else, and good photography is mostly choosing which cost you are willing to pay.

Imagine photographing a child playing in a garden in the late afternoon. You want a shutter speed of at least 1/500 to freeze the running, and you would like a soft background, so you open the aperture to f/2.8. With plenty of daylight, ISO 200 is enough and the photo is clean and sharp. Now imagine the same child indoors in the evening, where the light is much weaker. At f/2.8 and 1/500, the photo is too dark. You can raise the ISO to 1600, which brightens the image at the cost of some grain, or slow the shutter to 1/125 and accept a little motion blur, or add light with a lamp. There is no perfect answer, only the compromise that suits the moment. This is why cameras offer semi-automatic modes: in aperture priority you choose the aperture and the camera picks the shutter speed, and in shutter priority it is the other way round. Many experienced photographers use these modes most of the time.

Starting points by scene

For a portrait, the usual goal is a sharp face and a soft, uncluttered background. A good starting point is an aperture of f/2.8, a shutter speed of 1/200, and the lowest ISO the light allows, often ISO 100 to 400 outdoors. Focus on the eye nearest the camera, because viewers look at the eyes first. For a group, stop down to f/5.6 or f/8 so people at different distances are all sharp. For a landscape, the usual goal is sharpness from the nearest rock to the far horizon: an aperture of f/8 to f/11, ISO 100, and whatever shutter speed the light demands, often with the camera on a tripod. Focus about a third of the way into the scene. Light matters more than settings here: the same valley looks flat at noon and extraordinary at sunrise, when low light rakes across the ground and long shadows show every fold of the land.

For sports and fast action, shutter speed is the boss: 1/500 to 1/1000, an aperture of f/2.8 to f/4, and auto ISO with a maximum you are comfortable with, often ISO 3200. Use continuous autofocus and take bursts of frames so you can pick the sharpest. The right shutter speed depends on the sport: a child on a swing freezes at 1/250, a footballer needs 1/500 or faster, and a bird in flight may need 1/2000. For night photography, the starting point depends on the tripod. With one, use ISO 100, f/8 to f/11, and several seconds, chosen by trial. Without one, open the aperture as wide as it goes, raise the ISO to 3200 or higher, and keep the shutter at 1/60 or faster. Stars need the widest aperture, ISO 3200, and about 15 to 25 seconds on a tripod: longer than that and the stars trail into short lines, because the Earth is turning.

Common mistakes and how to fix them

Blurry photos are the most common complaint, and they usually have one of three causes. If everything is soft, the camera moved: use a faster shutter speed, a tripod, or a steadier hold with your elbows tucked in. If the subject is soft but the background is sharp, the focus landed in the wrong place: focus on the subject's eyes or the most important detail, and use a single focus point rather than letting the camera choose. If only the moving parts are blurred, the shutter was too slow for the action: raise the shutter speed or the ISO.

Photos that are too dark or too bright are usually an exposure problem. If the camera's automatic exposure is fooled by a bright sky or a dark background, use exposure compensation, a simple plus or minus control that brightens or darkens the whole image. A snowy scene usually needs plus one or two stops, because the camera tries to turn the white snow grey. Grainy photos mean the ISO was high, which happens when the light was poor: the fixes are to add light, use a wider aperture, slow the shutter with a tripod, or accept the grain as part of the photo's character. Washed-out colours usually mean harsh midday light or a wrong white balance setting, so shoot in the golden hour for richer colour.

Questions people ask

What is the best camera setting for portraits?

There is no single best setting, because the light and the lens change every time. A good starting point outdoors is an aperture of f/2.8, a shutter speed of 1/200, and the lowest ISO the light allows. Focus on the eye nearest the camera, take a test shot, and adjust from there.

What does aperture actually do?

Aperture is the size of the opening in the lens. A wide aperture, written as a small f-number like f/2.8, lets in more light and blurs the background. A narrow aperture, like f/11, lets in less light and keeps more of the scene sharp from front to back.

What shutter speed freezes fast motion?

A shutter speed of 1/500 or faster freezes most sports and running children. Very fast subjects, like a bird in flight, may need 1/1000 or 1/2000. If the photo is too dark at those speeds, open the aperture wider or raise the ISO.

When should I raise the ISO?

Raise the ISO when aperture and shutter speed alone cannot give you a bright enough photo, such as indoors or at night. A higher ISO brightens the image but adds grain, so keep it as low as the light allows and raise it only as far as you need.

Do I need a tripod for night photos?

For long exposures of a second or more, yes, a tripod is the only way to keep the photo sharp. Without one, use the widest aperture, a high ISO, and a shutter speed of 1/60 or faster, and rest the camera on something solid if you can.

Should a beginner use manual mode?

Not at first. Aperture priority and shutter priority modes teach you one setting at a time while the camera handles the rest, which is a faster way to learn. Move to full manual mode when you want total control, such as for studio work or long night exposures.
