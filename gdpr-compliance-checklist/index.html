GDPR Compliance Checklist

This checklist covers the GDPR basics for a small website: consent, privacy policy, data rights, cookies, and breach basics. Tick each item as you put it in place, and your ticks are saved in your own browser only.

Information only — this page is not legal advice, and it is not a guarantee of compliance. GDPR is a large law with rules that depend on what data you collect and why. For a business that handles sensitive data or large amounts of personal data, get advice from a qualified professional.

What GDPR means for a small website

GDPR is the General Data Protection Regulation, the European Union law that governs how personal data is collected, used, and stored. Personal data means any information that can identify a living person, directly or indirectly: a name, an email address, a photo, an online identifier, or even an IP address in some cases. If your website collects any of these from people in the European Union or the European Economic Area, GDPR applies to you — even if your business is based elsewhere, and even if your website is small.

GDPR is built on a few simple ideas. Collect only the data you need, for a purpose you can explain. Tell people what you collect and why. Keep it safe. Keep it only as long as you need it. And respect people's rights over their own data — including the right to see it, correct it, and ask for it to be deleted. A small website that follows those ideas honestly is most of the way there; the rest is paperwork and process, which is what this checklist covers.

This checklist is written for a small website: a business site, a blog, or an online shop with a contact form, a newsletter, or a simple checkout. It does not cover hospitals, schools, or businesses that process sensitive data such as health records at scale, which have extra rules. The full text of the regulation is published at gdpr-info.eu, and the European Commission data protection pages explain the law in plain terms.

Lawful basis and consent

Under GDPR, you must have a lawful basis for processing personal data — a reason the law recognises. For most small websites, the common bases are consent (the person agreed), contract (you need the data to deliver what they asked for), and legitimate interests (you have a genuine business reason that does not override the person's rights). You should know, for each kind of data you collect, which basis you rely on, and you should be able to explain it in one sentence.

Consent, where you rely on it, must be freely given, specific, informed, and shown by a clear action — a ticked box that the visitor ticks themselves, not a box ticked for them in advance. It must be as easy to withdraw consent as it was to give it. Silence, a pre-ticked box, or a vague statement buried in small print is not consent under GDPR.

Privacy policy and transparency

Your privacy policy is the document that tells visitors what you collect, why you collect it, how long you keep it, who you share it with, and what rights they have. It must be written in plain language, easy to find — usually linked from the footer of every page — and honest about what actually happens. A privacy policy copied from another website that describes data practices you do not follow is worse than no policy at all.

A good privacy policy for a small website covers the identity of the business, the data collected and the lawful basis for each kind, how long data is kept, who it is shared with (for example a hosting provider or a payment processor), and how visitors can exercise their rights. It should also say whether data is sent outside the European Union, and what safeguards apply if it is.

Data rights: access, correction, deletion

GDPR gives people rights over their personal data, and your website must be ready to honour them. The main rights are the right to see the data you hold about them, the right to have wrong data corrected, the right to have data deleted in some circumstances, the right to object to some kinds of processing, and the right to receive their data in a portable form. These rights are sometimes called data subject rights, and a request to exercise one is sometimes called a data subject request.

You should have a simple process for handling these requests: a way for people to reach you, a person responsible for answering, and a record of what was asked and what was done. The law gives you one month to respond to a request, with a possible extension of two further months for complex requests if you tell the person why. Most requests to a small website are simple, and the best habit is to answer them quickly and helpfully rather than waiting for the deadline.

Cookies and trackers

Cookies are small files that a website stores on a visitor's device, and trackers are the tools that use them to recognise visitors, remember choices, or measure behaviour. Some cookies are strictly necessary — they keep a visitor logged in, or remember what is in a shopping basket — and these do not need consent. Most other cookies, including analytics cookies and marketing cookies, need the visitor's consent before they are set.

A cookie banner should tell visitors what cookies you use and why, and it should let them accept or refuse the non-essential ones with equal ease. A banner with a large accept button and a hidden refuse link does not meet that test. Before you build a banner, list the cookies and trackers your website actually uses — including any added by tools you have embedded, such as video players, maps, or social media buttons — because you cannot honestly describe what you have not counted.

Security and breach basics

GDPR requires you to keep personal data safe, with measures that fit the risk. For a small website, the basics are: a secure connection (https), strong passwords and two-factor sign-in where available, software kept up to date, access limited to the people who need it, and regular backups stored safely. You do not need expensive systems, but you do need to take the basics seriously and to check them regularly.

A personal data breach is an event that exposes personal data — a hack, a lost device, an email sent to the wrong person, or a database left open to the internet. If a breach happens, the law requires you to tell your supervisory authority within 72 hours of becoming aware of it, unless the breach is unlikely to risk people's rights. If the breach is likely to create a high risk for the people affected, you must also tell those people without undue delay. Having a simple plan written down in advance — who to call, what to check, what to say — makes those 72 hours far less frightening.

Records and accountability

GDPR expects you to be able to show that you follow the rules, not just to claim that you do. For most small businesses, that means keeping a simple record of your processing: what data you collect, why, where it comes from, who you share it with, how long you keep it, and what security measures protect it. A single page or a simple table is enough for a small website — the point is that the record exists and is kept up to date.

Accountability also means knowing who does what. If your website uses a hosting provider, an email service, or a payment processor, those are data processors working for you, and GDPR expects a written agreement with each one that covers how they handle the data. Most established providers publish such agreements or include them in their terms; check that yours are in place, and keep a note of who your processors are.

If you transfer personal data outside the European Union — for example to a service based in another country — GDPR has extra rules about safeguards. Many well-known services have standard safeguards in place, but you should know where your data goes and be able to say so in your privacy policy. If you are unsure, ask your provider, and check the European Commission pages linked at the top of this guide.

What to do first

If you are starting from nothing, work through the checklist in this order. First, list the personal data your website collects and why — you cannot protect what you have not counted. Second, write or fix your privacy policy so that it honestly describes what you do. Third, fix your consent: unticked boxes, a fair cookie banner, and an easy way to withdraw consent. Fourth, set up a simple process for data requests, so that the first request does not catch you unprepared.

Fifth, check your security basics: https, strong passwords, updates, and backups. Sixth, write down your breach plan and your record of processing. Seventh, check your processors and your data transfers. None of these steps needs a lawyer or a large budget, and each one makes your website more trustworthy. A small website that does these things honestly is doing more for its visitors' privacy than many large ones.

Finally, remember that compliance is not a one-time project. Revisit this checklist once a year, and again whenever your website changes — a new form, a new tool, a new service, or a new way of using data. Each change is a moment to ask the same questions again: what data, why, for how long, and who can see it. Answering those questions honestly, every time, is what GDPR really asks of you.

Questions people ask

Does GDPR apply to my small website?

Yes, if your website collects personal data from people in the European Union or the European Economic Area, even if your business is based elsewhere and even if your website is small. Personal data includes names, email addresses, photos, and online identifiers. If you have a contact form, a newsletter, or analytics, GDPR almost certainly applies to you.

What counts as personal data?

Personal data is any information that can identify a living person, directly or indirectly. That includes a name, an email address, a photo, a location, an IP address in some cases, and an online identifier such as a cookie ID. Anonymous data that cannot be linked back to a person is not personal data, but most data a small website collects can be linked back.

Do I need a cookie banner?

If your website sets non-essential cookies, such as analytics or marketing cookies, you need the visitor's consent before setting them, and a cookie banner is the usual way to ask. Strictly necessary cookies, such as those that keep a visitor logged in or remember a shopping basket, do not need consent. Your banner should offer accept and refuse with equal ease.

What is a lawful basis?

A lawful basis is a reason the law recognises for processing personal data. The common ones for a small website are consent, contract (you need the data to deliver what the person asked for), and legitimate interests (you have a genuine business reason that does not override the person's rights). You should know which basis you rely on for each kind of data you collect.

How quickly must I respond to a data request?

GDPR gives you one month to respond to a data subject request, such as a request to see or delete personal data. For complex requests you may extend this by two further months, but you must tell the person why. Most requests to a small website are simple, and the best habit is to answer quickly and helpfully.

What should I do after a data breach?

First, contain the breach and work out what data was exposed and who is affected. Then tell your supervisory authority within 72 hours of becoming aware of the breach, unless it is unlikely to risk people's rights. If the breach is likely to create a high risk for the people affected, tell them too, without undue delay. Having a written plan in advance makes this much easier.
