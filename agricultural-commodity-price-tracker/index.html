Crop Price Tracker Guide

Crop prices move with the harvest, the weather, transport costs and demand, and a farmer who follows them can choose when and where to sell. This guide explains what moves prices, where official prices are published, and how to read a market report and keep a simple tracking sheet. It shows no live prices, because a price on a web page is out of date the moment it is printed.

What this is and who needs it

A crop price tracker is a simple habit, not a machine: a farmer records the prices of the crops they grow, week by week, and watches how those prices move. The record can live in a notebook, on a sheet of paper pinned to a wall, or in a phone. What matters is not the tool but the habit, because a farmer who knows last month's price, last year's price at the same time, and the price in two different markets can make a far better decision about when to sell and where to sell.

This guide is for farmers who sell their own produce, whether at a local market, to a trader, through a cooperative or direct to buyers. It is also useful for anyone who advises farmers, or who is learning how farm markets work. It explains the ideas in plain words: what a farm-gate price is, what a market price is, why the two are different, and how to read a price report without being misled by it.

This page shows no live prices and no price figures for any crop. Prices change every day, differ between markets in the same region, and depend on quality, quantity and the season. A price printed on a web page would be wrong for most readers most of the time. Instead, this guide teaches you how to find the real prices for your crops in your markets, and how to keep a record that stays useful year after year.

How it works, step by step

Tracking starts with choosing what to follow. Pick the crops you actually sell, and for each one pick the markets that matter to you: the nearest local market, the larger market you sometimes travel to, and the price a trader offers at your farm gate. Write these down before you start, because a tracker that follows everything follows nothing well. Three crops and two markets are a good start for most farmers.

Next, find the official price sources for your area. Many countries publish crop prices through a government agriculture department, a market board, or a radio or text service, and many markets display their prices on a board at the entrance. Ask at your local market, at the agriculture office, or at your cooperative which source farmers in your area trust. Official sources are not perfect, but they are far more reliable than a rumour or a single trader's word.

Then record the price on the same day each week, in the same way, with the unit written down. A price means nothing without its unit: the price per bag, per kilogram or per crate, for a named quality, at a named market, on a named date. The tracking sheet below shows the columns to use. After a few weeks, the sheet starts to show a pattern: prices rising or falling, one market paying more than another, and the weeks when prices usually turn. That pattern is the real value of tracking, and it is something no single price can give you.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

Tracking prices costs almost nothing. You need a notebook or a sheet of paper, a pen, and a few minutes once a week. The main cost is the habit itself: remembering to record the price on the same day each week, even in a busy season. Farmers who tie the habit to something they already do, such as the weekly market visit, find it far easier to keep up than farmers who rely on memory alone.

Before you start, decide on the details and write them at the top of your sheet. Write the crop and the variety, the unit you will use, the quality grade if your market uses one, and the markets you will follow. Agree these with yourself in advance, because changing the unit halfway through the season makes the record hard to read. If you sell in more than one unit, pick one unit for the record and convert the others when you write them down.

You also need to know how to read the prices you find. A price report usually gives the price per unit, the market, the date, and sometimes the range of prices paid that day, from low to high. The range matters: a crop that sold between two prices on the same day tells you that quality, timing and bargaining all play a part. When you record a price, note whether it is the price you were offered, the price you actually received, or the price published by the market, because those three can be different.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The first mistake is trusting a single price. A price from one market on one day tells you almost nothing, because prices move with the weather, the day of the week and the number of buyers present. Always compare at least two markets and at least a few weeks of records before you draw any conclusion. A farmer who sells on the strength of one good price often finds the next day's price was better, or that the next market would have paid more.

The second mistake is comparing prices without comparing units and quality. A price per large bag cannot be compared with a price per small bag, and a price for top quality cannot be compared with a price for mixed quality. Always write the unit and the quality beside the price, and convert to the same unit before you compare. Traders know this trick well, and a farmer who does not check the unit can be paid less while thinking they were paid more.

The third mistake is confusing the farm-gate price with the market price. The farm-gate price is what a buyer pays at your farm, and it is lower than the market price because the buyer pays for transport, handling and the risk of the journey. That difference is not always unfair, but it should be understood: if the gap between the farm-gate price and the market price is far larger than the transport cost, you may be better off taking the crop to market yourself, or selling through a cooperative that shares the transport.

The fourth mistake is waiting too long for a better price. Prices do rise after harvest, but stored crops can spoil, lose weight, or be eaten by pests, and the money tied up in storage could have been used for the next planting. A tracking sheet helps here too: if your records show that prices in your area usually rise for a few weeks after harvest and then fall again, you can plan your selling around that pattern instead of guessing.

A practical checklist you can follow

Use this checklist to set up tracking and keep it going through the season. It takes an hour to set up and a few minutes a week to maintain, and it pays for itself the first time it stops you selling at the worst price of the month.

  • Choose the crops you sell and the markets that matter to you, and write them down.
  • Find the official price source for your area: a market board, an agriculture office, a radio service, or a cooperative notice.
  • Copy the tracking sheet above into a notebook, with the unit and quality written at the top.
  • Record the price on the same day each week, and note whether it is an offered price, a received price or a published price.
  • Write a short note each week about anything that might explain the price.
  • Once a month, look back over the sheet and note the pattern: rising, falling, or flat.
  • Before you sell, check the price in at least two markets and check your own records for the same time last year.
  • After you sell, record the price you actually received, so next season's record is honest.

Keep old sheets. A record that covers two or three seasons is far more valuable than a record of one, because it shows the seasons when prices behaved unusually and the seasons when they followed the usual pattern. Over time, your own records become the most trusted price guide you own, because they describe your crops, your markets and your experience.

Tips from real practice

Talk to other farmers about prices, and share what you learn. A group of farmers who compare their records each week knows more than any single farmer, and a cooperative that collects prices from its members can bargain with traders from a position of strength. Price information is one of the few things that becomes more valuable when it is shared, because a trader cannot tell five farmers five different stories about the same market.

Watch the things that move prices before they move them. A dry spell in a growing region, a festival that raises demand, a road closure that cuts off a market, or a large buyer arriving in the area all show up in prices a few days later. Farmers who notice these things early can sell before a fall or hold on for a rise, while farmers who only watch the price itself are always reacting after the change.

Finally, remember that the best price is not always the highest price. A buyer who pays a little less but pays on the day, buys every week, and takes the crop from your farm may be worth more over a season than a buyer who promises a high price and pays late or not at all. Use your tracking sheet to judge prices, and use your experience to judge buyers, and you will sell better year after year.

Questions people ask

Why do crop prices change so much?

Crop prices change because supply and demand change all the time. A good harvest in one region can lower prices, while a dry spell or a flood can raise them. Festivals, school terms, transport costs, fuel prices and the number of buyers at a market all move prices too. That is why a price from last week is only a guide, and why a weekly record is more useful than a single price.

Where do farmers check official crop prices?

Many countries publish crop prices through a government agriculture department, a market board, or a radio or text service, and many markets display prices on a board at the entrance. Ask at your local market, at the agriculture office, or at your cooperative which source farmers in your area trust. Official sources are not perfect, but they are far more reliable than a rumour or a single trader's word.

What is the difference between a farm-gate price and a market price?

The farm-gate price is what a buyer pays for your crop at your farm, and the market price is what the same crop sells for at the market. The market price is higher because it includes transport, handling, market fees and the trader's margin. If the gap between the two is far larger than the transport cost, you may be better off taking the crop to market yourself or selling through a cooperative.

How do I read a commodity price report?

A price report usually gives the price per unit, the market, the date, and sometimes the range of prices paid that day. Always check the unit and the quality grade before comparing two prices, and note whether the price is the price offered, the price received, or the price published by the market. A range tells you that quality, timing and bargaining all played a part that day.

Should I sell now or wait for a better price?

That depends on your crop, your storage, and what your own records show. Prices often rise for a while after harvest, but stored crops can spoil, lose weight or be eaten by pests, and money tied up in storage cannot be used for the next planting. Check your records for the same time last year, check the price in more than one market, and weigh the cost of waiting against the price you might gain.

What is a futures price in simple words?

A futures price is a price agreed today for a crop that will be delivered in the future. It shows what buyers and sellers expect the price to be later, but it is not a promise: the real price on the day can be higher or lower. Small farmers rarely trade futures themselves, but the futures price is sometimes quoted in the news as a sign of where the market is heading.
