What this dictionary is

This is a curated dictionary of everyday words in six languages: English, Bengali, Spanish, Hindi, French and Arabic. It holds 266 words chosen for daily life — greetings, family, food, travel, numbers, time, home, body, animals, colours, adjectives and verbs. Search by the English word, or browse by category, and each entry shows the translation in all six languages, with a romanised pronunciation hint for Bengali, Hindi and Arabic.

The words are the ones you actually need when you travel, when you meet someone from another country, or when you start learning a language. You will not find rare words or technical terms here. What you will find is the vocabulary of ordinary days: saying hello, asking for water, counting to twenty, naming the days of the week, and talking about your family.

Every translation is a standard everyday word, checked against how people really speak. Where a language has more than one everyday word for the same thing, this dictionary gives the most widely understood one. Pronunciation hints use plain English letters, so you can sound out a Bengali, Hindi or Arabic word even if you cannot read its script yet.

How to use it to learn

The best way to use this dictionary is a little at a time. Pick one category — food, for example — and learn five words a day. Say each word out loud, cover the translations, and try to remember them. Come back the next day and test yourself again before you learn five more. Five words a day becomes 150 words in a month, which is enough to handle many everyday situations.

Learn words in pairs and groups, not one by one. Learn 'mother' and 'father' together, 'hot' and 'cold' together, 'morning' and 'evening' together. Opposites and families of words stick better than single words, because each word reminds you of the others. Use the category filter to study one group at a time.

Use the words as soon as you learn them. Greet someone in their language, name the food on your plate, count the stairs as you climb them. A word you have used once is worth ten words you have only read. And copy entries you want to keep: the copy button on each entry puts the whole translation on your clipboard, so you can paste it into your notes.

Review words you have already learned before you add new ones. A quick review takes two minutes: look at the English word, try to remember the translation, then check. Words you get right need less review; words you miss need another look tomorrow. This simple habit — test, check, repeat — moves words from short-term memory into long-term memory, which is where they need to be when you actually need them.

The languages covered

English is the language you search in. Every entry starts from an English word, so you can find what you need even if you know nothing of the other five languages yet.

Bengali is spoken by hundreds of millions of people, mainly in Bangladesh and the Indian state of West Bengal. It has its own beautiful script, and this dictionary shows Bengali words in that script with a romanised hint beside each one.

Spanish is spoken across Spain and most of Latin America. Its spelling is close to its sound, which makes it one of the friendliest languages for a beginner to read.

Hindi is one of the most spoken languages in the world, used across northern India and beyond. It is written in the Devanagari script, shown here with a romanised hint for every word.

French is spoken in France, Canada, and many countries in Africa. Its spelling takes practice, but its everyday words are short and useful.

Arabic is spoken across the Middle East and North Africa, and it is written from right to left. This dictionary shows Arabic words in Arabic script with a romanised hint, so you can start speaking before you learn to read the script.

False friends that catch learners out

A false friend is a word that looks familiar in another language but means something different. False friends are the classic trap for learners, because they feel safe and turn out to be wrong. Here are some real ones from the languages in this dictionary.

  • Spanish 'embarazada' does not mean embarrassed. It means pregnant. The Spanish word for embarrassed is 'avergonzado'. This is the most famous false friend in Spanish, and it has caused many awkward moments.
  • French 'actuellement' does not mean actually. It means currently or at the moment. The French word for actually is 'en fait'.
  • Spanish 'sensible' does not mean sensible. It means sensitive. The Spanish word for sensible is 'sensato'.
  • French 'demander' does not mean to demand. It means to ask. If you say 'je demande' you are asking politely, not making a demand.
  • Spanish 'éxito' does not mean exit. It means success. The Spanish word for exit is 'salida'.
  • Spanish 'largo' does not mean large. It means long. The Spanish word for large is 'grande'.

The lesson is simple: never guess a word from how it looks. Check it, even when you feel sure. This dictionary exists for exactly that reason.

Words that surprise learners

Some words in this dictionary surprise people the first time they see them. In Hindi, the word 'kal' means both tomorrow and yesterday — the tense of the sentence tells you which one. In Spanish, 'mañana' means both tomorrow and morning. In French, 'bonjour' is both hello and good morning. These are not mistakes in the dictionary: they are how the languages really work.

Other surprises come from words that share a meaning across languages. 'Mango', 'banana' and 'coffee' sound similar in many languages because the foods themselves travelled around the world and took their names with them. 'Hotel', 'bus' and 'bank' are close cousins in several languages for the same reason.

And some English words have no single-word match. 'Nice to meet you' is a full sentence in every language here, not one word. That is normal: languages divide the world differently, and part of learning a language is learning where its boundaries fall.

Worked examples

At a market. You want to buy apples. You search 'apple' and find the word in the local language, then search 'how much' — well, this dictionary does not have phrases, only words, so you search 'money' and 'ten' and hold up your fingers. It is not elegant, but it works, and the shopkeeper will usually smile and help you the rest of the way.

Meeting a family. You visit friends and meet their parents. You search 'mother' and 'father', say the words, and add 'thank you' at the end of the meal. Five words, learned in two minutes, and you have shown respect in their language. That is what a dictionary of everyday words is for.

Studying numbers. You filter by the numbers category and learn one to ten, then twenty, thirty, forty and fifty. The next day you test yourself: cover the translations and say each number out loud. Within a week you can count, give your phone number, and understand prices — all from one small group of words.

What this dictionary cannot do

This is a curated dictionary of everyday words, not a full language dictionary. It holds 266 words, while a full dictionary holds tens of thousands. It does not cover grammar, verb forms, or the many meanings a word can have. The word 'bank' here means the place that keeps money; it does not cover the bank of a river. The word 'right' here means a direction; it does not cover being correct.

For words not listed here, use a full dictionary. Cambridge Dictionary covers English words with clear examples. Oxford Learner's Dictionaries is written for learners, with simple definitions. WordReference covers many language pairs with forums where real speakers answer questions. Wiktionary is a free dictionary with entries in hundreds of languages.

Use this dictionary for the words of daily life, and use a full dictionary when you need depth. Together they cover far more than either one alone.
