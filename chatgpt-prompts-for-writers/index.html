30 ChatGPT Prompts for Writers

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

This page gives you 30 original ChatGPT prompts for writers, organised into five groups: ideas, outlines, dialogue, editing, and writer’s block. Every prompt comes with a one-line note on how to adapt it to your own writing.

Writers use AI best as a sparring partner, not a ghostwriter. A chatbot can throw out ideas, test a structure, sharpen a line of dialogue, or spot a weak paragraph, and you stay in charge of what survives.

The prompts below are written for that kind of partnership. Each one asks the AI to do a small, specific job, and the adaptation note shows how to point it at your own story, article, or essay.

AI output is a draft, not a finished piece. ChatGPT can produce flat phrasing, repeat ideas, and drift from your voice, so read everything it gives you with an editor's eye. Your judgment, your taste, and your voice are what make the writing yours.

How to Use These Prompts

Replace the words in square brackets with your own story, topic, or characters before you send a prompt. The more specific you are, the less generic the answer: a prompt that names your setting, your character, and your mood will always beat a prompt that says "give me ideas."

Treat the answers as raw material. Take the one idea that sparks something, ignore the rest, and rewrite everything in your own voice. A prompt that gives you five options is doing its job even if you only keep one, because the one you keep is the one that fits your work.

Ideas

Use these prompts to find starting points and fresh angles.

  1. "Give me five story ideas that combine [setting] with [unusual element], each in one sentence."
    Adapt it: name any setting and any unusual element.
  2. "List 10 what-if questions based on [theme] that could spark a short story."
    Adapt it: name your theme and change the number.
  3. "Suggest three fresh angles on [topic] that most writers have not covered."
    Adapt it: name any topic you want to write about.
  4. "Invent a character with one surprising habit, one secret, and one goal, and explain how they connect."
    Adapt it: ask for a different kind of character.
  5. "Give me five titles for a piece about [topic], each suggesting a different mood."
    Adapt it: name your topic and the moods you want.
  6. "Brainstorm 10 opening scenes for a story set in [place], each starting with a different sense."
    Adapt it: name any place and change the number of scenes.

Outlines

Use these prompts to give a piece of writing a shape before you draft it.

  1. "Turn this one-line idea into a three-act outline with a turning point in each act."
    Adapt it: paste your one-line idea.
  2. "Break my article idea about [topic] into five sections, each with a one-line purpose."
    Adapt it: name your topic and change the number of sections.
  3. "Help me arrange these scenes in the order that builds the most tension."
    Adapt it: paste your scene list.
  4. "Outline a chapter that moves from [starting point] to [ending point] in four beats."
    Adapt it: name the start and end of your chapter.
  5. "Give me two different structures for the same essay, and explain what each one does best."
    Adapt it: paste your essay idea or thesis.
  6. "Turn my messy notes into a clean outline with headings and two supporting points each."
    Adapt it: paste your notes and change the structure.

Dialogue

Use these prompts to make characters sound like people, not like scripts.

  1. "Write a short exchange between two characters who disagree about [issue], without either saying the word disagree."
    Adapt it: name any issue your characters argue about.
  2. "Rewrite this dialogue so each character sounds different: one speaks in short sentences, the other in long ones."
    Adapt it: paste your dialogue and change the contrast.
  3. "Give me five ways a character could say [line] to show anger, fear, joy, sarcasm, and relief."
    Adapt it: pick any line and any set of feelings.
  4. "Write dialogue for a scene where a character hides the truth, using what they do not say."
    Adapt it: describe the secret your character is hiding.
  5. "Turn this flat conversation into one with subtext: what each person wants but will not ask for."
    Adapt it: paste your conversation.
  6. "Write a first meeting between two strangers at [place], keeping it under 150 words."
    Adapt it: name any place and change the length.

Editing

Use these prompts to tighten and sharpen a draft you have already written.

  1. "Read this paragraph and cut it by a third, keeping every idea intact."
    Adapt it: paste one paragraph at a time.
  2. "Point out the three weakest sentences in this passage and explain why each one drags."
    Adapt it: paste any passage you are unsure about.
  3. "Rewrite this paragraph in a plainer style, keeping the meaning exactly the same."
    Adapt it: paste any paragraph that feels overwritten.
  4. "Find the repeated words in this passage and suggest a replacement for each."
    Adapt it: paste any passage that feels repetitive.
  5. "Check this section for flow: mark where the reader might get lost and suggest a bridge sentence."
    Adapt it: paste any section that jumps awkwardly.
  6. "Tighten this opening: make the first sentence hook the reader in under 20 words."
    Adapt it: paste your opening lines.

Writer's Block

Use these prompts when you are stuck and need a way back into the work.

  1. "Give me a five-minute writing warm-up based on [random word]."
    Adapt it: pick any word, however strange.
  2. "Ask me three questions about my story that will help me find the next scene."
    Adapt it: describe your story in one line first.
  3. "Suggest five ways to get past a scene where my main character is stuck."
    Adapt it: describe where your character is stuck.
  4. "Give me a short prompt that forces me to write from a different character's point of view."
    Adapt it: name the character you never write from.
  5. "Help me list what my character wants in this scene, what blocks them, and what they try next."
    Adapt it: name the scene you are stuck on.
  6. "Turn my complaint about being stuck into a 100-word piece of writing I can build on."
    Adapt it: write your complaint in plain words first.

Keeping Your Voice in Charge

The risk of writing with AI is not that it writes badly, but that it writes blandly. ChatGPT favours safe, middle-of-the-road phrasing, and a page of it reads like a page of anyone. Your voice is the thing that makes a piece yours: your rhythms, your images, your way of seeing.

Protect it by using AI for structure and options, and keeping the final words for yourself. Let the chatbot suggest five titles, then write a sixth that is better. Let it outline a chapter, then write the chapter in your own sentences. When you do rewrite an AI draft, read it aloud: if a sentence does not sound like you, change it until it does.

Making Prompts Your Own

The prompts on this page are starting points. As you use them, you will find the ones that fit your kind of writing, and you will start changing them: different lengths, different moods, different kinds of questions. Keep a note of the versions that work, organised by the stage of writing they help with.

Over time, that note becomes a toolkit you reach for without thinking. A prompt for openings, a prompt for stuck scenes, a prompt for tightening a draft: small tools, shaped by your own experience, worth more than any long list.

Questions people ask

Is it okay to use AI when I write?

Many writers use AI for brainstorming, outlining, and editing help, and keep the final writing their own. If you publish work, check the rules of the publication or platform, and be honest about how the piece was made.

How do I stop AI suggestions from sounding generic?

Give the AI specifics: your setting, your characters, your topic, and the mood you want. The more detail you put in the prompt, the less generic the answer will be.

Can ChatGPT help me finish a story?

It can suggest ways forward, such as what a character might try next, but the choice should stay yours. Use its ideas as options to react to, not as orders to follow.

What if the AI rewrites my paragraph badly?

Ask it to change only one thing at a time, such as cutting the length or fixing the flow. Small, specific edits give better results than asking for a full rewrite.

Can I use these prompts for non-fiction?

Yes. The ideas, outline, and editing prompts work for articles and essays as well as stories. Swap the story details for your topic and adjust the tone to suit your readers.
