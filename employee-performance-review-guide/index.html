Employee Performance Review Guide

A performance review is a structured conversation between a manager and an employee about goals, progress and growth. This guide shows how to set goals that can actually be judged, how to give feedback that helps, how to run the meeting fairly, and what to do afterwards. It is general management information, not legal advice, and employment rules differ by place, so check local law for anything formal.

What this guide is and who needs it

A performance review is a planned meeting where a manager and an employee look at the work of the past period together: what went well, what did not, and what comes next. Done well, it aligns the employee's work with what the team needs, recognizes good work before it is taken for granted, and catches small problems while they are still small. Done badly, it feels like a trial, and the employee leaves knowing nothing except that they are unhappy. The difference is almost entirely in the preparation.

This guide is for managers of any size of team, for small business owners holding their first reviews, and for team leads who have been given the job without any training. It explains how to set goals, how to give feedback, how to run the meeting, and what to do afterwards, with a review template you can copy. It is not a disciplinary process, and it is not a pay negotiation, though pay decisions may follow from it. If you only remember one thing from this guide, remember this: nothing in a review should be a surprise to the employee.

How a review works, step by step

A good review starts months before the meeting, when the manager and the employee agree a small number of goals in writing. Through the period, the manager keeps short notes with dates and examples, good and bad, so the review is built on evidence instead of memory. About a week before the meeting, the employee writes a short self-review against the same goals, which shows how they see their own work and often surfaces problems the manager never saw.

The manager then prepares: the goals, the notes, and one or two examples for every point they plan to raise. The meeting itself takes place in private, without interruptions, and lasts about an hour. The employee speaks first about each goal, the manager adds their view, and they agree together what the result was. Then they agree the goals for the next period, any training or support needed, and a date to check progress. Everything agreed is written down, and both sides keep a copy. The follow-up matters as much as the meeting: if the agreed actions are forgotten, the next review starts from nothing.

If the two sides disagree about a result, the disagreement itself belongs in the record. The manager writes their view, the employee writes theirs, and both are kept, because a record that hides disagreement is not a fair record. Most disagreements come from unclear goals or missing notes, which is why the preparation matters so much. If a goal changed during the period, say so: judging someone against a goal that moved is unfair, and the review should judge the work that was actually asked for.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

A review costs time, not money. Preparation takes one to two hours per person if the notes were kept through the period, and much longer if they were not, because the manager must then reconstruct months of work from memory. The meeting itself takes forty-five minutes to an hour, and the write-up takes half an hour more. For a manager with a team of eight, that is about two working days a year, which is a small price for a team that knows where it stands.

Before the first review, gather the job description, the goals agreed last time, and any notes from the period. You need a private room, a template for the write-up, and a calendar that protects the meeting from being pushed aside. Most teams hold a formal review once or twice a year, with short check-ins in between. Never hold a review in the middle of a crisis or on the day of a deadline, because neither side will have the attention it needs.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The most common mistake is recency bias: judging the whole period by the last few weeks. A strong finish hides a weak start, and one bad week erases months of good work. Notes kept through the period are the only cure, because memory is not fair to anyone. The second mistake is vague feedback. Telling someone to do better, be more proactive, or improve their attitude gives them nothing to work with. Feedback needs a behaviour, an example with a date, and the effect it had, or it is only noise.

Other mistakes are just as easy to make. Comparing employees to each other turns a review into a ranking nobody asked for; judge each person against their own goals. Turning the meeting into a lecture wastes the one chance to hear the employee's side, so ask questions and wait for the answers. Linking everything to pay makes the employee hear only the money and forget the rest, so keep the pay conversation separate where you can. And never skip the written record: without it, both sides will remember the meeting differently, and the next review will start with an argument about what was agreed.

A review template you can copy

Use the same template for every review, so the team knows what to expect and the notes stay easy to compare. Copy the headings below into your own document and fill them in together at the meeting.

  • Employee name, role, and review period. The basics, so the record is clear a year later.
  • Goals agreed last time. Each goal, what actually happened, and a short note on why.
  • Strengths, with examples. What the employee did well, with a dated example for each point.
  • Areas to improve, with examples. What needs to change, with a dated example and the effect it had.
  • Training or support needed. Courses, mentoring, tools, or a change in workload.
  • Goals for the next period. A small number of clear goals, each with a result that can be seen and a date.
  • Employee comments. Space for the employee to write their own view, in their own words.
  • Signatures and date. Both sides sign to show they have read the record, not that they agree with every word.

Keep the completed template private and secure, and give the employee a copy. The template is a record of a conversation, not a scorecard, so write in full sentences and keep the tone respectful. If a goal was missed for reasons outside the employee's control, say so in the record, because the note is what the next reviewer will read.

A practical checklist you can follow

Work through this list for every review, starting a week before the meeting.

  • Goals for the period were agreed in writing at the start.
  • Notes with dates and examples were kept through the period.
  • The employee was asked for a short self-review a week ahead.
  • An example is ready for every point you plan to raise.
  • The meeting is booked in a private room with no interruptions.
  • The employee speaks first about each goal.
  • Actions, goals and training are written down at the meeting.
  • A follow-up date is set, and the employee gets a copy of the record.

If any item on the list is missing, fix it before the meeting rather than during it. A review held without notes turns into guesswork, and a review held without the employee's self-review turns into a lecture. The checklist exists so the meeting can be about the work, not about finding the paperwork.

Tips from real practice

Give feedback all year, not once a year. A short word after a good piece of work, and a quiet conversation soon after a problem, mean the review holds no surprises and the employee already knows where they stand. Praise in specific words: saying that a report was clear, on time and well argued means far more than saying good job. And separate the person from the problem: talk about what happened and what should happen next, not about what kind of person the employee is.

End every review with clear next steps, and say them out loud before you write them down, so both sides hear the same words. Keep the tone calm even when the news is hard, and give the employee time to respond rather than rushing past the difficult part. Finally, remember that the review is about the employee's future as much as their past: a good review leaves the employee knowing what to do next, what support they will get, and that their work is seen.

When goals change in the middle of a period, update them in writing at the time, not at the review. A goal that quietly changed is the most common cause of an unfair review, because the manager judges the new goal while the employee worked toward the old one. A short note, agreed by both sides, takes five minutes and saves the argument later. The same applies to the review notes themselves: keep them in one safe place, share them only with the people who need them, and never discuss one person's review with another member of the team.

This guide is general management information, not legal advice. Employment rules differ by place: reviews can affect pay, promotion and dismissal, so check local law before you put anything formal in writing. Never use a review to surprise an employee with a decision that is already made, and keep review notes private and secure.

Questions people ask

What is a performance review for?

A review aligns what the employee does with what the team needs. It recognizes good work, fixes problems early, and plans the next goals and the training to reach them. It is a conversation, not a verdict.

How should goals be set for a review?

Set a small number of clear goals at the start of the period, each with a result that can be seen and a date. Write them down and agree them together, so the review judges the work against goals both sides accepted.

How do I give difficult feedback kindly?

Describe the behaviour, not the person, and use a specific example with a date. Say what effect it had, ask what happened, and agree a fix together. Kind feedback is honest and specific, not soft and vague.

Should reviews use ratings or words?

Words carry the real meaning, and ratings on their own say almost nothing. If you use ratings, keep them simple and always add a sentence of explanation with an example, so the employee knows what the rating means.

What happens after a review?

Both sides should leave with written actions: the goals for the next period, any training or support, and a date to check progress. The manager follows up on those actions, and the notes are kept private and secure.

How often should reviews happen?

Once or twice a year is common for a formal review, with short check-ins in between. The formal meeting works best when feedback has been steady all year, so nothing in it is a surprise.
