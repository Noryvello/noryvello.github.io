Photography Composition Rules Guide

Composition is where you place things inside the frame, and it is the difference between a snapshot and a photograph that holds the eye. These eight rules give you a starting point for every shot, and the exercises help you practise them one at a time.

Two people can stand in the same place with the same camera and come home with very different photographs. The difference is rarely the camera — it is composition, the choice of what goes where inside the frame. A good composition guides the eye to the subject, keeps out distractions, and makes the picture feel balanced.

This guide covers eight rules that photographers have used for generations: the rule of thirds, leading lines, framing, and five more. Each rule is simple to understand and can be practised with any camera, including a phone. Use the picker below to study one rule at a time, with a practice exercise for each.

Rules are a starting point, not a law — once a rule feels natural, try breaking it on purpose and see what happens.

What composition is

Composition is the arrangement of everything inside your frame: the subject, the background, the empty space and the edges. When you press the shutter you are making dozens of small choices at once — where to stand, what to include, what to leave out, whether the horizon is level. The rules in this guide give those choices a structure, so you are deciding on purpose instead of by accident.

The eye is drawn to certain things first: faces, bright areas, sharp details and strong lines. A good composition puts those eye-catching elements where they help the picture, and keeps them away from the edges, where they pull the eye out of the frame. Before you take a shot, take a second to scan the whole frame, corners included, and ask whether everything in it earns its place.

You do not need special equipment to practise composition. A phone camera is ideal, because it is always with you and its screen shows you exactly what the picture will look like. Turn on the grid in your camera settings — it makes the rule of thirds and level horizons much easier to see.

The eight rules

Rule of thirds. Divide the frame into nine equal parts with two horizontal and two vertical lines. Place important elements on the lines, or where they cross, rather than in the centre. The four crossing points are natural resting places for the eye, and putting your subject on one of them makes the picture feel balanced without being stiff.

Leading lines. Roads, rivers, fences, paths and shadows can all act as lines that pull the viewer's eye through the picture towards the subject. A line that starts near a corner and runs diagonally towards the subject feels more dynamic than a straight one, and a line that leads nowhere can leave the viewer feeling lost, so give your lines a destination.

Framing. Use a doorway, arch, window or overhanging branches to create a frame inside your frame. Framing adds depth, hides messy edges and tells the viewer exactly where to look. A darker frame around a brighter subject works especially well.

Symmetry. When both halves of a picture mirror each other, the result feels calm and formal. Reflections in lakes and puddles, building fronts and straight roads are the easiest sources of symmetry. A perfectly symmetrical shot is satisfying, but one small difference — a single person, a different window — can make it more interesting.

Negative space. Empty space is not wasted space. A large area of sky, water or plain wall around a small subject makes the subject feel important and gives the picture room to breathe. Negative space works best when the empty area is genuinely clean, so watch for stray objects at the edges.

Depth and layers. A photograph is flat, but you can make it feel three-dimensional by including something in the foreground, something in the middle distance and something in the background. A rock or a fence in the foreground, a person in the middle and hills behind will always feel deeper than the same scene shot with an empty foreground.

Patterns and repetition. Rows of windows, tiles, seats or trees repeating across the frame are pleasing to the eye on their own. Fill the whole frame with the pattern, and if one element breaks the pattern, that break becomes a strong focal point — the one red door in a row of blue ones, for example.

Fill the frame. Move closer until the subject fills the frame and everything that does not belong is gone. This works best for faces, food, flowers and details. Most beginners stand too far away; one step closer than feels natural is usually the right distance.

Practice exercises

The best way to learn composition is to practise one rule at a time. Pick a rule, spend a short walk using only that rule, and look at your photos afterwards to see which ones worked. The picker below gives a practice exercise for each rule, and a few more ideas are listed here.

  • Take the same subject five ways: centred, on a third, framed, with leading lines and filling the frame. Compare the five and decide which one you like best — and why.
  • Photograph your street using only leading lines for a whole week. You will start noticing lines everywhere, which is the point.
  • Limit yourself to ten photos on an outing instead of a hundred. Slowing down forces you to compose each shot properly.
  • Look at photographs you admire and work out which rules they use. Most strong photos use two or three rules at once.

When to break the rules

Every rule in this guide can be broken well. A centred portrait can feel powerful and direct. A tilted horizon can add energy to a sports shot. A subject placed at the very edge of the frame can create tension. The difference between a mistake and a creative choice is whether you did it on purpose.

That is why the rules are worth learning first: once you know what a balanced picture looks like, you can see what an unbalanced one is doing and decide whether it works. Break one rule at a time, keep the rest, and check the result on your screen before you move on. If the picture feels stronger broken, keep it broken.

Questions people ask

What is the rule of thirds?

The rule of thirds divides the frame into nine equal parts with two horizontal and two vertical lines. Placing your subject on one of the lines, or where two lines cross, usually makes a stronger picture than placing it dead centre.

Do I need a special camera to practise composition?

No. A phone camera is ideal for practising composition, because it is always with you and its screen shows you exactly what the picture will look like. Turn on the grid in your camera settings to help.

What are leading lines?

Leading lines are lines in the scene — a road, a river, a fence, a shadow — that run through the frame and pull the viewer's eye towards the subject.

How do I practise composition?

Practise one rule at a time. Spend a short walk using only that rule, then look at your photos afterwards and see which ones worked. The picker on this page gives an exercise for each rule.

Should I crop my photos after taking them?

Cropping is a useful second chance: you can straighten a horizon, remove a distraction at the edge, or move the subject onto a third. It is better to compose well when you shoot, but a careful crop can save a good photo.
