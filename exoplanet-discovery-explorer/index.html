Exoplanet Discovery Explorer

An exoplanet is a planet that orbits a star other than our Sun. Thousands have been confirmed, and they range from gas giants bigger than Jupiter to rocky worlds close to the size of Earth. This guide explains what exoplanets are, how astronomers find them, and which discoveries matter most.

How to use this guide

This guide explains exoplanets in plain words: what they are, how they are found, and which ones have changed what we know about the universe. You do not need a science background to read it, and you do not need to know any mathematics. Each section takes one idea, from the first discovery to the search for worlds like Earth, and explains it with everyday comparisons.

If you are new to the subject, read the sections in order, because each one builds on the last. If you already know what an exoplanet is, skip to the sections on the famous discoveries and the habitable zone, which are where the most exciting ideas live. The questions at the end answer the things people ask most often, and the honest limits box near the end says plainly what this page cannot do.

One honest point first: everything on this page comes from public knowledge about discoveries that have already been announced. This page cannot show you a live count of exoplanets or the latest discovery, because the number grows all the time and new finds are announced every year. When you want the latest number, the official NASA exoplanet archive is the place to check, and this guide tells you how to think about what you find there.

What an exoplanet is

An exoplanet is a planet that orbits a star other than our Sun. The word comes from outside and planet, and it covers every kind of world beyond our Solar System: gas giants bigger than Jupiter, rocky planets close to the size of Earth, frozen worlds far from their stars, and hot worlds so close to their stars that a year lasts only a few days. Our Solar System has eight planets, but it is only one of countless star systems in the galaxy.

For most of human history, exoplanets were only a guess. Philosophers wondered whether other stars had planets, but no one could see them, because a planet is tiny and dim next to its star. A star outshines its planets by a factor of billions, so spotting a planet next to its star is like spotting a firefly next to a lighthouse from kilometres away. That is why the first confirmed exoplanets were only found in the 1990s, even though telescopes had existed for centuries.

The first exoplanets ever confirmed were found in 1992, orbiting a dead star called a pulsar, by astronomers Aleksander Wolszczan and Dale Frail. Three years later, in 1995, Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz found the first exoplanet around a star like our Sun, a planet called 51 Pegasi b. That discovery won them the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2019, and it opened the field: within a few decades, thousands of exoplanets had been confirmed.

How exoplanets are found

Astronomers find exoplanets with three main methods, and each one suits different kinds of planet. The transit method watches a star's brightness and looks for a tiny, regular dip, which happens when a planet crosses in front of the star and blocks a small part of its light. The dip is small, often less than one percent, but it repeats with every orbit, which is what gives it away.

The radial velocity method watches the star itself. A planet's gravity tugs on its star, so the star wobbles slightly as the planet orbits it. That wobble changes the colour of the star's light in a regular pattern, shifting it slightly towards blue and then towards red, and sensitive instruments can measure that shift. This method found the first exoplanet around a Sun-like star, and it is still used to weigh planets found by other methods.

The third method is direct imaging, which means taking a photograph of the planet itself. This is the hardest method, because the star's glare drowns out the planet's faint light, and it only works for large planets far from their stars. A fourth method, called microlensing, uses the way a star's gravity bends light to reveal a planet passing in front of a distant star. Each method finds different kinds of worlds, which is why astronomers use them all.

The transit method in detail

The transit method is the workhorse of exoplanet hunting, and it is beautifully simple in idea. Imagine watching a distant lighthouse and noticing that its light dims very slightly, on a regular schedule. If the dimming repeats exactly, something is passing in front of the light, and if that something is the right size, it is a planet. The depth of the dimming tells astronomers how big the planet is compared to its star.

The Kepler space telescope, which flew from 2009 to 2018, used this method to find thousands of exoplanets. Kepler stared at one patch of sky, watching more than 150,000 stars at once, and recorded their brightness with extraordinary care. Whenever a star dimmed in the regular pattern of a transit, Kepler had found a candidate, and follow-up observations confirmed whether it was really a planet. The TESS telescope, launched in 2018, now carries on the same work across the whole sky.

The transit method has a limit that is worth knowing: it only finds planets whose orbits happen to line up with our view from Earth. If a planet's orbit is tilted so that it never crosses in front of its star as seen from here, the transit method will never see it. That means the exoplanets we have found are only a small fraction of the planets that are really out there, and the true number is far larger.

The famous ones

Some exoplanets are famous because they were first. The planet 51 Pegasi b, found in 1995, was the first around a star like our Sun, and it surprised everyone: it is a gas giant half the size of Jupiter, but it orbits its star in only four days, far closer than Mercury orbits our Sun. Nobody had expected a giant planet to live so close to its star, and its discovery forced astronomers to rethink how planets form and move.

Other exoplanets are famous because they are near. Proxima Centauri b, announced in 2016, orbits Proxima Centauri, the closest star to our Sun at about 4.2 light years away. It is a rocky planet roughly the size of Earth, sitting in the habitable zone of its star, which makes it the closest known world that might, in theory, have liquid water. It is also the target of the most serious thinking about interstellar travel, even though reaching it is far beyond today's rockets.

The TRAPPIST-1 system, announced in 2017, is famous because it is crowded: seven rocky planets, all close to the size of Earth, orbiting a small, cool star. Three of them sit in the habitable zone. The system is a favourite for study because seven similar worlds around one star let astronomers compare them, and because their transits are easy to watch. If any system will teach us how common Earth-like worlds are, it may be this one.

The habitable zone

The habitable zone is the region around a star where a planet could have liquid water on its surface, if it has the right atmosphere. It is sometimes called the Goldilocks zone, because it is not too hot and not too cold: too close to the star and any water boils away, too far and it freezes solid. The zone is closer in for small, dim stars and further out for big, bright ones.

Being in the habitable zone does not mean a planet is habitable. Venus is often counted as being near the inner edge of our Sun's habitable zone, and its surface is hot enough to melt lead, because its thick atmosphere traps heat. Mars sits near the outer edge, and its water is frozen or buried. The zone is a starting point for the search, not a guarantee of anything.

What the habitable zone really means is a place worth looking. When astronomers find a rocky planet in the habitable zone of its star, they mark it as interesting, and they try to learn more about its atmosphere. The next generation of telescopes will study the air of such worlds, looking for water vapour and for the gases that life might produce. A planet in the habitable zone with the right atmosphere would be one of the great discoveries in the history of science.

Could we ever live on an exoplanet?

Not with today's technology, and not soon. The nearest exoplanet, Proxima Centauri b, is about 4.2 light years away, which is roughly 40 trillion kilometres. The fastest spacecraft humans have ever built would take tens of thousands of years to get there. A journey to another star is a problem for future generations, not for ours.

Even if we could get there, living on an exoplanet would be a second challenge. We know almost nothing about the surfaces, the air or the weather of any exoplanet. A planet the size of Earth in the habitable zone might have crushing air pressure, or no air at all, or storms that never stop. The only honest answer to whether we could live there is that we do not know yet, and we will not know until we can study these worlds far more closely.

That does not make the search pointless. Studying exoplanets teaches us about our own planet: how common Earth-like worlds are, how atmospheres form, and how rare or common the conditions for life might be. Every exoplanet we find is a data point in the biggest question in science, which is whether life exists anywhere else. The answer may come not from visiting another world, but from reading the light of its atmosphere through a telescope.

Common mistakes about exoplanets

The most common mistake is thinking that every exoplanet is Earth-like. Most confirmed exoplanets are gas giants, or rocky worlds far hotter or colder than Earth, because those are the easiest to find. An Earth-sized planet is a promising find, but size alone says nothing about air, water or temperature, and most Earth-sized worlds we know of would be terrible places to live.

The second mistake is trusting a discovery from a random video or social media post. Exoplanet news attracts dramatic headlines, and some of them promise far more than the science supports. A planet described as a second Earth is usually just a rocky world in the habitable zone, which is interesting but not the same thing. Check any dramatic claim against the official NASA exoplanet archive or a reputable science news source before believing it.

The third mistake is thinking that the number of confirmed exoplanets is the number of planets that exist. The thousands confirmed so far are only the ones our methods can find, and our methods miss most planets: worlds whose orbits never cross their star as seen from Earth, worlds too small to tug their star measurably, and worlds around stars too faint to study. The true number of planets in our galaxy is far larger than the number we have confirmed, and it may be that most stars have planets of their own.

This page teaches public knowledge about confirmed exoplanet discoveries. It does not show a live count of exoplanets, and it never presents an unconfirmed claim as a discovery. For the latest confirmed total, check the official NASA exoplanet archive, and treat dramatic claims about new worlds from unofficial pages with caution.

Questions people ask

What is an exoplanet?

An exoplanet is a planet that orbits a star other than our Sun. They range from gas giants bigger than Jupiter to rocky worlds close to the size of Earth. Thousands have been confirmed since the first ones were found in the 1990s, and they have shown that planets are common around other stars, not a rare accident of our own Solar System.

How are exoplanets found?

Astronomers find them mainly with three methods: the transit method, which watches for a tiny, regular dip in a star's brightness as a planet crosses in front of it; the radial velocity method, which measures the star's slight wobble from the planet's gravity; and direct imaging, which photographs the planet itself. A fourth method, microlensing, uses the way a star's gravity bends light. Each method finds different kinds of worlds.

How many exoplanets are there?

Thousands have been confirmed, and the number grows all the time as new discoveries are announced. The true number of planets in our galaxy is far larger than the confirmed count, because our methods can only find a fraction of them. For the latest confirmed total, check the official NASA exoplanet archive, which is the authoritative list.
