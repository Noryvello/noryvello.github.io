Moon Phases Explained

The Moon changes shape in the sky because we see different amounts of its sunlit half as it orbits the Earth. The Moon itself does not change: half of it is always lit by the Sun, and the phase we see depends on where the Moon sits in its orbit around us.

Some nights the Moon is a thin crescent, some nights it is a blazing full disc, and some nights it is nowhere to be seen at all. It looks as if the Moon is growing and shrinking, but the Moon itself never changes size. What changes is how much of its sunlit side faces us.

The Moon shines by reflected sunlight, and the Sun always lights exactly half of it. As the Moon travels around the Earth, we see that lit half from different angles, and the changing view is what we call the phases. The full cycle takes about twenty-nine and a half days, which is why the word month comes from the Moon.

Why the Moon seems to change shape

The Moon does not produce its own visible light. It shines because sunlight falls on it and reflects back to us, the way a mirror catches a lamp. The Sun lights one half of the Moon at all times, and the other half is dark. Which part of the lit half we can see depends on where the Moon is in its orbit around the Earth.

When the Moon sits between the Earth and the Sun, the lit half faces away from us and we see the dark side: that is the new Moon, and it is invisible against the daytime sky. As the Moon moves around its orbit, more of the lit half turns towards us, and the crescent grows. When the Earth sits between the Sun and the Moon, the whole lit half faces us, and we see a full Moon. Then the lit part shrinks again as the Moon heads back towards the new Moon position.

The whole cycle takes about twenty-nine and a half days. That is the length of a lunar month, and it is the reason our calendar months are roughly thirty days long. The Moon's orbit is the oldest clock in the sky.

The eight phases in order

The cycle is usually divided into eight phases. It begins with the new Moon, when the Moon is dark and hidden. Then comes the waxing crescent, a thin sliver of light that grows each night. Waxing means growing, and it describes the first half of the cycle, when the lit part gets bigger night by night.

Next is the first quarter, when half the Moon is lit. The name confuses people, because the Moon looks half lit, not a quarter lit. Quarter refers to the cycle: the Moon is a quarter of the way around its orbit. After that comes the waxing gibbous, when more than half is lit and the Moon is swelling towards full. Gibbous is an old word for bulging or humped, which describes the shape well.

The full Moon is the middle of the cycle, with the whole face lit. Then the process runs in reverse: the waning gibbous shrinks, the last quarter shows half the Moon lit again, and the waning crescent dwindles to a sliver before the next new Moon. Waning means shrinking, and it describes the second half of the cycle.

In the northern hemisphere, the lit part grows from the right and shrinks from the right. In the southern hemisphere, the picture is flipped: the lit part grows from the left. The phases themselves are the same everywhere on Earth, but the Moon looks upside down from the other side of the equator.

When to see each phase

Each phase keeps its own hours, and once you know them you can find the Moon without looking it up. The new Moon rises and sets with the Sun, so it is up during the day and invisible against the bright sky. The waxing crescent appears low in the west just after sunset and sets early in the evening.

The first quarter Moon is up in the afternoon, is high in the sky at sunset, and sets around midnight. The full Moon rises around sunset, crosses the sky all night, and sets around sunrise, which is why a full Moon night feels so bright. The last quarter Moon rises around midnight and is still visible in the morning sky. The waning crescent is a dawn Moon, low in the east just before sunrise.

That timetable is why the Moon is sometimes visible during the day. The first quarter Moon is already up in the afternoon, and the last quarter Moon lingers into the morning. A Moon in the daytime sky is not unusual at all: it is simply a phase whose hours overlap with daylight.

Why the Moon looks different near the horizon

A Moon low in the sky often looks bigger and more orange than the same Moon high overhead. The size is an illusion: the Moon is the same size all night, but our brains judge it against trees and buildings near the horizon and imagine it larger. You can test this by holding a coin at arm's length next to the Moon when it is low and again when it is high. The Moon covers the same amount of the coin both times.

The colour is real. When the Moon is low, its light travels through much more air to reach us, and the air scatters the blue part of the light away, leaving the warm orange and red tones. It is the same effect that makes sunsets red, and it is strongest when the air is dusty or hazy.

The Moon also always shows us the same face. It spins on its axis in the same time it takes to orbit the Earth, so the same side always points towards us. The far side of the Moon is not permanently dark: it gets just as much sunlight as the side we see. It is simply the side we never see from Earth.

Questions people ask

Why does the Moon change shape?

The Moon does not change shape. Half of it is always lit by the Sun, and we see different amounts of the lit half as the Moon orbits the Earth, which creates the phases.

How long is a full moon cycle?

One full cycle of phases takes about twenty-nine and a half days, which is the length of a lunar month and the reason our calendar months are roughly thirty days long.

What does waxing and waning mean?

Waxing means the lit part is growing, which happens in the first half of the cycle from new Moon to full Moon. Waning means the lit part is shrinking, in the second half from full Moon back to new Moon.

Why is the first quarter Moon half lit?

Quarter refers to the cycle, not the amount lit. At first quarter the Moon is a quarter of the way around its orbit, and we see half of its lit side.

Can the Moon be visible during the day?

Yes. The first quarter Moon is up in the afternoon, and the last quarter Moon lingers into the morning. A daytime Moon is simply a phase whose hours overlap with daylight.
