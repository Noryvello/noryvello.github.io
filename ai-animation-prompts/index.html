AI Animation Prompts: How to Write Prompts for Cartoons and Animated Clips

By the Noryvello team · Published 20 November 2026 · Updated 20 November 2026

Animation prompts work differently from realistic video prompts: the style words set the look, the character description must repeat in every shot, and the motion words are cartoon ones like bounce and waddle. This guide explains how to build those prompts, keep a character steady across clips, and tell a short story without words. Every pattern here uses characters you invent yourself.

A realistic video prompt asks the generator to copy the world: real light, real skin, real physics. An animation prompt asks it to copy a drawing instead, which is a different job with different rules. The look comes from style words, not from camera words. The motion comes from cartoon physics, where a character can squash flat and bounce back. And the character has to be described so plainly that the generator can draw the same one again in the next shot.

That last part is the hard part, and it is what this guide spends the most time on. Anyone can get one charming cartoon clip out of a generator. Getting the same charming character in five clips in a row takes a method: a short character description, repeated word for word, and a style description that never changes. The patterns in this guide are built that way, and every one of them uses an original character you invent yourself.

Create your own characters and styles. Do not ask for a copy of a famous character or a famous film's look: using someone else's character can break the tool's terms of use and can cause copyright problems. Check the tool's terms of use before you share or sell anything you make, and remember that buttons and menus inside the tools change over time.

What makes animation prompts different from realistic video prompts

Realistic prompts lean on the camera: lens words, light words, and framing words that tell the generator how a photograph would look. Animation prompts lean on the drawing: the style, the outlines, the colours, and the way things move. A realistic prompt might say golden hour light through a window with a shallow depth of field. An animation prompt says flat colours, thick outlines, and a soft paper texture. The two prompts are doing the same job — fixing the look — but they use different words to do it.

The motion is different too. Realistic motion follows physics: people walk, water falls, hair blows in the wind. Cartoon motion follows feeling: a happy character bounces, a sleepy character droops, a surprised character stretches tall and then snaps back. The generator knows these cartoon moves from the animated films and clips it has seen, so naming them plainly — bounce, waddle, float, stretch — works better than describing them in long sentences.

The third difference is forgiveness. A realistic face that is slightly wrong looks unsettling, because we know exactly what a real face should look like. A cartoon face that is slightly different from the last shot is easier to accept, because cartoons are already simplified. That forgiveness makes animation a kinder first project than realism, but it is not unlimited: the character still has to be recognisably the same one, which is why the character description matters so much.

Style words that set the look

The style words go near the start of the prompt, and they decide the whole look of the clip. Pick one style per project and use the same words in every prompt, so the generator does not drift from one look to another between shots.

  • 3D cartoon. Rounded, smooth, toy-like characters with soft shadows and bright colours. Say: 3D cartoon style, smooth rounded shapes, soft studio light, bright cheerful colours.
  • Anime. Clean lines, large expressive eyes, detailed hair, and vivid colour. Say: anime style, clean lines, large expressive eyes, vivid colours.
  • Stop-motion clay. Handmade figures with visible fingerprints and a slightly rough surface. Say: stop-motion clay style, handmade clay figures, soft warm light, gentle handmade texture.
  • Paper cut-out. Flat shapes with paper edges and a craft feel. Say: paper cut-out style, flat coloured paper shapes, soft shadows, simple craft look.
  • Flat vector. Bold flat colours, simple shapes, no shading. Say: flat vector style, bold flat colours, simple clean shapes, minimal background.
  • Watercolour. Soft bleeding colours and a painted feel. Say: watercolour style, soft painted colours, gentle paper texture, dreamy light.

Two habits make style words work harder. First, name the style and one texture word together — the texture is what keeps the look from drifting. Second, never mix two styles in one prompt: a 3D cartoon with watercolour shading is two looks fighting, and the generator will usually pick one and ignore the other, or blend them into something muddy.

Character prompts: same character, different shots

A character prompt is a short description you repeat in every shot, word for word. It has four parts: what the character is, what it looks like, what it wears, and one thing that makes it recognisable. A good one reads like this: a small round orange cat with big green eyes, a tiny blue scarf, and a white patch on its chest. That is one sentence, and it is enough for the generator to draw the same cat again.

The recognisable detail is the part people skip, and it is the part that matters most. Big green eyes, a tiny blue scarf, a white patch — these are the anchors the generator holds on to when everything else shifts. Without an anchor, the cat in shot three is a different cat from the cat in shot one, even if the style words are the same. With an anchor, the viewer reads the two cats as one character, even if the pose and the light have changed.

Keep the description short and concrete. Five or six describing words are plenty; ten or twelve start to fight each other, and the generator drops some of them at random. Write the description once, save it, and paste the exact same words into every prompt. If you want to change the character, change the description everywhere at once, and generate the shots again from the new version.

Motion words for cartoons

Cartoon motion is bigger and simpler than real motion, and the words for it are short and plain. The generator has seen these moves in animated clips, so naming them directly works better than describing the physics behind them.

  • Bounce. The character hops with energy, landing soft and springing up again. Good for joy, excitement, and greetings.
  • Squash and stretch. The character squashes flat and then stretches tall, like a rubber toy. Good for surprise, landings, and comic emphasis.
  • Waddle. The character walks with a side-to-side rock, like a duck or a penguin. Good for small round characters and gentle humour.
  • Float. The character drifts slowly through the air, weightless and calm. Good for dreams, magic, and quiet moments.
  • Spin. The character turns right around, fast or slow. Good for showing off, dancing, and celebrations.
  • Sneak. The character creeps forward slowly on tiptoe, hunched and careful. Good for mischief and suspense.
  • Wave. The character raises an arm and waves, big and friendly. Good for hellos, goodbyes, and invitations.

Use one motion word per shot, and pair it with a speed word: bounce happily, waddle slowly, float gently. One motion done clearly reads as a cartoon. Three motions in one shot read as a mess, because the generator cannot give each one enough time.

Telling a story without words

Short animated clips work best without dialogue, because the generator cannot animate speech reliably yet: mouths move oddly, and the words rarely match the lips. A story told in pictures — a cat wakes up, stretches, finds a sunny spot, and falls asleep again — needs no words at all, and it is much easier to generate cleanly.

The structure of a wordless story is simple: a character wants something, tries something, and gets it or does not. The cat wants the sunny spot on the rug, the sunny spot is taken by a sunbeam that keeps moving, the cat follows it, and finally curls up in the light. That is four beats, each one a single clear action, and each beat becomes one shot. The viewer follows the want, not the words, and the story lands without a single spoken line.

Facial expressions do the work that dialogue would do. A happy bounce, a drooping tail, a surprised stretch — these are the punctuation of a wordless story, and they are easier for the generator than speech. If you need words, put them on the screen as short text after the clips are made, or add a voiceover over the finished video, instead of asking the generator to animate talking.

8 ready animation prompt patterns

Each pattern below is a complete prompt you can copy, adapt, and use as a starting point. The character descriptions are written to be repeated word for word in every shot of a project. Replace the character with your own invention, and keep the structure: style, character, motion, camera, mood.

  1. A cat's morning. 3D cartoon style, smooth rounded shapes, soft morning light. A small round orange cat with big green eyes, a tiny blue scarf, and a white patch on its chest stretches slowly after a nap, then yawns. Camera still. Mood warm and sleepy.
  2. A robot learning to dance. Flat vector style, bold flat colours, simple clean shapes. A small boxy silver robot with round yellow eyes and a red antenna spins slowly, then bounces in time. Camera still. Mood cheerful and playful.
  3. A penguin's first snow. Stop-motion clay style, handmade clay figures, soft winter light. A small round penguin with a tiny red hat and a yellow beak waddles through soft snow and looks up in wonder. Camera pans slowly to the right. Mood gentle and magical.
  4. A fox in the forest. Watercolour style, soft painted colours, gentle paper texture. A slim red fox with a white-tipped tail and pointed ears trots softly between tall trees in morning mist. Camera follows slowly. Mood quiet and dreamy.
  5. A frog on a lily pad. Anime style, clean lines, large expressive eyes, vivid colours. A small bright green frog with big golden eyes sits on a lily pad and blinks slowly, then hops to the next pad. Camera still. Mood fresh and calm.
  6. A baker's busy morning. Paper cut-out style, flat coloured paper shapes, soft shadows. A round cheerful baker with a tall white hat and a blue apron kneads dough, then slides a tray into the oven. Camera still. Mood warm and busy.
  7. A dragon learning to fly. 3D cartoon style, smooth rounded shapes, soft sunset light. A small teal dragon with tiny wings and a friendly smile flaps hard, lifts off the ground, and floats gently. Camera tilts up slowly. Mood hopeful and joyful.
  8. A hedgehog's autumn walk. Stop-motion clay style, handmade clay figures, soft golden light. A small brown hedgehog with a tiny leaf stuck to its back waddles along a path covered in fallen leaves. Camera pans slowly to the left. Mood cosy and quiet.

Notice the shape every pattern shares: style first, then the character description, then one motion, then the camera, then the mood. That shape is the whole technique, and it works for any character you invent.

Keeping a character consistent across clips

Consistency is a habit, not a single trick, and it has four parts. First, write the character description once and never change it: the same words, in the same order, in every prompt. Second, write the style description once and never change it either. Third, generate the shots in order and check each one against the first before you move on. Fourth, when a shot drifts — the scarf changes colour, the eyes change size — regenerate it from the same prompt before you change any words, because the drift is often random and a fresh generation may fix it.

A reference image helps a great deal, if your tool accepts one. Generate the character once as a still image you like, then use that image as the starting point for the video clips. The generator holds the character steadier when it can see the character than when it only has words, and the still image becomes the anchor for the whole project.

Finally, keep the cast small. One character is easy to hold steady. Two characters are twice the work, because both descriptions must repeat and both faces must hold. Three or more characters in a first project is asking for trouble. Start with one character, finish the story, and add a second character only when the first one stays steady without effort.

Mistakes to avoid

Too much detail in one prompt. A prompt that describes the character, the setting, the weather, the time of day, three actions, and two camera moves gives the generator more to get wrong than to get right. Keep prompts to two or three sentences, one action, and one camera move, and let the cut between shots carry the story.

Copying a famous character. Asking for a copy of a famous character or a famous film's look is a mistake twice over: it can break the tool's terms of use and cause copyright problems, and it usually produces a poor copy anyway, because the generator is guessing at a design it is not supposed to reproduce. Invent your own character instead. An original cat with a blue scarf is yours to use, and it is more fun to draw than a copy.

Changing the style between shots. If shot one is 3D cartoon and shot two drifts toward watercolour, the video looks like two projects stitched together. Paste the same style words into every prompt, and check each clip against the first one before you keep it.

Asking for dialogue. Animated mouths and speech are still unreliable, and a talking character usually looks wrong. Tell the story in pictures, and add words as text on the screen or as a voiceover after the clips are made.

Questions people ask

Can AI make cartoons?

Yes, AI video tools can make cartoon clips from written prompts, using style words like 3D cartoon, anime, or stop-motion clay. Short clips with one character and one motion work best, and the story is usually told in pictures rather than dialogue.

What words give a 3D cartoon look?

Say 3D cartoon style, smooth rounded shapes, soft studio light, and bright cheerful colours, and use the same words in every prompt. The style words go near the start of the prompt, before the character description.

How do I keep the same character in every clip?

Write the character description once — what it is, what it looks like, what it wears, and one recognisable detail — and repeat it word for word in every prompt. Check each clip against the first one, and regenerate any shot that drifts before you change the words.

Can AI animate dialogue?

Not reliably yet. Animated mouths rarely match spoken words, so short clips work better as wordless stories told in pictures. Add any words as text on the screen or as a voiceover after the clips are made.

What is the best length for an animated clip?

Short clips of a few seconds each work best. One action per clip, joined with clean cuts, gives you a finished video that holds together, while long clips give the generator more time to drift.

Can I use a famous cartoon character?

No, you should not ask for a copy of a famous character or a famous film's look. It can break the tool's terms of use and cause copyright problems. Invent your own character instead, and check the tool's terms of use before you share or sell anything you make.
