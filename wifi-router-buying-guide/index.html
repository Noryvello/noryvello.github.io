Wi-Fi Router Buying Guide

A good Wi-Fi router is the one that covers your whole home at the speed you actually use. This guide explains coverage, speed, frequency bands, placement and security in plain words, so you can choose with confidence and set the router up well.

Most homes buy a router once and then live with it for years, so a little thought before buying pays off every day. The things that decide whether your Wi-Fi feels fast are simpler than the numbers on the box: how far the signal has to travel, how many walls it passes through, how many devices share it, and where the router sits.

This guide is general guidance. Actual speeds and coverage depend on your home, its walls, and your internet plan — a router cannot deliver more speed than the plan you pay for.

Coverage vs speed: what matters most

Speed is the number on the box. Coverage is whether the signal reaches the far bedroom. A router can be very fast next to itself and useless two rooms away, and no speed figure fixes that. For most homes, coverage is the thing to solve first: a steady signal everywhere beats a blazing-fast signal in one corner.

Think about what your household actually does. Browsing, messages and email need very little speed. Video calls need a steady connection more than a fast one. Streaming video needs a moderate, steady speed — far less than the headline numbers on router boxes. The router's top speed only matters if your internet plan can supply it, and in most homes the plan is the slower of the two.

So start with coverage. Walk through your home and note where you actually use Wi-Fi. A router that covers those rooms well is worth more than one with a bigger speed number that leaves the back bedroom in a dead zone.

Frequency bands in plain words

Most routers broadcast on two bands, 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz, and understanding the difference takes most of the mystery out of Wi-Fi.

The 2.4 GHz band reaches further and passes through walls and floors better, keeping a connection alive in the far room and the garden. Its trade-off is speed: it carries less data at once, and because it is a crowded band — neighbours' routers, Bluetooth gadgets and microwaves share it — it can slow down in busy areas.

The 5 GHz band is faster over short range. It carries more data, which suits video calls, streaming and large downloads in the same room as the router. Its trade-off is reach: the signal weakens faster with distance and struggles more with thick walls.

Many routers offer both bands under one network name and steer devices between them. A good rule of thumb: let nearby devices use 5 GHz and let distant or low-demand devices — a smart speaker, a printer, a sensor — use 2.4 GHz. Some newer routers also offer a 6 GHz band, faster still over short range but shorter in reach; it is a bonus, not a necessity.

How many devices does a home really run?

It is easy to underestimate how many devices share your Wi-Fi. Count them before you buy, and you will see why a router's ability to handle many connections matters as much as its speed.

A typical household might have two phones, a laptop or two, a tablet, a television, and a game console — that is already seven or eight. Add a smart speaker, a printer, a doorbell or a camera, and the devices of every person in the house, and twenty or more connected devices is normal, not unusual.

Most of those devices are quiet most of the time: a printer and a smart bulb use almost nothing. The load comes from the few devices doing heavy work at once — two video calls, a stream and a download, for example. A router that handles many simultaneous connections calmly will feel better than a faster one that stumbles when the household is busy.

Placement rules that matter more than the router

Where the router sits matters more than which router you buy. A well-placed modest router will outperform an expensive one hidden behind the television.

Put the router somewhere central, so the signal travels the shortest distance to every room. Raise it: a shelf or a table at waist height or above beats the floor. Keep it out in the open, not inside a cupboard or behind a fish tank, a mirror or a metal cabinet — water, glass and metal all weaken the signal.

Keep it away from thick obstacles. A router pressed against a concrete wall spends half its signal warming the wall. If the router must sit near the wall where the internet cable enters, use a longer cable to reach a better spot.

Avoid the kitchen if you can: microwaves and large metal appliances interfere with the 2.4 GHz band. Small moves — a metre to the left, a shelf higher — can change a dead zone into a working room, so experiment before you decide the router is the problem.

One router or a mesh system?

A single router suits a small flat or a home where one central spot reaches every room. If every room already gets a usable signal from one position, a mesh system adds cost and complexity for no benefit.

A mesh system is a set of two or three units that work as one network, passing the signal from unit to unit. It suits larger homes, homes with thick walls or several floors, and homes where one router leaves a room or a garden office with no signal. You place one unit near the internet connection and the others part-way toward the rooms that need coverage.

Before buying mesh, try the cheaper fixes first: move the router and raise it. If the gap is real and one router cannot close it, mesh is the cleanest answer — one network name, no manual switching as you walk around the house.

Security basics for a new router

Set up security the day the router arrives, not later. Three habits cover most of it.

First, change the default admin password. Every router ships with a well-known default password for its settings page, and anyone who knows the model can look it up. Pick a long, unique password and store it safely.

Second, set a strong Wi-Fi password and use the strongest security mode the router offers — WPA3 if available, otherwise WPA2. Avoid the older WEP mode entirely; it is trivially broken. Consider a separate guest network for visitors so they never see your main devices.

Third, keep the firmware updated. Firmware is the software inside the router, and updates fix security holes. Check for updates every few months, or switch on automatic updates if the router offers them.

Questions people ask

What matters more, coverage or speed?

Coverage, for most homes. A fast signal that does not reach the far bedroom is no use, while a steady signal everywhere covers browsing, calls and streaming. Sort out coverage first, then check that the speed meets what your household actually does.

Where should I place my router?

Somewhere central, raised off the floor, and out in the open — on a shelf or table, away from thick walls, metal, mirrors and water. Keep it away from the microwave and large appliances, and move it a little at a time to test each spot before you judge it.

Do I need a mesh system?

Only if one router leaves real dead zones. A small flat with a central router rarely needs mesh. A larger home, a home with thick walls or several floors, or a garden office with no signal are the cases where a mesh system earns its place.

How do I secure a new router?

Change the default admin password, set a strong Wi-Fi password with WPA3 or WPA2 security, and keep the firmware updated. Those three steps cover most home router security, and a guest network keeps visitors off your main devices.
