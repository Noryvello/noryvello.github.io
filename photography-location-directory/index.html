Photography Location Directory

This directory covers the kinds of places photographers love, from golden-hour viewpoints and city streets to coasts, forests and markets. For each location type you will find what to shoot there and practical tips for getting the best from it, with no fixed addresses, because the best spots are usually the ones near you.

This page lists location types, not specific places, and gives no addresses: the best photography spots are usually close to home. Always respect private land, local rules and the people around you, and check access, tides and weather before you go.

Golden-hour spots

The golden hour is the hour after sunrise and the hour before sunset, when the sun is low and the light turns warm and soft. Any open view works: a hilltop, a field edge, a rooftop, a lakeside path. What to shoot: long shadows, backlit trees and grasses, silhouettes of people and animals, and the warm glow on buildings and water. The light changes fast, so arrive early and work quickly.

Practical tips: face away from the sun for warm, even light on your subject, and face towards it for silhouettes and sunbursts. A lens hood helps with flare when you shoot into the light, and a tripod helps as the light fades. The best golden-hour spot is one you can reach often, because the same view looks different in every season. Visit your nearest hill or open field at sunset a few times and you will learn exactly where the sun sets and which angles work best.

City streets

City streets are full of photographs: shopfronts, crossings, bus stops, stairways, reflections in windows, and the endless variety of people going about their day. What to shoot: the rhythm of a busy pavement, a single figure against a plain wall, interesting light between buildings, and the small details most people walk past, such as a painted door, a market stall or a cat asleep on a step.

Practical tips: walk slowly and look up as well as ahead, because the best subjects are often above eye level. Early morning gives empty streets and long shadows; rain gives reflections that double every light. Be respectful with people: a smile and a nod go a long way, and if someone clearly does not want to be photographed, move on. Keep your camera ready rather than buried in a bag, because street moments last a second and then they are gone.

Coast and shoreline

The coast offers some of the most dramatic light anywhere: sunrise over the water, waves breaking on rocks, wet sand acting like a mirror, and the changing colours of the sea and sky. What to shoot: the line where water meets land, rock pools at low tide, fishing boats and harbours, seabirds, and long exposures that turn moving water into silk. The shoreline changes every day with the tide, so no two visits are the same.

Practical tips: check the tide times before you go, and never let the tide cut off your way back. Keep your camera away from spray and sand, and wipe the lens often. A tripod and a slow shutter speed turn waves into soft mist, while a fast shutter freezes spray in mid-air. Wear shoes with grip on wet rock, watch for slippery seaweed, and keep a safe distance from cliff edges and large waves. The sea is beautiful and it deserves respect.

Forest and woodland

Forests reward patience. What to shoot: light breaking through the canopy in shafts, ferns and fungi on the forest floor, the texture of bark, autumn colour, and the quiet geometry of straight trunks. Fog and mist turn an ordinary wood into something magical, and overcast days give soft, even light that suits woodland far better than harsh sun, which creates bright patches and deep shadows.

Practical tips: go early, when the light is low and the woods are quiet. Look for a path or a stream to lead the eye into the frame. A telephoto lens helps you pick out details, while a wide lens suits the scale of tall trees. Take care with exposure: a camera metering a bright sky through the trees will turn the forest floor black, so expose for the shadows or use a tripod and a longer exposure. Stay on the paths where they exist, and leave fungi and plants where you found them.

Markets and busy public places

Markets are a feast for a camera: colour, texture, movement and character in every direction. What to shoot: piles of fruit and vegetables, the hands of a vendor at work, steam rising from a food stall, the pattern of an awning, and the faces of people who have spent their lives at the same stall. Railway stations, fairs and festivals offer the same energy on a larger scale.

Practical tips: markets are private or semi-private spaces, so ask before photographing a stallholder up close, and buy something if you spend time at a stall. The best light is often early morning, when stalls are being set up and the light is soft. A short lens suits the close quarters of a market, and a fast shutter speed freezes the movement of the crowd. Keep your belongings secure in a crowd, and be ready for the moment: markets move fast, and the best shots are the ones you were ready for.

Mountains and open hills

Mountains give scale that no other landscape can match. What to shoot: ridges fading into blue distance, a path winding up a slope, a lone tree or building that shows the size of the land, cloud shadows moving across a valley, and the first or last light touching a summit. Open hills and moorland offer the same feeling on a smaller scale, with the added bonus of easier access.

Practical tips: mountain weather changes fast, so check the forecast, carry a waterproof layer, and tell someone where you are going. The light is best at the edges of the day, which means early starts and late finishes. A tripod is worth its weight for the low light of dawn and dusk. Use a person, a tree or a building in the frame to give a sense of scale, because mountains without a reference point can look surprisingly small in a photograph. Never climb beyond your ability for a photo.

Rivers, lakes and waterfalls

Water is one of the most photogenic subjects there is. What to shoot: reflections in a still lake at dawn, a river winding through a valley, a waterfall with a slow shutter speed turning the fall into silk, rain on a pond, and the patterns of light on moving water. Rivers and lakes change with the seasons: frozen in winter, green in spring, golden in autumn.

Practical tips: still water gives the best reflections, so go early before the wind picks up. For silky waterfalls, use a tripod and a shutter speed of half a second or longer, with the lowest ISO your camera allows. A polarising filter cuts reflections on the water surface and deepens the colour of the sky, which is useful on bright days. Be careful on wet banks and rocks, keep your distance from fast water, and never wade into a river you do not know.

Gardens, parks and architecture

Gardens and parks are the easiest places to practise photography, and they reward the effort all year round. What to shoot: flowers in close-up, the shape of a single tree through the seasons, a path curving into the distance, and the way light falls through leaves. Architecture offers a different kind of subject: lines, symmetry, repeating patterns, and the contrast between old and new buildings in the same frame.

Practical tips: in a garden, the best light is early morning, when flowers are fresh and the light is soft, and a close-up lens or a telephoto helps you pick out single blooms. For architecture, look for symmetry, shoot from a low angle to make a building feel tall, and wait for a person to walk into the frame to give it scale. Many gardens and buildings are private, so respect opening times and rules, and ask before using a tripod where it might block a path.

How to plan a shoot at any location

A little planning makes any outing more productive. Before you go, check the weather, the sunrise and sunset times, and how to get there and back. Think about what you want to photograph and pack only the gear you need: a camera, one or two lenses, a spare battery, a memory card, and water and a snack for yourself. A tripod is worth carrying if you plan to shoot in low light or use slow shutter speeds.

When you arrive, spend ten minutes just looking before you take a single photo. Walk around, notice where the light falls, and find the angles that interest you. Take a few test shots, check them on the screen, and then work the scene: try different heights, different lenses, and vertical as well as horizontal frames. The best photograph at any location is rarely the first one you take. Stay safe, respect the place and the people in it, and leave it exactly as you found it.

Questions people ask

What is the best time of day for photography?

The hour after sunrise and the hour before sunset, known as the golden hour, give warm, soft light that flatters almost any subject. Midday sun is harsh and creates strong shadows, while overcast days give soft, even light that suits forests, waterfalls and close-up work.

Do I need permission to photograph in a public place?

The rules differ by country, so check the law where you live. In many places, photography in public spaces is allowed, but private property, some venues and some events have their own rules. When you are unsure, ask. Being polite and respectful matters more than any rule.

What gear should I take on a first photo outing?

Keep it simple: your camera, one lens, a spare battery, a memory card, and water and a snack. A tripod is useful for low light and slow shutter speeds but is not essential for a first outing. The best gear is the gear you actually carry.

How do I find good photography spots near me?

Walk your local area at different times of day and in different weather, and notice where the light falls. Look for water, hills, old buildings, markets and parks. Ask local photographers and walking groups for ideas, and revisit the same places in different seasons.

What should I do if the weather turns bad?

Protect your camera from rain with a bag or a waterproof cover, and seek shelter if there is lightning or strong wind. Do not give up too soon: storm light can be dramatic, and overcast skies give soft light that suits many subjects. Some of the best photographs are taken just after the rain stops.

Can I photograph people I do not know?

It depends on where you are and what you are photographing, and the rules differ by country. As a matter of respect, be open about what you are doing, smile, and ask before taking a close portrait of a stranger. If someone asks you not to photograph them, respect that and move on.
