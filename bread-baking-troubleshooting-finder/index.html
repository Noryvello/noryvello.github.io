Bread Baking Troubleshooting Finder

Pick the problem with your loaf and this finder shows the likely causes and the fixes to try first. It covers twelve common bread problems, from a loaf that never rose to a crust that came out tough.

How to use this finder

Choose the problem that best matches your loaf, then press the button. You will see the most likely causes first, followed by the fixes to try on your next bake, in the order worth trying them. Most bread problems have more than one possible cause, so read the whole list and pick the one that fits what you remember about your bake. If your dough felt very wet, for example, the water amount is a better suspect than the yeast.

Keep notes about each bake if you can. Write down the flour you used, how long the dough rose, how warm the room was, and how the loaf looked when it came out. A short note takes a minute and makes the next bake much easier to diagnose. Bakers who change one thing at a time learn faster than bakers who change everything at once, because they can see exactly which change fixed the problem.

Why most bread problems happen

Almost every bread problem comes down to one of five things: flour, water, yeast, time, or temperature. Flour builds the structure. Water wakes the yeast and lets the gluten form. Yeast makes the gas that lifts the dough. Time lets flavour and structure develop. Temperature controls how fast all of it happens. When one of the five is off, the loaf tells you, and it usually tells you in a clear way.

Water is the one beginners misjudge most often. Too little water gives a stiff dough that cannot rise well, and the crumb comes out dry and tight. Too much water gives a slack dough that spreads instead of holding its shape, and the inside can stay gummy. The right amount depends on your flour, because different flours absorb different amounts of water. That is why recipes give a range and why experienced bakers judge the dough by feel as much as by the numbers.

Temperature matters just as much. Dough rises slowly in a cool room and fast in a warm one, and the same recipe can take twice as long on a cold day. Yeast is also sensitive to heat: water that feels hot to your hand is too hot for yeast and can kill it. Warm water, about the temperature of a comfortable bath, is the safe range for mixing dough.

Reading your loaf

The crumb, the inside of the bread, is the best record of what happened during the bake. A tight, even crumb with small holes usually means the dough was under-proofed or the gluten was under-developed. Large, uneven holes with a dense layer at the bottom often mean the dough was over-proofed and collapsed under its own weight. A damp line through the middle usually means the loaf was under-baked or cut while still hot, so the steam had nowhere to go.

The crust tells its own story. A pale crust usually means the oven was not hot enough or the loaf needed more time. A very dark crust with a pale bottom can mean the oven shelf was too high or the baking tray held too much heat. A crust that goes soft within an hour often means the loaf cooled in a closed space where steam softened it. Let bread cool on a rack with air all around it, and wait until it is fully cool before slicing, unless the recipe says otherwise.

This page is general baking guidance, not food-safety advice. Throw away any loaf that smells off or shows mould, and do not taste raw dough, because raw flour can carry harmful bacteria. If you are unsure whether a loaf is safe to eat, throw it away.

One change at a time

When a bake goes wrong, it is tempting to fix everything at once: more yeast, less water, longer rise, hotter oven. That usually makes the next loaf harder to read, because you cannot tell which change helped and which one made things worse. Pick the single most likely cause from the finder, change only that, and bake again. If the problem stays, move to the next cause on the list.

It also helps to use the same recipe while you are learning. Every recipe has its own balance of flour, water, yeast and salt, and switching recipes after every failure hides the pattern. Bake the same loaf three or four times, changing one thing each time, and you will build a feel for the dough that no recipe can teach. Bread baking rewards patience more than precision, and most failed loaves are still good eating, even when they are not pretty.

Questions people ask

Why did my bread not rise at all?

The most common reasons are yeast that is old or dead, water that was too hot and killed the yeast, or a room that was too cold for the dough to ferment. Check the yeast first by stirring a little into warm water with a pinch of sugar and waiting ten minutes for bubbles.

Can I fix a loaf that is already baked?

A baked loaf cannot be made to rise or change its crumb, but it can still be useful. Dense or dry bread makes good toast, breadcrumbs, croutons or bread pudding, so it rarely needs to go to waste.

Why is my bread dense even though it rose?

A loaf can rise and still bake up dense if the dough was under-kneaded, if it was knocked back too hard and lost its gas, or if the oven was not hot enough to give it a final spring. Gentle handling after the first rise and a fully preheated oven usually help.

How do I know if my yeast is still alive?

Stir the yeast into a little warm water with a pinch of sugar and leave it for about ten minutes. If it foams and bubbles, it is alive. If nothing happens, the yeast is dead and the dough will not rise.

Does the type of flour change the result?

Yes. Bread flour has more protein than plain flour, so it builds stronger gluten and gives a chewier, better-risen loaf. Plain flour still works, but the crumb is usually softer and the loaf rises less.
