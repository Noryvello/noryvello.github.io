How to Make AI Voiceovers for Videos

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

Making an AI voiceover means writing a script that sounds good spoken aloud, picking a voice that fits the video, generating the audio, and syncing it to your pictures. This guide walks through each step, including the emotion limits to expect.

A good voiceover can carry a video. It explains what the pictures show, sets the pace, and gives the audience a reason to keep watching. AI voice tools have made voiceovers available to anyone: no microphone, no quiet room, no recording session. But the tools only do half the work. The script, the voice choice, and the edit decide whether the result sounds professional or flat.

This guide covers the full process, from the first draft of the script to the finished video. It is written for beginners, and it is honest about the limits: AI voices are excellent narrators, but they are not actors.

AI voices imitate emotion but do not feel it, so a voiceover that needs genuine warmth, excitement, or drama will sound thinner than a human performance. Plan your scripts around that limit, and always listen to the full audio before publishing.

Start with the script, not the voice

Beginners often open a voice tool first and start typing. A better order is to write the script in a plain document before you touch any tool. The script is the foundation: if it reads well aloud, the voiceover will sound good; if it does not, no voice can save it.

Plan the script against your video. Know what each part of the video shows, and write the words that belong over those pictures. A voiceover that describes something the audience cannot see feels confusing, while one that simply repeats what is obvious feels pointless. The best voiceovers add what the pictures cannot say on their own.

Keep the script shorter than you think you need. Spoken words take longer than written words feel, and a crowded script forces the voice to rush. Leave breathing room: short sentences, clear ideas, and gaps where the pictures can speak for themselves.

Writing scripts for spoken delivery

Writing for the ear is different from writing for the eye. Read every sentence aloud as you write it. If you stumble, the voice will stumble too. Short sentences work better than long ones. Contractions such as "it is" shortened to "it's" sound natural, while formal phrasing sounds stiff when spoken.

Cut filler words and roundabout phrasing. "In this video, we are going to be taking a look at" can become "Here is how it works." Every unnecessary word costs the listener attention. Aim for sentences a friend could follow on the first hearing, because the audience only gets one hearing.

Use punctuation as stage directions. A comma creates a short pause, a full stop creates a longer one, and a new paragraph creates a clear break. If a sentence needs a breath in the middle, break it into two sentences. The voice follows your punctuation, so punctuate the way you want it to sound.

Picking the right voice

Most voice tools offer a list of ready-made voices, and choosing one is the most important decision in the process. Listen to several voices reading the same lines from your script, not the tool's sample sentences. A voice that sounds great on a sample may not suit your words.

Match the voice to the video's mood. A calm, steady voice suits tutorials and explainers. A warmer, more energetic voice suits stories and promotions. A voice that is too dramatic for a quiet topic will feel wrong, and a voice that is too flat for an exciting topic will feel dull. The right voice disappears into the video; the wrong one distracts from it.

Consider consistency too. If you make a series of videos, using the same voice across them builds familiarity. Switching voices between episodes of the same series can confuse the audience, so pick a voice you will be happy to live with.

Pacing, pauses, and emphasis

Pacing is what separates a good voiceover from a monotonous one. Most tools let you adjust the speed, and a small change makes a big difference. A voice that is slightly slower than default often sounds clearer and more confident, while a voice pushed too fast sounds anxious and hard to follow.

Pauses are just as important. A pause before an important point gives it weight. A pause after a surprising fact lets it land. You can create pauses by breaking the script into shorter sentences and paragraphs, or by using the pause controls if the tool offers them. Silence is part of the performance, not dead air.

Emphasis is the hardest part to control. If the voice stresses the wrong word, reword the sentence so the meaning is unmistakable. Moving the important word to the start or end of the sentence usually helps, because voices naturally stress those positions. When in doubt, simplify the sentence rather than fighting the voice.

Generating and reviewing the audio

Generate the audio in sections rather than all at once. Working paragraph by paragraph makes it easier to fix problems, because you can regenerate one section without redoing the whole script. It also makes syncing easier later, since you can place each section over the pictures it belongs to.

Listen to every section in full, with headphones if you can. Check for mispronounced words, wrong emphasis, odd pauses, and sentences that run together. Small problems that you notice will be noticed by your audience too, and fixing them now is cheap.

When a word is mispronounced, try spelling it the way it sounds. Many voice tools read phonetic spellings more reliably than the correct spelling of an unusual name. If a sentence keeps coming out wrong, rewrite the sentence. The voice is not going to change its mind; the text has to change.

Syncing the voiceover to video

Syncing means placing the voiceover over the right pictures at the right time. Import the audio into your video editor and lay it on the timeline under the video. Start by aligning the first sentence with the opening shot, then work through the video section by section.

If the voice runs ahead of the pictures, stretch the pictures: hold a shot a little longer, or add a short pause in the audio. If the voice lags behind, trim a pause or shorten a shot. Small adjustments like these are normal, and they are the reason generating in sections is so useful.

Check the whole video from start to finish before you export it. Watch it once without touching anything, the way your audience will. If a sentence lands over the wrong picture, or a pause feels too long, fix it now. A final full watch-through catches problems that checking sections one by one misses.

The honest limits: emotion and performance

AI voices are good narrators and poor actors. They can read a script clearly, vary their pace, and sound pleasant for a long passage. What they cannot do is feel the words. A line that needs genuine excitement, grief, or humour will come out flatter than a human would deliver it, because the voice is imitating the sound of emotion rather than experiencing it.

This matters most in stories, personal videos, and anything that depends on timing and feeling. If your video needs a performance, consider recording the key lines yourself and using the AI voice for the rest. A short human moment in the middle of a narrated video often lands harder than a fully generated one.

None of this is a reason to avoid AI voiceovers. For explainers, tutorials, demos, and updates, they are an excellent fit. The trick is to match the tool to the job: let the AI voice do the narrating, and keep the moments that need a real human for a real human.

Questions people ask

Should I write the script before choosing a voice?

Yes. Write the script first, then test voices by having them read lines from your script. A voice that sounds good on a sample sentence may not suit your actual words.

How do I fix a mispronounced word?

Try spelling the word the way it sounds, or rewrite the sentence so the word is easier to say. If a sentence keeps coming out wrong, change the text rather than regenerating the same words.

Can AI voices sound emotional?

They can imitate emotion, but the performance is thinner than a human one over a long passage. For narration and explainers this rarely matters, but for stories and personal videos it does.

Should I generate the whole script at once?

No. Generate in sections, paragraph by paragraph. It is easier to fix one section than to redo a whole script, and sections are easier to sync to the right pictures.

How do I sync a voiceover to video?

Import the audio into your video editor, align the first sentence with the opening shot, then adjust each section. Stretch shots or trim pauses to keep the words and pictures together.
