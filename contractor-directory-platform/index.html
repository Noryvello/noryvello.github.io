What this is

A contractor directory is a listing service that helps you find tradespeople — builders, plumbers, electricians, roofers, plasterers, decorators — in your area. Some directories are run by trade bodies, some by review websites, and some by local councils or community groups. They all do the same basic job: they put names in front of you when you need work done.

What no directory can do is vouch for the person listed. A listing tells you that a business exists and says what it does; it does not tell you whether the work is good, whether the price is fair, or whether the job will finish on time. That checking is your job, and this guide is about how to do it well.

This page lists no contractors and no company names, because a list on a web page cannot check anyone. Instead it explains how directories work, where the trustworthy ones live, the ten checks to run before hiring anyone, the questions that reveal a bad contractor, and the red flags that should send you walking away.

How contractor directories work

Most directories work the same way. A business creates a listing with its name, the trades it covers, the area it serves, and a phone number or contact form. Some directories add reviews from past customers; some add badges for trade-body membership or insurance; a few verify identity documents before a listing goes live. The directory earns its money from the businesses, from advertising, or from membership fees — not from you.

That business model matters, because it shapes what you see. A directory that charges businesses to appear will show you the businesses that pay, not necessarily the best ones. A directory that ranks by reviews will favour businesses with many reviews, which usually means large firms rather than the skilled one-person business down the road. Neither is dishonest — but neither is a recommendation.

The useful question is not 'is this directory good?' but 'what does this directory actually check?' A trade-body register checks qualifications and insurance before listing. A review site checks very little beyond the review itself. Knowing the difference tells you how much weight to give a listing, and how much checking is left for you to do.

Where to look

Start with the official places. Most countries have trade bodies for the main trades, and many keep a public register of members — search for the trade body for the trade you need, in your country or region, and use its register first. A member of a trade body has usually had qualifications and insurance checked, and the body can help if something goes wrong.

Next, ask people you trust who had similar work done. A neighbour who had a roof replaced last year can tell you things no directory can: did the crew turn up on time, did they clean up, did the price hold, would they hire the same firm again? One honest account from someone you know is worth a page of star ratings.

Review sites come third, and they need reading with care. Look for reviews that describe a real job in detail — what was done, how long it took, what it cost — and be wary of pages where every review is five stars with no text. Check the dates too: a business with twenty good reviews from three years ago and nothing since may have changed hands, staff or standards.

Local community groups and notice boards can help, especially for small jobs, but treat every recommendation as a lead to check, not a guarantee. The person recommending may be a friend of the contractor, or may have had one easy job done well. Run the ten checks below on every name, however it reached you.

The 10 checks before hiring

Run all ten checks before you agree to anything. They take an afternoon, and they are the difference between hiring a contractor and gambling on one.

  1. Identity: a real business name, a real address, and a phone number that someone answers. A business that exists only as a mobile number is hard to find later.
  2. Insurance: proof of public liability insurance, in date, covering the kind of work you need. Ask to see the certificate, not just a claim.
  3. Licences and registration: whatever your trade and region require — check with the official register or trade body, not with the contractor's own website.
  4. Qualifications: the certificates for the trade, where the trade has them. For gas and electrical work this is not optional.
  5. Past work: photos of finished jobs, and ideally a past job you can look at in person.
  6. References: two or three past customers you can contact, for jobs like yours.
  7. Written quote: a detailed quote in writing, listing materials, labour and what is not included.
  8. Payment schedule: stages tied to finished work, never full payment upfront.
  9. Guarantee: what is guaranteed, for how long, and in writing.
  10. Contract: a written agreement covering the work, the price, the dates and what happens if something goes wrong.

A good contractor will welcome these checks — they prove the contractor is genuine, and they protect the contractor too. A contractor who resists them, rushes you, or calls them unnecessary is giving you useful information about how the job will go.

The questions that matter

Once a contractor passes the ten checks, the questions decide whether they are right for your job. Ask who will actually do the work: the person quoting may not be the person on site, and you want to know who is coming through your door and how experienced they are.

Ask how long the job will take, and what could make it take longer. Weather, material delays and hidden problems all stretch a schedule; a contractor who names these honestly is more trustworthy than one who promises a fixed date with no caveats.

Ask what is included in the price, in detail. Does it cover materials, waste removal, scaffolding, making good afterwards? The cheapest quote is often the one that left the most out, and the extras arrive later as surprises.

Ask how changes are handled. Almost every job changes somewhere along the way — a wall opens to reveal a surprise, or you change your mind about a finish. Agree in advance that changes are priced in writing before the work is done, not argued about afterwards.

Finally, ask what happens if something goes wrong. Who fixes it, how fast, and at whose cost? The answer tells you how the contractor thinks about their own work, and it belongs in the written agreement.

Red flags

Some signs should stop you in your tracks. No written quote is the biggest: a contractor who will only talk prices is a contractor who can change them. No insurance is another — if someone is hurt or something is damaged on your property, the bill can land on you.

Pressure to decide today is a sales tactic, not a scheduling need. Genuine contractors are busy and will hold a quote for a reasonable time; a contractor who says the price only stands until tonight is trying to stop you checking them.

A demand for full payment upfront is a serious warning. Standard practice is a deposit followed by stages tied to finished work. Anyone who wants everything before starting has no reason to finish well — or to finish at all.

A price far below everyone else's usually means something is missing: cheaper materials, no insurance, no guarantee, or work that will need doing again. Get at least three quotes, and be suspicious of the one that stands apart — in either direction.

Other red flags: no fixed address, no references, a different business name on the quote than on the van, and a refusal to put anything in writing. Any one of these is a reason to pause. Two or more is a reason to walk away and keep looking.

Common mistakes

The most expensive mistake is hiring the cheapest quote without checks. A low price feels like a saving until the work fails, the contractor vanishes, and you pay a second contractor to undo and redo it. The ten checks exist precisely because the cheapest quote is the one that most needs them.

The second is no written contract. A handshake feels friendly and trusting, but it leaves no record of what was agreed — the price, the materials, the dates, the guarantee. When memories differ, and they always do, the person with nothing in writing loses.

The third is paying too much too early. Once a contractor holds most of the money, your leverage is gone. Tie every payment to a visible stage of finished work, keep the final payment until the job is complete and you have walked through it, and never pay the last instalment before the snag list is done.

The fourth is skipping the walk-through. When the work is finished, go through it room by room, in daylight, with the contractor, and write down every defect — the snag list. Small defects found at handover get fixed; small defects found a month later get argued about.

Questions people ask

How do I find a good contractor?

Start with official registers and trade bodies, then ask people you trust who had similar work done, and compare at least three written quotes. A good contractor is easy to check: a real business address, proof of insurance, and past customers willing to talk.

Where should I look?

Look at official trade registers, trade body member lists, and review sites where reviewers are verified — and search for the trade body for your country or region. Local community groups can help too, but treat every recommendation as a lead to check, not a guarantee.

What should I check before hiring?

Check identity, insurance, licences or registration, past work, references, a written quote, the payment schedule, and the guarantee. The ten checks in this guide cover each one — run all ten before you agree to anything.

What questions should I ask?

Ask who will actually do the work, how long it will take, what is included in the price, how changes are handled, and what happens if something goes wrong. A good contractor answers plainly; a contractor who dodges the questions is telling you something.

What are the red flags?

No written quote, no insurance, pressure to decide today, a demand for full payment upfront, no fixed address, and a price far below everyone else's are all red flags. Any one of them is a reason to pause; two or more is a reason to walk away.

Should I get it in writing?

Yes, always. A written agreement should state the work, the price, the materials, the start and finish dates, the payment stages and the guarantee. Without it, you have no record of what was agreed if the work goes wrong.
