Seed Variety Comparison Guide

A seed variety is a type of crop bred for particular traits, such as high yield, disease resistance or a short growing season, and choosing between varieties is one of the most important decisions a grower makes each year. This guide explains the traits that matter, how to read a seed packet, and how to compare varieties side by side with a simple table. It names no variety as the best, because the right variety depends on your soil, your season and your market.

What this is and who needs it

Every crop comes in many varieties, and the differences between them are large. Two varieties of the same crop can ripen weeks apart, resist different diseases, need different amounts of water, and produce very different yields on the same field. Choosing a variety is therefore not a small detail: it shapes the whole season, from the day of sowing to the day of selling. A good choice makes the season easier, and a poor choice makes it harder in ways that no amount of later work can fully fix.

This guide is for growers who choose their own seed: small farmers, home gardeners, and anyone who wants to understand what the words on a seed packet really mean. It explains the traits that matter when comparing varieties, how to weigh one trait against another, and how to test a new variety without risking the whole crop. It does not recommend specific varieties, because the right variety in one place can be the wrong variety in another.

The guide is built around a simple idea: compare varieties on paper before you compare them in the field. The comparison table below lets you put three varieties side by side and judge them on the same traits, so the decision is made with a clear head rather than in the rush of the sowing season. Used with advice from local growers and local trial results, it is a reliable way to choose.

How it works, step by step

Start by listing what matters most on your farm. For most growers, the list includes yield, disease resistance, the length of the growing season, tolerance of drought or wet soil, and how well the crop sells at the local market. Write your list before you look at any seed, because it is easy to be impressed by a single trait, such as a very high yield, and to forget the traits that matter more to you, such as a variety that ripens before the dry season.

Next, gather the information for the varieties you are considering. The seed packet is the first source: it usually names the variety, the days to harvest, the expected plant height, and the diseases the variety resists. The seller can add more, and local growers can tell you how a variety actually performed in your area last season. Local trial results, where they exist, are the most valuable source of all, because they describe real plants in real soil near you, not the results from a faraway test field.

Then fill in the comparison table with what you have learned, and judge each variety against your list. No variety wins on every trait, so the decision is always a trade-off: a variety with the highest yield may need more water, and a variety that resists disease may ripen later than you would like. The table makes these trade-offs visible, so you can choose the variety that fits your farm rather than the one with the most impressive single number.

Costs, time and what you need before you start

Seed costs vary widely by crop, variety and place, so this guide quotes no prices as fact. What matters is how to think about the cost. Seed is a small part of the total cost of a season, but it decides the size of the harvest, so the cheapest seed is not always the best value. A variety that costs a little more but resists the disease that ruined last year's crop can be the cheapest choice in the end, because it saves the cost of a failed season.

Allow time for the decision. The best time to compare varieties is well before the sowing season, when sellers still have stock and you can talk to other growers without rushing. If you wait until the week of sowing, you will take whatever is left on the shelf, and the choice will be made for you. Start the comparison a month or more before you need the seed, and order early for varieties that sell out quickly.

Before you start, gather what you need. You need to know your soil type, even roughly: sandy, clay, or in between. You need to know the length of your growing season, from the usual sowing date to the usual harvest date. You need to know the diseases and pests that affected your crop last season. And you need to know what your buyers want: a variety that grows well but that nobody will buy is a poor choice, however high its yield.

Common mistakes and how to avoid them

The first mistake is choosing on yield alone. Yield figures on a seed packet describe what the variety achieved under good conditions, often in a trial field with plenty of water and careful management. On a real farm, with real weather and real soil, the highest-yielding variety may not be the highest-yielding at all, and a variety with a lower headline yield but stronger disease resistance may produce more food in the end. Always read the yield figure together with the resistance, the season length and the water needs.

The second mistake is planting a new variety on the whole field at once. However good a variety sounds, it has not yet been tested on your soil, in your weather, with your way of farming. The safe way is to plant the new variety on a small part of the field and the familiar variety on the rest, then compare them through the season. If the new variety does well, you can plant more of it next year. If it does badly, you have lost a small patch, not a whole season.

The third mistake is trusting a seller's word alone. Sellers know their products, but they also want to sell them. Check what a seller tells you against what local growers say and against local trial results, and be careful with any claim that sounds too good to be true. A variety that promises the highest yield, the strongest resistance, the shortest season and the lowest price all at once is promising more than any real variety can deliver.

The fourth mistake is ignoring the market. A variety that grows beautifully but produces a size, colour or taste that local buyers do not want will be hard to sell, and the best yield in the world is worth little if the crop sits unsold. Before you choose, ask your buyers what they want, and check what sold well at the market last season.

A practical checklist you can follow

Use this checklist each time you choose seed, and keep the filled-in comparison table with your farm records so next year's choice starts from what you learned this year.

  • List the traits that matter most on your farm, before you look at any seed.
  • Note your soil type, the length of your growing season, and last season's diseases and pests.
  • Ask your buyers what size, colour and taste they want, and what sold well last season.
  • Collect information from the seed packet, the seller, local growers and local trial results.
  • Fill in the comparison table, and leave any cell blank rather than guessing.
  • Choose the variety that fits your farm best, not the one with the highest yield figure.
  • Plant a new variety on a small part of the field first, and compare it through the season.
  • Keep the table and your notes, so next year's decision starts from real experience.

The checklist is short on purpose. Choosing seed is a decision that rewards a calm hour with a notebook far more than a rushed visit to the shop, and the growers who choose best are usually the ones who started earliest and asked the most questions.

Tips from real practice

Talk to the growers whose fields you admire. Every area has farmers whose crops look healthy year after year, and they are usually glad to say which varieties they plant and why. Their advice is grounded in your soil and your weather, which makes it more useful than any catalogue. Ask them what they tried and dropped, as well as what they kept, because the varieties a good grower stopped planting teach as much as the ones they still plant.

Keep your own records from season to season. Note the variety, the sowing date, the weather, the problems that appeared, and the harvest, in the same notebook as your comparison tables. After three seasons, those notes describe your farm better than any book can, and they make each year's choice easier and more confident. The best variety for your farm is the one that has already proved itself on your farm.

Finally, remember that no variety is good forever. Diseases change, markets change, and a variety that served you well for years can slowly fall behind. Try one new variety on a small patch each season, even when you are happy with what you have. That small experiment keeps you learning, and it means you will have a replacement ready when the day comes that your favourite variety no longer performs as it once did.

Traits in this guide are explained generally, and no variety data is given here, because real performance depends on your soil, your season and your way of farming. Check local trial results and ask local growers and sellers before you choose, and always test a new variety on a small patch first.

Questions people ask

What should I compare when choosing a seed variety?

Compare yield, disease resistance, days to harvest, tolerance of drought or wet soil, the soil type it suits, the seed cost, and how well the crop sells at your local market. No variety wins on every trait, so the decision is a trade-off. The comparison table on this page lets you judge three varieties side by side on the same traits.

Does a high-yield variety always win?

No. Yield figures on a seed packet describe what the variety achieved under good conditions, often in a trial field. On a real farm, a variety with a lower headline yield but stronger disease resistance or a better fit for your season can produce more food in the end. Always read the yield figure together with resistance, season length and water needs.

What is disease resistance in simple words?

Disease resistance means a variety is less likely to catch a particular disease, or suffers less damage when it does. It is not a guarantee: a resistant variety can still get sick in a bad year, but it usually stays healthier than a variety with no resistance. Resistance is specific, so a variety that resists one disease may be fully open to another.

How important is the growing season?

Very important. A variety that needs a long season will not ripen in time if your season is short, and a variety that ripens very early may flower during the hottest or wettest part of the year. Check the days to harvest on the packet against the length of your season, from your usual sowing date to your usual harvest date.

Where can I get local variety advice?

Start with experienced growers in your area, especially those whose crops look healthy year after year. Local agriculture extension services and local trial results are also valuable, because they describe real plants in real soil near you. Sellers can add information, but check what they say against local experience before you decide.

Should I try a new variety on the whole field at once?

No. Plant a new variety on a small part of the field and your familiar variety on the rest, then compare them through the season. If the new variety does well, plant more of it next year. If it does badly, you have lost a small patch, not a whole season. One small experiment each year also keeps you learning.
