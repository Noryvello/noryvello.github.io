How to Jump-Start a Car

Jump-starting a car is straightforward once you know the cable order — and the order matters, because it keeps sparks away from the battery. This guide gives you the numbered steps, a checker to test your order, and the cases where you should not jump-start at all.

Safety first: never jump-start a battery that is cracked, leaking, swollen, or frozen, or one that smells strongly of rotten eggs. Keep your face away from the battery, wear eye protection if you have it, and call for roadside help if anything looks wrong.

Before you start: check it is safe to jump-start

Do not jump-start a car if the battery is cracked, leaking, or swollen, or if it smells strongly of rotten eggs. Do not jump-start a frozen battery, and do not jump-start a car if the battery terminals are badly corroded or the cables are damaged. In any of these cases, call for roadside help instead. Jump-starting a damaged battery can cause it to leak acid or, in rare cases, to burst.

Use proper jump leads with insulated clamps, and use a donor car with a battery of the same voltage — in practice, another ordinary car. Keep both cars switched off while you connect the cables, and keep the clamp ends from touching each other or any metal part of either car. Wear eye protection if you have it, and keep your face away from the battery.

How to jump-start, step by step

  1. Park the donor car close by. Park the working car nose to nose with the flat car, close enough for the leads to reach, and switch both cars off.
  2. Connect the red clamp to the flat battery. Attach one red clamp to the flat battery's positive (+) terminal.
  3. Connect the other red clamp to the good battery. Attach the second red clamp to the good battery's positive (+) terminal.
  4. Connect the black clamp to the good battery. Attach one black clamp to the good battery's negative (-) terminal.
  5. Connect the last black clamp to bare metal. Attach the last black clamp to a piece of unpainted metal on the flat car — a bolt on the engine block, away from the battery. This is the earthing point.
  6. Start the good car. Start the donor car and let it run for a few minutes.
  7. Start the flat car. Try starting the flat car. If it does not start, wait a few more minutes and try again.
  8. Remove the clamps in reverse order. Take off the black clamp from the metal first, then the black clamp from the good battery, then the red clamp from the good battery, then the red clamp from the flat battery.
  9. Drive the car. Drive the jump-started car for a good run so the battery can recharge, and have the battery checked soon.

Why the order matters

The order is not a ritual — it keeps sparks away from the battery. A battery gives off gas while it charges, and a spark near the battery can ignite that gas. That is why the last connection, the one most likely to spark, goes to bare metal away from the battery rather than to the battery itself. It is also why you connect the red clamps first and remove them last: the red side is live, and the black side is the earth.

If you are unsure of the order, use the cable-order checker above before you touch the leads. It is a quick way to be sure, and it is far better to check on a screen than to guess beside a battery.

When not to jump-start

Do not jump-start a battery that is cracked, leaking, swollen, or frozen. Do not jump-start if you can see or smell anything wrong with the battery — a strong rotten-egg smell means gas is escaping. Do not jump-start a car with a damaged cable, a missing battery cap, or terminals covered in thick corrosion. And do not keep trying if the car does not start after a few attempts — the problem may not be the battery, and repeated attempts can damage the starter motor.

If the car starts but dies again quickly, the battery may not be holding a charge, or the charging system may be faulty. Drive to a garage or call for help rather than jump-starting the car again and again. A battery that keeps going flat needs checking, not another jump.

After the jump-start

Once the car is running, drive it for a good run — a short trip to the shops is not enough to recharge a flat battery. Turn off anything you do not need, such as the air conditioning and the heated seats, so the battery charges faster. Have the battery and the charging system checked soon, especially if the car has gone flat more than once. A battery that keeps failing is telling you something, and it is cheaper to listen early than to be stranded later.

Questions people ask

What order do jump leads go on?

Red clamp to the flat battery's positive terminal, red clamp to the good battery's positive terminal, black clamp to the good battery's negative terminal, then black clamp to unpainted metal on the flat car. The cable-order checker above lets you test the order before you start.

Why does the last black clamp go to metal, not the battery?

A battery gives off gas while it charges, and a spark near the battery can ignite that gas. The last connection is the one most likely to spark, so it goes to bare metal away from the battery, where a spark is harmless.

How long should I drive after a jump-start?

Drive for a good run so the battery can recharge — a short trip is not enough. Turn off things you do not need, such as air conditioning, so the battery charges faster, and have the battery checked soon.

Can I jump-start a car in the rain?

You can, but take extra care: keep the battery area as dry as you can, keep the clamp ends from touching each other, and wear eye protection if you have it. If the rain is heavy and you are unsure, call for roadside help instead.

What if the car still will not start?

Do not keep trying — the problem may not be the battery. Wait a few minutes and try once more; if it still will not start, call for help. Repeated attempts can damage the starter motor, and a car that dies again quickly needs a garage, not another jump.
