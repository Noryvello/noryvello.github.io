Solar power — electricity from sunlight

A solar panel is made of cells that turn light into electricity. When sunlight hits a cell, it pushes tiny charged particles into motion, and that motion is an electric current. Panels sit on roofs or in open fields, and they feed either a home, a battery or the grid. A panel works best in strong direct sun, but it still works on cloudy days — it uses daylight, not only sunshine — so solar power is used in grey northern countries as well as sunny ones. The set-up cost is the main barrier: panels, wiring and fitting cost a medium amount up front, and the running cost after that is very low, since sunlight is free and panels have no moving parts. The limits are clear: panels make no power at night, make less in winter, and need roof space or land. A typical home system pays for itself over several years through lower bills, but the exact time depends on the country, the roof and the price of electricity, so check a local source before planning one.

Wind power — electricity from moving air

A wind turbine works like a fan in reverse. Moving air pushes the blades around, the blades turn a shaft, and the shaft drives a generator that makes electricity. Turbines stand on hills, plains and coasts, and many newer ones stand offshore, where the wind is stronger and steadier. A single large turbine can power hundreds of homes when the wind blows well. The set-up cost is medium to high, and the running cost is low. The limits are the weather itself: a turbine makes nothing on a calm day, and it shuts down in very strong winds to protect itself. Turbines also need space and planning permission, and some people dislike how they look or sound. Even so, wind is one of the cheapest ways to make electricity in many windy countries.

Hydropower — electricity from flowing water

Hydropower uses water that is already moving. A dam holds back a river and lets water fall through turbines, or a small plant sits in a flowing stream. The falling water spins the turbine, and the turbine makes electricity. Big dams can make a lot of power and can store energy by holding water back and releasing it when demand is high. The set-up cost of a dam is high, but the running cost is low and a dam can last for decades. The limits are serious, though: a dam floods land, changes how a river flows, and affects fish, farms and homes downstream. Small run-of-river plants avoid most of that damage but make less power. Hydropower suits hilly countries with steady rainfall, and it does not suit flat, dry land.

Geothermal, biomass and tidal power

Geothermal energy is heat from inside the earth. In volcanic regions the ground is hot close to the surface, and that heat can warm homes directly or make steam to drive a turbine. It works day and night in any weather, which makes it steady, but it is only practical in some places. Biomass energy comes from plant and animal matter — wood, crops, farm waste and even rubbish. It can be burned for heat or turned into gas and fuel. It is renewable only if the plants grow back as fast as they are used, and burning still makes smoke, so it needs care. Tidal and wave power use the sea: tides rise and fall on a timetable, which makes them predictable, and waves carry a lot of energy. Both are still expensive to build, and the sea is a harsh place for machines, so they are used in only a few places today.

Comparing the energy types

No single type wins everywhere. Solar is simple and works on any roof, wind is strong where the air moves, hydro is steady where rivers run, and geothermal is steady where the ground is hot. Storing renewable energy

The hardest problem with solar and wind power is not making it — it is keeping it for later. The sun shines in the day and the wind blows when it wants, but people need power in the evening and on calm days. Batteries are the most familiar answer: a home battery stores the afternoon sun for the evening, and large batteries beside wind farms smooth out the ups and downs. Pumped water storage is the oldest large-scale answer — spare electricity pumps water uphill into a reservoir, and the water runs back down through turbines when power is needed. A third answer is the grid itself: linking regions together lets a windy coast supply a calm city, and lets a sunny desert supply a cloudy north. None of these is perfect — batteries are expensive, reservoirs need the right hills, and grids need planning — but storage is the part of renewable energy that is improving fastest, and it is what turns sunshine and wind from a sometimes-power into an always-power.

Renewable energy around the world

Different countries use what their land gives them. China, the United States and India have built some of the world's largest solar farms, using open land and strong sun. Denmark gets a large share of its electricity from wind turbines, many of them standing offshore in the North Sea. Norway makes most of its electricity from hydropower, thanks to steep mountains and heavy rainfall. Iceland heats most of its homes with geothermal energy from volcanic ground, and its hot water comes straight from the earth. Brazil has used sugar-cane ethanol as a vehicle fuel for decades, and Kenya generates a large share of its electricity from geothermal power in the Rift Valley. The lesson is the same everywhere: the best renewable energy is the one that fits the local weather, land and needs.

Three myths, corrected

Myth one: solar panels need desert sun. They work best in strong sun, but they still make useful power under cloud, and Germany — not a sunny country — was one of the first to build solar power at a large scale. Myth two: wind turbines never stop. They do stop: in very strong winds they shut down to protect themselves, and on calm days they make nothing at all. That is why wind power is usually paired with storage or other sources. Myth three: hydropower is always clean. The electricity itself is clean, but building a dam floods land, changes a river and uses large amounts of concrete, and a flooded valley can release greenhouse gases as plants rot underwater. Every energy type has a cost; the honest question is which cost is smallest for the power you need.

Questions people ask

What is renewable energy?

Renewable energy comes from sources that nature replaces: sunlight, wind, flowing water, heat from inside the earth, and plants. Unlike coal, oil and gas, these sources do not run out when we use them, and making power from them usually makes far less pollution.

Does solar work on cloudy days?

Yes, but with less power. Solar panels use daylight, not only direct sunshine, so they keep working under cloud — typically at a lower output than on a clear day. They make no power at night, which is why homes with solar panels usually stay connected to the grid or use a battery.

Is wind power reliable?

Wind power is reliable in the sense that turbines work well when the wind blows, but the wind itself comes and goes. Turbines shut down in very strong winds and make nothing on calm days, so wind power is usually used alongside storage, other energy sources or a grid connection.

What is the cheapest renewable energy?

There is no single cheapest answer — it depends on the place. In sunny countries solar is often cheapest, in windy countries wind is often cheapest, and where rivers run, hydropower can be very cheap to run. Set-up costs, running costs and local prices all change the answer.

Can a home run on renewables alone?

It can, but it takes planning. A home needs enough solar panels or another source, plus a battery or a grid connection for nights and cloudy days, and good insulation so less energy is wasted. Many homes run partly on renewables and take the rest from the grid.

What is the difference between renewable and clean energy?

Renewable means the source is replaced by nature — sunlight, wind, water. Clean means making the power causes little or no pollution. Most renewables are also clean, but not all clean energy is renewable: nuclear power, for example, makes little air pollution but uses fuel that is mined and runs out.
