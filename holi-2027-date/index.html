What Holi is

Holi is the Hindu spring festival of colours. It celebrates the arrival of spring and the victory of good over evil, and it is one of the most joyful occasions in the Hindu calendar. On the day of colours, people throw coloured powder and water at each other, gather with family and friends, and share food and sweets. The festival is as much about the mood as it is about the rituals: strangers become friends, old quarrels are set aside, and whole streets turn bright with colour.

The story behind Holi is told through Prahlad and Holika. Prahlad was a young devotee whose father, the demon king Hiranyakashipu, demanded to be worshipped as a god and punished his son for refusing. Holika, the king's sister, was said to be immune to fire, so she sat in a great fire holding Prahlad in her lap, intending to burn him. Instead, Holika was destroyed by the flames and Prahlad survived unharmed. The bonfire lit on the eve of Holi, Holika Dahan, recalls that moment: evil burned away while devotion was protected. That is why the bonfire comes first and the colours follow the next day — the fire marks the victory, and the colour play celebrates it.

Holi is celebrated across India and Nepal and by Hindu communities around the world. The spirit of the festival travels easily: wherever there are people who love the story and the colours, Holi finds a home. In many places the celebrations are public, with neighbourhood gatherings, music, and streets full of colour. In others they are smaller, centred on family and the local temple. Either way, the two days keep the same shape — a bonfire in the evening, then a day of colour.

The two days of Holi 2027

The first day is Holika Dahan, sometimes called Chhoti Holi, on the evening of Sunday 21 March 2027. A bonfire is lit after sunset, and people gather around it, offering prayers and circling the fire. The fire is the centrepiece of the evening: it is the retelling of the Prahlad and Holika story in flame, and it closes the old year with a symbolic burning of what is harmful. Families often bring offerings to the fire and mark the moment together before the colour play begins the next morning.

The second day is Rangwali Holi, also known as Dhulandi, on Monday 22 March 2027. This is the festival of colours itself. People smear coloured powder on each other's faces, throw colour and water, and celebrate through the morning and into the afternoon. Music, dancing, and shared meals are all part of the day, and the colours are deliberately bright and messy — the point is to let go and enjoy the festival together. By the end of the day, everyone looks the same under the colour, which is part of the festival's charm.

It helps to keep the two days separate in your plans. If you are arranging a gathering, the bonfire belongs to Sunday evening and the colours belong to Monday. Guests who want to take part in both should plan for an evening event on the 21st and a daytime event on the 22nd. If you are travelling for Holi, the same split applies: arrive in time for the bonfire on Sunday evening, and expect the main celebration on Monday.

How the Holi date is set

Holi follows the Hindu lunar calendar. It falls on Phalguna Purnima, the full-moon day of Phalguna, the last month of the lunar year. Because the lunar calendar runs shorter than the solar year, the Gregorian date of Holi moves every year, landing somewhere in February or March. That is why Holi in 2027 falls on 21 and 22 March, while in other years it may fall weeks earlier or later.

The bonfire is lit in the evening, in the Pradosh Kaal after sunset, while the Purnima tithi is running, and avoiding Bhadra, a period considered inauspicious for the lighting of the fire. The colours are then played the next day. This is the basic structure of the festival every year: the full-moon evening for the fire, the following day for the colours. The exact timing of the bonfire depends on the local panchang, and the recommended time for lighting the fire varies from city to city. For that reason this page does not give muhurat clock times — the right time to light the bonfire where you are comes from your local panchang, not from a single clock time that could apply everywhere.

The full moon is what anchors the festival. Phalguna Purnima is the full moon of the last lunar month, and the bright evening moon is part of the occasion: the bonfire burns under a full moon, and the colours begin the next morning. If you want to understand why Holi falls when it does, the answer is the moon. Follow the full moon of Phalguna, and you find Holi.

Regional variation

Holi is a festival with many local traditions, and the date is not identical everywhere. The dates on this page — Holika Dahan on Sunday 21 March and Rangwali Holi on Monday 22 March — are the dates most widely observed in 2027. But some regions, including parts of Punjab and Haryana, play colours on Tuesday 23 March 2027, a day later than the main date. This is because their local almanacs reckon the tithi differently, and their tradition places the colour play on the day after the one observed elsewhere. If you are in Punjab or Haryana, or if your family follows that tradition, check your local panchang rather than assuming the colours fall on the 22nd.

The Braj region, home to Mathura and Vrindavan, is a special case of its own. There, celebrations run for days before the main day, with each place following its own local tradition. The towns of Braj are deeply tied to the stories of Krishna, and Holi there is a long festival rather than a two-day one: different temples and villages hold their own celebrations in the days leading up to the main day, and visitors come from far and wide to take part. If you are planning a trip to Mathura or Vrindavan for Holi, expect a festival that starts well before 21 March and follows the local calendar of events.

Elsewhere, too, local customs shape the festival. Some communities emphasise particular rituals on the bonfire evening; others have their own way of marking the day of colours. The dates given here are the anchor, but the details belong to each place. Before you make plans — especially if you are travelling, booking time off, or organising a public gathering — check a local panchang for the dates and timings in your area. A day's difference is normal for Holi, and it is better to follow the local reckoning than to assume one date fits everywhere.

Planning around Holi 2027

For most people, Holi 2027 is a two-day occasion: Sunday evening for the bonfire, Monday for the colours. If you are hosting, that means two different kinds of gathering — a calm evening around the fire, and a lively, messy morning of colour. If you are a guest, it means keeping both days free. The colour play is an outdoor affair in most places, so it is worth planning for the weather and for clothes you do not mind staining. Water is part of the festival, so anything you carry should be able to get wet.

For families with children, Holi is a festival that children remember. The colours, the crowds, and the shared excitement make it one of the year's highlights. At the same time, the bonfire evening is a moment to tell the story of Prahlad and Holika, so that the colours the next day carry their meaning. Holi works best when the two days are understood together: the fire explains the colours, and the colours celebrate the fire.

Whatever your plans, the dates to hold are Sunday 21 March for Holika Dahan and Monday 22 March for Rangwali Holi — and Tuesday 23 March if your region follows the later date for the colours. Check your local panchang, gather your colours, and enjoy the festival.
