Supply Chain Risk Checklist

A supply chain usually breaks at its weakest link, and the weakest link is rarely the one you are watching. This interactive checklist helps you find the risks across suppliers, transport, demand and compliance, tick them off as you check them, and decide what to fix first.

This checklist helps you find and think about risks — it cannot predict disruptions or guarantee that your supply chain will keep running. Use it alongside your own knowledge of your suppliers, routes and markets, and seek professional advice for legal or financial decisions.

Why a supply chain risk checklist matters

A supply chain is only as strong as its weakest link, and the weak link is rarely the one you are watching. Most disruptions start quietly: a supplier's quality slips, a route gets slower, a certificate quietly expires. By the time the problem is obvious, the options are expensive. A checklist turns vague worry into a list of specific risks you can see, score and work through one at a time.

This page gives you that list. It covers four risk areas — suppliers, transport, demand and compliance — and each item is something you can check with a short investigation, not a guess. Tick the items that apply to your business, then use the scoring section to decide what to fix first. The checklist is saved in your browser, so you can work through it over several sittings and come back to it whenever you review the chain.

Supplier risks

Suppliers are the foundation of the chain, and supplier risk is the risk you can do most about. A single source for a critical part is the classic danger: if that supplier fails, your production stops. But quieter risks matter too — a supplier whose quality is slipping, whose lead times are drifting longer, or whose finances are shaky. Tick every item below that applies to your business.

If you ticked several supplier items, start with the one that would stop production fastest. Finding a second source for a critical part takes time, so begin the search before you need it, not after a failure. Even a supplier you rarely use is worth more as a tested backup than as a name in a drawer.

Transport and logistics risks

Goods in transit are goods at risk. A route that has always worked can close overnight — a port strike, a storm, a border change — and if every shipment uses the same route, every shipment stops together. Transport risk also includes the quieter costs: freight prices rising, insurance that no longer covers the real value, and customs paperwork that keeps causing delays on the same route.

The cheapest fix in transport is usually a second route you have tested once. You do not need to split every shipment; you need to know, before a crisis, that an alternative exists and how long it takes. Check your insurance cover at the same time — many policies quietly fall behind as shipment values grow.

Demand and inventory risks

Demand risk is the risk of being wrong about what customers will buy, in either direction. Too little stock and you miss sales; too much and your money sits on shelves. Both directions get worse when forecasts are guesses, when a few customers dominate your orders, or when stock levels were set years ago and never revisited.

Good stock decisions start with honest numbers: what actually sold, how fast, and to whom. If a few customers account for most orders, that concentration is itself a risk worth naming, because losing one of them changes everything. Review slow-moving stock regularly — it is cash in disguise, and clearing it frees money for stock that actually sells.

Compliance and paperwork risks

Compliance risk is the risk that a rule you did not know about stops your goods. Import and export rules change, licences expire, labelling rules differ by market, and a single missing certificate can hold a shipment at a border. These risks are easy to prevent and expensive to ignore, because a border official will not wait while you apply for a permit.

Give compliance a named owner. When everyone assumes someone else is checking expiry dates and rule changes, nobody is. A simple calendar of renewal dates, reviewed once a month, prevents most compliance surprises.

How to score what you find

A ticked item is a risk you have named, and naming it is the first step — but not every tick matters equally. Score each ticked item on two simple questions: how bad would it be if it happened, and how likely is it to happen soon? The items that score high on both questions are your priorities; fix those first, even if they are not the easiest.

For each priority risk, write one mitigation step with a name and a date. Finding a backup supplier is a mitigation. Testing a second route is a mitigation. Setting a calendar reminder for a licence renewal is a mitigation. A risk with no mitigation step is just a worry — the step is what turns the checklist into protection.

  • High impact and likely soon: fix first, with a named owner and a date
  • High impact but unlikely: prepare a plan, even a simple one
  • Low impact and likely: fix when cheap, or accept it openly
  • Low impact and unlikely: note it and move on

Turning the checklist into a routine

A checklist used once is a snapshot; a checklist reviewed regularly is a habit that keeps the chain healthy. Review the full list once a quarter, and review the supplier section monthly if you depend on a small number of suppliers. After each review, update the ticks and the mitigation steps — a risk that is fixed should be unticked, and a new risk should be added to the list in your notes.

Share the results with the people who can act on them. A warehouse manager who knows which route is fragile will spot trouble sooner than a report ever will. Keep the review short and practical: what changed since last time, what is fixed, and what is the one next step. If the review takes more than an hour, the list has grown too complicated — simplify it back to the risks that would actually stop the business.

What to do when a risk becomes real

When a disruption happens, speed matters more than perfection. First, work out what is actually affected: which orders, which customers, which dates. Then tell the affected customers early and honestly — a customer who hears bad news from you will usually wait, while a customer who discovers it alone will usually leave. Then work the mitigation you prepared, or build one fast if you have none.

After the disruption, hold a short review while memories are fresh. What was the first warning sign, and did anyone see it? Which mitigation helped, and which was missing? Write the answers down and add them to the checklist. Every disruption is a free lesson about where the chain is weak — the businesses that learn fastest are the ones that get disrupted least.

Questions people ask

What are the four main areas of supply chain risk?

Suppliers, transport, demand and compliance. Supplier risk covers who makes your goods; transport risk covers how they move; demand risk covers what customers will buy; compliance risk covers the rules and paperwork that can stop a shipment.

How often should I review my supply chain risks?

Review the full checklist once a quarter, and check the supplier section monthly if you depend on a small number of suppliers. Review again immediately after any disruption, while the lessons are still fresh.

What is the most dangerous supply chain risk for a small business?

Usually it is depending on one supplier for a critical part, or on one route for all shipments. Either one can stop the business completely, so a tested backup — even one you rarely use — is worth more than it costs.

How do I decide which risk to fix first?

Score each risk on impact and likelihood. Fix the risks that are both high impact and likely to happen soon, with a named owner and a date. Prepare simple plans for high-impact risks that are unlikely, and accept or note the low-impact ones.

Do my ticks get saved anywhere?

Your ticks are saved only in your own browser on your own device, so you can come back to the checklist later. Nothing is sent to any server, and clearing your browser data will clear the ticks too.

What should I do when a disruption actually happens?

Work out what is affected, tell the affected customers early and honestly, then work the mitigation you prepared. Afterwards, hold a short review, write down what the first warning sign was, and add the lesson to your checklist.
