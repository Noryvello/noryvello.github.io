What a personal statement is for

A personal statement answers two questions: why do you want this course, and why should the course want you. That is all it has to do, but it has to do it in your own words, with real examples, in a short space. Admissions readers see hundreds of statements, and the ones they remember are specific, honest and clearly written.

The statement is not a life story and not a list of grades. Grades are already on your application; the statement is where you show what lies behind them. The best statements sound like a real person talking about something they care about, not like a list of qualities copied from a guide.

The structure that works

Most strong statements follow the same shape. An opening of one or two sentences says what draws you to the subject, starting with the most specific thing you can say. A paragraph on motivation develops that opening and shows how your interest grew. One or two paragraphs on experience give the evidence: what you have read, done or made, and what you learned from it. A paragraph on goals says what you hope to get from the course and what you hope to do afterwards. A closing of one or two sentences returns to the opening idea and finishes with why you are a good fit.

Writing about motivation

Motivation is the hardest part to write well, because it is easy to fall back on empty phrases. Saying you are passionate about a subject tells the reader nothing, because everyone says it. What tells the reader something is the detail: the book that changed how you thought, the project that kept you up late, the question you could not leave alone. One specific detail beats a paragraph of general enthusiasm.

Write about how your interest grew rather than claiming it was always there. Readers trust a story of growing interest far more than a claim of lifelong passion, and it gives you something real to write about instead of a slogan.

Writing about experience

Experience does not have to mean a formal placement or a famous name. Reading, a school project, volunteering, a part-time job, making something, or helping someone can all count, as long as you can say what you did and what you learned. The test for any example is simple: could you talk about it for two minutes if someone asked? If not, it is not doing any work in your statement.

For each example, give three things: what you did, what happened, and what you took from it. The third part is the one people forget, and it is the part that matters most, because it is where you show thinking rather than just activity.

Writing about goals

Goals do not need to be grand. Saying what you hope to study in more depth, what skills you want to build, and what you might do afterwards is enough. Readers do not expect you to have your whole life planned; they want to see that you have thought about what the course is for and what you will do with it.

Be honest about uncertainty if you feel it. Saying you are drawn to two areas of the subject and want to explore both is a better answer than a made-up career plan, and it shows the kind of thinking the course itself will ask of you.

Red flags to check before you send

Run the checklist at the top of the page over your finished draft. The most common problems are easy to fix once you see them: claims without examples, phrases that sound impressive but say nothing, and a statement that is really about your life story rather than the course. Cut anything you cannot back up with a real example, and cut any sentence that would fit just as well in someone else's statement.

Read the whole thing out loud. Anything that sounds awkward when spoken will read awkwardly too, and reading aloud is the fastest way to find sentences that are too long or too tangled. Then ask someone else to read it and tell you the main message in one sentence. If they cannot, the structure needs work, not the wording.

Questions people ask

How long should a personal statement be?

Check the length limit for your own application, because limits vary and they are enforced. Whatever the limit, aim to use most of it: a statement that is much shorter than the limit looks unfinished, and one that runs over it will be cut.

Should I mention my grades?

Only if a grade tells a story, such as a subject you improved in or a result that opened a door. Your grades are already on your application, so the statement should spend its space on things the grades cannot show.

Can I use humour?

A light touch is fine, but humour is risky in writing: what sounds funny to you can read as flippant to a stranger. If you use it, keep it gentle and brief, and check with a reader you trust that it lands the way you meant.

What if I have no relevant experience?

Then write about what you do have: reading, a school project, something you made, or a question you have thought hard about. Relevant experience is wider than most people think, and honest reflection on a small experience beats a padded list.

Should someone else read my statement?

Yes. A teacher, tutor or friend who knows you well can spot claims that need examples and sentences that do not say what you meant. Ask them to tell you the main message in one sentence, and listen to what they say.
