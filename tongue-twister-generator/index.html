How to say a tongue twister

Read the twister once in your head, then say it out loud slowly, shaping every word clearly. Say it a second time a little faster, and a third time as fast as you can without tripping. If you stumble, slow down again — speed only counts when the words come out right.

Why tongue twisters are tricky

Tongue twisters repeat sounds that are close but not the same, such as "s" and "sh" or "p" and "b". The mouth has to switch between them quickly, and the brain plans the next sound while the mouth is still finishing the last one. That is why even a short line can tie your tongue in knots.

Questions people ask

What is a tongue twister?

A tongue twister is a phrase that is hard to say quickly because it repeats similar sounds, such as 'She sells seashells by the seashore'. The repeated sounds trip up the mouth, which is what makes them fun to try.

Why are tongue twisters hard to say?

They pack similar sounds close together, so the mouth has to switch between them very fast. The brain plans the next sound while the mouth is still saying the last one, and the two get mixed up.

Can tongue twisters help with speaking?

They can be a fun way to practise clear speech, because they make you slow down and shape each sound carefully. Start slowly, say each word clearly, and only speed up when the words come out right.

What is the hardest tongue twister?

Many people say 'The sixth sick sheikh's sixth sheep's sick' is the hardest, because it repeats the 's' and 'sh' sounds so closely. Other favourites are 'Pad kid poured curd pulled cod' and 'How much wood would a woodchuck chuck' — try them slowly first.
