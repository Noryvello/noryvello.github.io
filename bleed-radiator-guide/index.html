How to Bleed a Radiator

Bleeding a radiator means letting out the air trapped at the top of it, so hot water can fill the whole radiator again. This guide shows you how to tell whether air is the problem, what you need, and the steps to bleed a radiator safely.

A radiator only warms a room well when hot water can flow through all of it. When air gets trapped inside, it gathers at the top and pushes the water down, so the top of the radiator stays cold while the bottom warms up. The room then takes longer to heat, and the heating system works harder than it should. Bleeding the radiator opens a small valve at the top corner and lets that air hiss out until water reaches the valve again.

Air finds its way into a heating system in ordinary ways. It can enter when the system is filled or topped up, when radiators are removed and refitted, or slowly over time through tiny gaps. A little air now and then is normal, and bleeding is a routine household job rather than a repair. It takes only a few minutes per radiator once you know the steps, and you need no more than a radiator key and a cloth.

Let the heating cool before you start. The water inside a radiator can be hot enough to scald, so never bleed a radiator while the heating is on or while the radiator is still hot. Keep a cloth and a bowl under the valve, protect the floor, and if you are unsure about any step, stop and call a heating engineer.

Signs your radiator needs bleeding

The clearest sign is a radiator that is warm at the bottom and cold at the top. Run your hand slowly from the bottom of the radiator to the top after the heating has been on for a while. A smooth, even warmth from bottom to top means the radiator is fine. A top that stays noticeably cooler than the bottom points to trapped air sitting where hot water should be.

Noise is another clue. Gurgling, bubbling or trickling sounds inside a radiator usually mean air is moving through the water. You may hear it when the heating first comes on, or as a faint background noise while the system runs. A radiator that takes much longer than the others in the house to warm up is also worth checking, especially if the delay is new and the other radiators behave normally.

Not every cold radiator needs bleeding, though. A radiator that is cold all over may have its valve turned off, and a radiator that is cold at the bottom while the top is warm is usually not an air problem at all. If you do not have a bleed key, do not force the valve with pliers or a screwdriver. The valve is small and soft, and forcing it can round off the square head or crack the valve, which turns a simple job into a leak. A bleed key costs little and fits every standard radiator valve.

How to bleed a radiator, step by step

  1. Turn the heating off and let the radiators cool fully. Bleeding a hot system risks a scald and makes the job messier.
  2. Lay a towel on the floor under the radiator and put a bowl under the bleed valve, which sits at one top corner.
  3. Find the bleed valve. It is a small valve with a square head, usually on the side of the radiator away from the pipework.
  4. Push the bleed key onto the square head so it sits firmly.
  5. Turn the key slowly, about a quarter turn, until you hear air hissing out. Hold the cloth just under the valve.
  6. Wait while the air escapes. The hiss may last a few seconds or a little longer if a lot of air has built up.
  7. As soon as a steady stream of water appears, turn the key back to close the valve. Close it firmly but do not over-tighten it.
  8. Wipe up any water, then check the valve for drips over the next few minutes.
  9. Check the boiler pressure gauge. Bleeding lets water out of the system, so the pressure may have dropped. Top the system up only in the way your boiler manual describes.
  10. Turn the heating back on and check the radiator again. It should now warm evenly from bottom to top.

After bleeding

Once the heating is back on, give the radiator time to warm through, then feel it again from bottom to top. If the top is still cold, let the system cool and repeat the bleed once more. A second bleed sometimes clears air that was trapped in a pocket the first time round.

It is worth checking the other radiators in the house while you are at it. Air often affects more than one radiator, and the one furthest from the boiler is usually the first to show it. Work through them one at a time, starting with the ground floor and moving up, and check the boiler pressure again when you have finished.

Keep an eye on the bleed valve for the rest of the day. A slow drip usually means the valve was not closed firmly enough, and a gentle extra turn will stop it. If the valve keeps dripping after that, or the square head feels damaged, leave it alone and call a heating engineer rather than forcing it tighter.

When bleeding does not fix it

If the radiator is cold at the bottom after bleeding, the cause is usually sludge, a build-up of rust and dirt that settles at the bottom of the radiator and blocks the water. Bleeding cannot move sludge, and the fix is a system flush, which is a job for a heating engineer.

If one radiator stays cold all over while the rest of the house heats normally, check that its valve is open. Some radiators have a thermostatic valve that may be set low or stuck, and some have a lockshield valve at the other end that may have been closed during earlier work. If the valves are open and the radiator is still cold, the pipe or valve may be blocked, and again a professional is the right call.

If the boiler pressure keeps dropping after you top it up, or you find damp patches near pipework, do not keep topping the system up. Falling pressure can point to a leak somewhere in the system, and that needs a heating engineer to find and fix. Bleeding is a simple maintenance job, and anything beyond it belongs to someone trained for it.

Questions people ask

How do I know if my radiator needs bleeding?

Feel the radiator when the heating has been on for a while. If the top is cold while the bottom is warm, or you hear gurgling, trapped air is the likely cause and bleeding usually helps.

Should the heating be on or off when I bleed a radiator?

Off, and the radiator should be cool. Bleeding a hot radiator risks a scald, and air moves out more cleanly when the water is still.

How much water should come out?

Only a small amount. Close the valve as soon as a steady stream of water appears. If you open the valve too far, more water escapes than you need.

Why is my radiator still cold after bleeding?

Air may not have been the cause. A radiator that is cold at the bottom often has sludge inside, and a radiator that is cold all over may have a closed valve or a wider system problem. Both are jobs for a heating engineer.

How often should I bleed my radiators?

Only when they show signs of trapped air, such as cold spots at the top or gurgling. There is no fixed schedule, and bleeding radiators that heat evenly is not needed.
