Few-Shot Prompting Explained with Examples

By the Noryvello team · Published 18 November 2026 · Updated 18 November 2026

Few-shot prompting means giving a chatbot a few examples of what you want before you ask it to do the task. The examples teach it the format, the style, and the pattern in a way that instructions alone often cannot.

Imagine asking someone to sort your post into piles without telling them the categories — then showing them three sorted letters and saying "like this." The second way works far better, and it works for chatbots too. That is few-shot prompting.

The name comes from machine learning: "shot" means an example, and "few" means you only need a handful. Two or three good examples usually do the job. This guide explains why it works, shows a full worked example, and tells you when it helps and when it does not.

Examples guide a chatbot, but they do not guarantee correct answers. A chatbot can still misread the pattern or invent details, so check the output — especially for classification tasks where a wrong label has real consequences.

What Few-Shot Prompting Means

In few-shot prompting, you show the chatbot what you want before asking for it. Instead of describing the format in words — "write a short, warm reply in two sentences" — you give two or three finished examples and then present the new task. The chatbot studies the pattern across your examples: the length, the tone, the structure, the way each one starts and ends. Then it follows the same pattern for the new item. It is the difference between describing a dance and demonstrating it. The technique is simple, but it is one of the most reliable ways to get consistent output from a chatbot.

Zero-Shot, One-Shot, Few-Shot: The Difference

With no examples — zero-shot — you describe the task in words and hope the chatbot's idea of the format matches yours. Often it does, for simple tasks. With one example — one-shot — the chatbot has a model to copy, which fixes most format problems. With a few examples — few-shot, usually two to five — the chatbot can see what stays the same across examples and what changes, which is what makes the pattern clear. More examples are not always better: beyond about five, the returns shrink and the prompt gets long. Start with three. If the output is inconsistent, add one or two more; if it is consistent, three was enough.

A Worked Example: Three Examples Before the Task

Say you want short, friendly replies to customer messages for a small bakery. Without examples, the chatbot might write long, formal replies. With three examples, it learns the pattern. Your prompt could look like this. Example 1 — Message: "Do you have gluten-free bread?" Reply: "Yes, we bake a fresh gluten-free loaf every morning — it usually sells out by noon, so do pop in early." Example 2 — Message: "Can I order a birthday cake for Saturday?" Reply: "Of course — we would love to make it. Tell us the size and any flavours you like, and we will have it ready for Saturday morning." Example 3 — Message: "What time do you close?" Reply: "We close at six, and the ovens come out for the last time at five — you are very welcome until then." Then the task: "Now reply to this message in the same style: 'Do you sell coffee as well?'" The chatbot has learned the pattern — short, warm, specific, with a helpful detail — and the reply follows it naturally.

When Few-Shot Prompting Helps

Few-shot prompting shines in three situations. First, format: when you need output in a particular shape — a certain length, a certain structure, a certain way of starting and ending — examples show the shape better than words. Second, style: when you want a particular tone — warm, plain, playful, formal — examples carry the tone in a way adjectives cannot. Third, classification: when you want items sorted into categories, examples show where the boundaries fall. A chatbot shown three messages labelled "urgent," "routine," and "spam" will sort the next one far more reliably than a chatbot given only the category names.

When It Does Not Help

Few-shot prompting is not always the right tool. For open-ended creative work, examples can narrow the chatbot's thinking: it copies the pattern instead of exploring. For factual questions, examples add nothing — a question needs an answer, not a model. For tasks where the examples are hard to write — a brand-new kind of problem with no obvious template — describing the task well matters more than forcing examples. And if your examples are inconsistent with each other, the chatbot learns a muddled pattern and the output gets worse, not better. When in doubt, try the task without examples first; add them only if the output is inconsistent or off-style.

Tips for Picking Good Examples

Good examples share three qualities. They are consistent: each one follows the same pattern, so the chatbot can see what stays the same. They are varied: they show the pattern working on different inputs, so the chatbot learns the rule rather than memorising one case. And they are the quality you want: the chatbot will match the standard of your examples, so a sloppy example teaches sloppy output. Write your examples as if they were the finished work — because in a sense, they are. If you cannot write a good example yourself, that is useful information: it may mean the task needs a clearer description before examples will help. A useful test is to read your examples as a stranger would: if the pattern is not obvious to you, it will not be obvious to the chatbot either.

Few-Shot Prompting and Longer Conversations

Few-shot prompting works best at the start of a chat, where the examples set the pattern for everything that follows. Once the chatbot has followed the pattern a few times, you can usually drop the examples and just give new tasks — the pattern carries forward in the conversation. If the chatbot drifts, paste the examples again as a reminder. For repeated work — weekly replies, regular reports, a series of similar tasks — save your best few-shot prompt as a template and reuse it. It becomes a small, reliable tool: the same examples, the same quality, every time.

Questions people ask

How many examples should I give?

Three is a good starting point. Two can work for simple formats, and five helps for trickier patterns. Beyond five, the extra examples rarely improve the output and make the prompt long.

What makes a good example?

A good example is consistent with the others, shows the pattern on a different input, and matches the quality you want. Write examples as if they were finished work, because the chatbot will copy their standard.

Does few-shot prompting work on every chatbot?

Yes. Every chatbot learns from examples in the prompt, because the technique relies on pattern-matching, which all chatbots do. The exact wording of the prompt may need small tweaks between chatbots.

Can few-shot prompting make answers worse?

Yes, if the examples are inconsistent or poor quality. The chatbot copies what it sees, so muddled examples teach a muddled pattern. Check your examples carefully before you send them.

Is few-shot prompting the same as fine-tuning?

No. Few-shot prompting shows examples inside a single conversation, and nothing is saved or changed permanently. Fine-tuning retrains a model on many examples, which is a different and much larger process.
