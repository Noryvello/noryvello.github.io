How Solar Panels Work, Explained

A solar panel turns sunlight into electricity using the photovoltaic effect: light knocks tiny charged particles loose inside the panel and their movement becomes an electric current. An inverter then changes that current into the kind of electricity homes use.

A solar panel looks like a flat sheet of dark glass, but inside it is doing something remarkable: turning light into electricity with no moving parts. The process is called the photovoltaic effect, and it has been understood for well over a century, even though affordable panels are much more recent.

This page explains the idea in plain words: how sunlight becomes electricity, what the inverter does, how panels connect to the grid, and what affects how much a panel produces. It is a general explainer, not installation advice.

Sunlight becomes electricity

A solar panel is made of many small cells, each one built from a material that reacts to light. When sunlight hits a cell, the light's energy knocks tiny charged particles loose inside the material. Those particles are electrons, and when they move in one direction they form an electric current.

The cell is designed so the electrons move in a useful direction rather than wandering at random. One side of the cell collects them, the other side lets them flow back, and the loop between the two is the circuit that carries electricity away from the panel.

The current a panel produces directly is direct current, or DC — the same kind of current that comes from a battery. It flows in one direction only. Homes, however, run on alternating current, or AC, which flows back and forth. That is why every solar setup needs an inverter.

From direct current to household current

The inverter is the box that sits between the panels and the home. It takes the direct current from the panels and turns it into alternating current at the right voltage for household use. Without it, the electricity from the panels could not run a fridge, a light or a phone charger.

The inverter also watches over the system. It tracks how much electricity the panels are producing, and it shuts the system down safely if something goes wrong, such as a power cut. In a power cut, most home systems stop feeding electricity into the house, so that workers repairing the grid are not put at risk.

There are different kinds of inverter, and they are arranged in different ways — some systems use one large inverter for all the panels, while others use a small inverter on each panel. The details vary, but the job is the same: turn DC into AC, safely and steadily.

Connected to the grid

Most home solar systems are connected to the electricity grid. During the day, when the panels are producing, the home uses that electricity first. If the panels produce more than the home needs at that moment, the extra flows out to the grid. At night, when the panels produce nothing, the home draws electricity from the grid as usual.

The arrangement between the household and the grid depends on local rules, which vary a lot from place to place. Some places credit households for the electricity they send out; others do not. The general idea is the same everywhere: the grid acts as a shared pool that the home both gives to and takes from.

Some homes also have a battery, which stores extra electricity during the day for use in the evening. A battery is not part of how a panel works, but it changes how the household uses what the panels produce. Whether a battery makes sense depends on the home, the local rules and the cost.

What affects how much a panel produces

Sunlight is the biggest factor. Bright, direct sun gives the most output; shade cuts it sharply, and clouds lower it. Even a small patch of shade over part of a panel can reduce what the whole panel produces, because the shaded cells hold back the rest.

Angle and direction matter too. A panel produces the most when sunlight falls on it as directly as possible, so installers think carefully about which way the panels face and how steeply they tilt. The best position depends on where you live and the time of year.

Season and time of day set the rhythm. Long summer days give more electricity than short winter days, and output is highest around the middle of the day. Temperature plays a part as well: panels work best in cool, bright conditions, and very hot weather can slightly reduce their output.

Questions people ask

Do solar panels work on cloudy days?

Yes, but they produce less. Panels still make some electricity from diffuse daylight on a cloudy day, just not as much as in bright, direct sun.

What does the inverter do?

The inverter turns the direct current from the panels into alternating current, which is the kind of electricity homes use. It also monitors the system and shuts it down safely if something goes wrong.

Do panels work at night?

No. Solar panels need light to produce electricity, so they produce nothing at night. Homes connected to the grid draw electricity from the grid after dark.

Does shade matter for solar panels?

Yes, a lot. Even a small patch of shade over part of a panel can reduce what the whole panel produces, because the shaded cells hold back the rest.
