Fuel Cost Calculator — Trip Fuel & Distance Expense

To find your trip fuel cost, divide the total travel distance by your vehicle's fuel efficiency to get the fuel volume required, then multiply by the fuel price per liter or gallon. Enter your trip figures below for an instant, currency-agnostic breakdown with step-by-step working.

Honest limit: This tool computes steady-rate fuel consumption from the numbers you provide. Real-world fuel usage varies with stop-and-go city traffic, elevation climbs, wind resistance, vehicle load, and driving speed.

How the Fuel Cost Formula Works

Estimating travel fuel cost requires two clean arithmetic steps:

  1. Fuel needed: Trip Distance ÷ Fuel Efficiency
  2. Total fuel cost: Fuel Needed × Fuel Price per Unit

Keep your units consistent: use kilometers with km/L and price per liter, or miles with MPG and price per gallon. The math works in any currency.

3 Worked Examples

  • Example 1 — Highway Commute (Metric): A 240 km trip in a car averaging 15 km/L requires 240 ÷ 15 = 16.00 liters. At 1.50 per liter, total fuel cost is 16.00 × 1.50 = 24.00.
  • Example 2 — Weekend Road Trip (Imperial): A 360 mile round trip at 30 MPG uses 360 ÷ 30 = 12.00 gallons. At 3.40 per gallon, total cost is 12.00 × 3.40 = 40.80.
  • Example 3 — Delivery Van Route: A 180 km route at 9 km/L burns 180 ÷ 9 = 20.00 liters. At 1.25 per liter, fuel expense equals 20.00 × 1.25 = 25.00.

Questions people ask

How do I calculate fuel cost for a road trip?

Divide your total trip distance by your vehicle fuel efficiency to find the total fuel volume needed, then multiply that volume by your local fuel price per unit.

Does this calculator work with both kilometers and miles?

Yes. Enter distance in kilometers with efficiency in km per liter, or enter distance in miles with efficiency in miles per gallon, and match the fuel price to that same volume unit.

How do I calculate a round-trip fuel expense?

Double your one-way distance before entering it, or multiply your calculated one-way fuel cost by 2.

Why is actual fuel consumption sometimes higher than the estimate?

Heavy traffic, steep hills, high highway speeds, air conditioning, cargo weight, and low tire pressure all reduce real-world mileage compared to steady test averages.
