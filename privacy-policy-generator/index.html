What a privacy policy must say


A good privacy policy answers three questions in plain words. First, what do you collect? Second, why do you collect it? Third, who else sees it? If your policy answers those three honestly, it is already doing most of the work. Everything else — how long you keep things, what rights visitors have, how to contact you — supports those three answers.


What you collect depends on your site. A simple blog may collect nothing at all, or only the anonymous counts from a web analytics service: which pages people read, how long they stay, which country they visit from. A site with a contact form collects whatever visitors type into it — usually a name, a contact address, and the words of the message. A shop collects order details: what was bought, where to send it, and how it was paid for. A site that shows adverts may let advertising partners set cookies used for advertising, so the adverts can match the visitor's interests.


Why you collect it should be a plain reason, not a grand phrase. You collect order details to send the order. You collect messages to answer them. You look at analytics counts to learn which pages people find useful. You show adverts to pay for the site. A reason a visitor can understand is a reason they can trust.


Who else sees it is the part people skip most often, and it matters most. If the answer is nobody, say so — that is a strong, honest statement. If an analytics service sees the visit counts, or advertising partners see the cookies used for advertising, say that too, in plain words. Visitors forgive a site that collects a little and says so; they do not forgive a site that collects quietly and says nothing.


The questions explained


The generator asks four questions, and each one shapes the policy it writes. The site name is the name at the top of your policy — the name visitors know your site by. If you leave it blank, the generator writes "Your Site" as a placeholder, and you should replace it with your real name before you publish.


The type of site changes the opening paragraph. A blog publishes articles people read. A shop sells goods and takes orders. A tool site helps visitors do a small task in their browser. Pick the one that fits best; if your site is a mix, pick the type that describes its main job, and edit the finished text by hand afterwards.


The data question is the most important one, because it decides the heart of the policy. "None" means your site truly collects nothing — no analytics, no contact form, no adverts, no accounts. If any of those exist, pick the matching option instead. "Analytics" covers a web analytics service that counts visits. "Contact form" covers messages visitors send you. "Ads" covers advertising partners and the cookies used for advertising. If your site has more than one of these, pick the biggest one and add a sentence about the others by hand after you generate the text.


The contact method is how a visitor reaches you about their data. It can be an email address, or a contact page on your site. If you leave it blank, the generator writes the placeholder "[your email]" — replace it with a real contact method before you publish, because a policy with no way to reach you is a policy that does not do its job.

Common mistakes


The most common mistake is copying a big company's policy and changing the name at the top. A big company's policy describes a big company's site: accounts, payments, apps, offices in many countries, services you have never heard of. Pasted onto a small blog, it promises things you do not do, mentions services you do not use, and confuses every visitor who reads it. A short policy that is true beats a long policy that is borrowed.


The second mistake is saying "we collect nothing" while running an analytics service. Analytics counts are data: they record that someone visited, which page they read, and how long they stayed. A visitor who finds out you run analytics after you said you collect nothing will not trust the rest of the policy. If you run analytics, say so — one honest sentence is all it takes.


The third mistake is forgetting the contact method. A policy with no way to reach you leaves a visitor with a question and nowhere to ask it. Always include a real contact method, and check that it works — an address nobody reads is the same as no address at all.


The fourth mistake is writing the policy once and never looking at it again. Sites change: you add a contact form, you start showing adverts, you open a shop. Each change should bring a fresh look at the policy, because a policy that describes last year's site is not a policy at all.


The fifth mistake is hiding the policy where nobody can find it. A privacy policy belongs in the footer of every page, one tap away, not buried three menus deep. If visitors cannot find it, it cannot do its job.


What it cannot do


This generator writes a starting template in plain words. It is not legal advice, and it cannot take the place of a lawyer. It does not know the rules of your country, and privacy rules differ from place to place — what is enough in one country may not be enough in another. If your site is a real business, have a lawyer check the finished policy before you publish it.


It cannot check whether the policy matches your site. It only knows the four answers you gave it. If your site also runs a newsletter, takes payments, stores accounts, or shares data with a service you did not mention, the generated policy will not cover those things — and it has no way to warn you. You must read the finished text and add what is missing.


It cannot cover special cases. If your site is aimed at children, handles health information, takes payments, or stores detailed records about people, those situations carry rules of their own that a short template cannot cover. Those sites need proper legal advice, not a generated page.


Finally, it cannot keep itself up to date. When your site changes, the policy you generated does not change with it — you must come back, answer the questions again, and publish the new version. Think of this generator as a first draft, written in honest words, that you finish and keep true yourself.



Questions people ask


What is a privacy policy?
A privacy policy is a page that tells visitors what your site collects about them, why it collects it, and who sees it. It is the honest record of how your site treats people's information, written in plain words so anyone can read it.


Does my site need one?
If your site collects anything at all — even simple visitor counts from an analytics service or messages from a contact form — a privacy policy is the honest thing to publish, and in many places the law expects one. Even a site that collects nothing benefits from a short page that says so plainly.


Is this legal advice?
No. This generator gives you a starting template written in plain words, not legal advice. If your site is a real business, or it handles payments or other people's personal details, have a lawyer check the policy before you publish it.


What if I use Google Analytics?
Then choose the analytics option in the generator. The policy will say you use a web analytics service and explain what it measures, such as which pages people visit. Do not say you collect nothing while an analytics service is running on your site.


Can I use this for my shop?
Yes. Pick the shop option for the type of site, and choose honestly what your shop collects. A shop usually collects order details and messages, so the contact form option or a fuller description fits better than the no-data option. Have a lawyer check the finished policy for a real shop.


How often should I update it?
Update it whenever your site changes in a way that affects people's data: when you add analytics, adverts, a contact form, a shop, or a new tool. Even with no changes, read it once a year and check that every sentence is still true.
